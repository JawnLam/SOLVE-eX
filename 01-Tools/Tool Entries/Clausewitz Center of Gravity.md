# Clausewitz Center of Gravity

**One-line summary:** A strategic concept identifying the **source of power, balance, or cohesion** in any system — the center of gravity — and concentrating force on it (or its critical vulnerabilities) to produce decisive effect with limited resources.

**When to reach for it:** Military strategy (foundational); business strategy where focused effort matters more than broad-front engagement; organizational change initiatives; political and persuasion campaigns; competitive analysis where defeating the adversary requires identifying their structural source of strength.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Carl von Clausewitz's *On War* (1832) introduced the **center of gravity (Schwerpunkt)** as the strategic concept of identifying the source of an opponent's power, balance, or cohesion. The argument: in any system, some elements are load-bearing (their loss collapses the system) while others are merely supporting (their loss is absorbed). Strategic effort should concentrate on the load-bearing element — the center of gravity.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **strategy is more about choosing where to apply force than about how much force to apply.** Spreading effort thin across multiple targets achieves little; concentrating effort on the COG can produce decisive effect with limited resources. Most strategic failures are not failures of resource (had enough force) but of focus (applied to the wrong place).

Modern military doctrine (US JP 5-0; Joe Strange's *CG-CC-CR-CV* framework) operationalizes this:

- **CG (Center of Gravity)** — the source of power
- **CC (Critical Capabilities)** — what the COG can do that produces effect
- **CR (Critical Requirements)** — what the COG needs to function
- **CV (Critical Vulnerabilities)** — weak points in the requirements

Strategic effort concentrates on attacking critical vulnerabilities — the weak links that, if broken, deny the COG its critical requirements, which deny it its critical capabilities, which neutralize its center of gravity.

A second insight: **centers of gravity can be physical, moral, or political.** Physical (an army, a fleet, a manufacturing capacity); moral (the will of a population, a leader's resolve); political (the legitimacy of a regime, an alliance's cohesion). Different COG types require different attack strategies. A war's military COG might be the army; the political COG might be the public's support for the war.

A third insight: **the framework transfers extensively beyond military.** In business strategy, the COG of a competitor might be a key product line, a distribution agreement, a brand identity, a CEO. In organizational change, the COG of resistance might be a key influencer, a budget process, a cultural narrative. The structural question — "what's the load-bearing element?" — is domain-general.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The broad-front waste.** Strategies that distribute effort across many targets often achieve no decisive effect on any. COG analysis directs concentrated effort.
2. **The wrong-target failure.** Attacking visible-but-not-load-bearing targets (peripheral capabilities, easy targets) leaves the system intact. COG analysis identifies which target actually matters.
3. **The own-COG-blindness.** Strategists often analyze opponent COGs while ignoring their own. Defending one's own COG is as critical as attacking the opponent's. Recognizing this redirects defensive priorities.

For military strategists, business strategists, change leaders, political campaigners, and any context requiring focused strategic action, COG analysis is foundational.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the system. Whose COG are you analyzing — opponent, own, or both?      |
|    2 | Identify candidate centers of gravity. What's the source of power /             |
|      | cohesion / balance? Could be physical, moral, or political.                     |
|    3 | Stress-test. If this element were neutralized, would the system collapse?      |
|      | If yes, COG candidate confirmed. If no, search elsewhere.                       |
|    4 | Identify Critical Capabilities. What does the COG do that produces effect?     |
|    5 | Identify Critical Requirements. What does the COG need to function?            |
|    6 | Identify Critical Vulnerabilities. Which requirements are weakest?            |
|    7 | Concentrate strategic effort on critical vulnerabilities. Direct attack on COG  |
|      | is often defended; indirect attack via vulnerabilities is often achievable.    |
|    8 | Analyze own COG and protect it. The same logic applies in reverse.             |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE CG-CC-CR-CV STRUCTURE (modern operationalization)

   CENTER OF GRAVITY (CG):
       The source of power, balance, or cohesion.

   CRITICAL CAPABILITIES (CC):
       The functions the CG provides — what it does that
       matters strategically.

   CRITICAL REQUIREMENTS (CR):
       What the CG needs to perform its critical capabilities.

   CRITICAL VULNERABILITIES (CV):
       The weakest of the critical requirements; the points
       where attack will most effectively deny capabilities.

   Strategic logic: attack CVs → deny CRs → CCs degrade → CG
   neutralized.

THE COG-IDENTIFICATION TEST

   For each candidate CG, ask:
       If this element were eliminated, would the system
       collapse / lose strategic coherence?

   If yes → confirmed CG candidate.
   If no → not a CG; look elsewhere.

   Caveat: large systems may have multiple CGs at different
   levels (strategic, operational, tactical). The strategic
   COG is what military doctrine focuses on; operational and
   tactical COGs nest below it.

THE THREE TYPES OF CENTERS OF GRAVITY

   PHYSICAL:
       Army, fleet, factory, key infrastructure.
       Attack: destroy physically.
       Examples: Nazi Germany's industrial capacity (WWII
       strategic bombing); Iraqi Republican Guard (1991).

   MORAL:
       Will of population, leadership resolve, troop morale,
       national identity.
       Attack: undermine through propaganda, demonstration of
       force, atrocity, exhaustion.
       Examples: US public will in Vietnam; Confederate
       resolve in 1865.

   POLITICAL:
       Regime legitimacy, alliance cohesion, leadership
       coalition.
       Attack: disrupt diplomatic relations, expose
       corruption, isolate leadership.
       Examples: Allied unity in WWII (defended); Saddam
       Hussein's coalition support (attacked in 1991).

   Wars often have multiple CG types simultaneously; strategy
   addresses all relevant.

THE BUSINESS-STRATEGY APPLICATIONS

   Competitor analysis:
       What's the competitor's CG?
           - Key product line?
           - Distribution channel?
           - Brand identity?
           - Founder / CEO?
           - Patent portfolio?

       Critical capabilities of that CG?
       Critical requirements?
       Critical vulnerabilities?

       Strategic effort: target vulnerabilities, not directly
       attack the CG (which is usually well-defended).

   M&A integration:
       What's the acquired company's CG that we want to preserve?
       What are the requirements that keep it functioning?
       What in our integration plan threatens those requirements?

   Organizational change:
       What's the CG of the resistance?
           - Key influencer?
           - Budget process?
           - Cultural narrative?
           - Workflow that benefits the resisting group?

       Address vulnerabilities to weaken resistance.

THE OWN-COG ANALYSIS

   Equally important: what is YOUR center of gravity?

   Defending own COG often takes priority over attacking
   opponent's. Loss of own COG = strategic defeat regardless
   of opponent damage.

   Audit:
       What's our source of strategic power?
       What capabilities does it produce?
       What does it require to function?
       What are its vulnerabilities?
       Are those vulnerabilities defended?

   Many organizations spend heavily on offense and neglect
   defense of own COG. Routine audit catches this.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. WRONG COG IDENTIFIED
        Attacking peripheral capabilities while real COG
        operates. Recovery: stress-test (would system collapse
        if neutralized?).

   2. DIRECT-ATTACK-ON-WELL-DEFENDED-COG
        COGs are usually well-defended; direct attack is
        costly. Recovery: attack via critical vulnerabilities,
        not the COG itself.

   3. SINGLE-TYPE FOCUS
        Attacking only physical / moral / political when all
        three matter. Recovery: address relevant CG types
        comprehensively.

   4. OWN-COG NEGLECT
        All offensive attention; own defense thin. Recovery:
        own-COG analysis as priority.

   5. STATIC ANALYSIS
        COG analysis at one moment; doesn't update. Recovery:
        periodic re-evaluation; opponent's CG may shift.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   System / Opponent / Own:  __________________________

   Candidate Center of Gravity (CG):  __________________

   Stress test: if eliminated, system collapses?  Y / N

   Critical Capabilities (what CG does):  ______________

   Critical Requirements (what CG needs):  _____________

   Critical Vulnerabilities (weakest CRs):  ____________

   Strategy: which CV to target, with what means:
       _____________________________________________

   For OWN COG: same analysis; what defenses?
       _____________________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Strategy is concentration, not spread. The COG framework's value is enforcing focused effort on the highest-leverage point. Resist the urge to attack everything; identify the COG and concentrate. (2) Attack vulnerabilities, not the COG directly. The COG is usually well-defended. Critical vulnerabilities are the weak links in what the COG needs. Targeting them is more achievable than direct COG attack. (3) Multiple CG types matter. Physical, moral, and political COGs may all be in play. Single-type focus misses dimensions of the contest. (4) Defend own COG. Equally important as attacking opponent's. Many organizations neglect own-COG defense while pouring resources into offensive plans. Routine own-COG audit catches this.
