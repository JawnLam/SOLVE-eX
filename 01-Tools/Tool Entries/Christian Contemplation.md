# Christian Contemplation

**One-line summary:** A family of non-discursive Christian prayer practices — Centering Prayer, lectio divina, the Jesus Prayer, Ignatian imaginative prayer — distinct from petition or discursive meditation, in which the practitioner rests in presence with God beneath words and images.

**When to reach for it:** Sustained spiritual practice within a Christian frame; recovery from over-reliance on discursive religious thinking; or as a contemplative practice for those whose tradition is Christian and for whom Buddhist forms feel non-native.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Christian contemplative practice has continuous lineage from the Desert Fathers (3rd–5th century) through medieval monasticism (Benedictine *lectio divina*) to the Spanish mystics (John of the Cross, Teresa of Avila) and contemporary recovery (Thomas Merton, Thomas Keating's Centering Prayer). It is *prayer* — not generic meditation — but its non-discursive form distinguishes it from petitionary prayer and from doctrinal study.

Two operative streams are most accessible to contemporary practitioners:

- **Apophatic / via negativa** — strip away images, concepts, even feelings; rest in unknowing; *Cloud of Unknowing* tradition. Centering Prayer is the modern version: choose a sacred word as anchor, return when thoughts come, sit 20 minutes twice daily.
- **Cataphatic / via positiva** — engage image and narrative; *lectio divina* (slow reading of scripture) and Ignatian imaginative prayer (placing oneself in a gospel scene) are the canonical forms.

The non-obvious operational insight: contemplation is *receptive*, not active. Centering Prayer's instruction is "When you notice a thought, return to the sacred word" — without judgment, without progress-evaluation. The practice trains a posture of consent rather than of striving. This distinguishes it sharply from concentration-based forms; the goal isn't sustained focus but ongoing return.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the structured practice prevents:

1. **Religious thinking as substitute for religious experience.** Discursive engagement with theology is valuable but operates at a different layer than contemplative experience. Sustained practice opens that other layer.
2. **Spiritual restlessness.** Frequent switching between prayer styles, books, traditions prevents depth. A defined daily practice produces what variety cannot.
3. **Tradition-incongruence.** Many Western practitioners try Buddhist forms first; the cultural and theological mismatch is real. Christian contemplation offers equally rigorous practice within native frame.

For consulting and executive work specifically: contemplative practice (in any tradition) is an underappreciated source of equanimity, perception, and value-alignment under pressure. The Christian forms add the dimension of *receptivity* — being acted upon by something larger than the self — which has both therapeutic and ethical implications.

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
CENTERING PRAYER (apophatic — Keating's instructions)

    1. Choose a sacred word as the symbol of your intention to consent to God's
       presence and action within. (E.g., "love", "peace", "abba".)

    2. Sit comfortably, eyes closed, settle briefly. Silently introduce the
       sacred word.

    3. When you become aware of thoughts (memories, plans, feelings, images),
       return ever so gently to the sacred word.

    4. At the end of the prayer period, remain in silence with eyes closed for
       a couple of minutes.

    DURATION:    20 minutes, twice daily (morning and afternoon)
    POSTURE:     seated, back supported, feet on floor, hands resting
    NOT REQUIRED: emptiness of mind, "good" sessions, mystical experiences

    The four "R's" of Keating: Resist no thought. Retain no thought. React to
    no thought. Return to the sacred word.

LECTIO DIVINA (cataphatic — Benedictine four movements)

    1. LECTIO (reading)         — read a short scripture passage slowly,
                                    several times. Listen for what stands out.

    2. MEDITATIO (reflection)   — what word, phrase, or image stays with you?
                                    Sit with it; let it speak.

    3. ORATIO (response)        — respond from the heart. A prayer, an
                                    objection, a question, a thanks.

    4. CONTEMPLATIO (rest)      — rest in the presence beneath the words.
                                    Wordless. Receptive.

    Optional 5th: ACTIO (action) — what does this prayer move me toward today?

    DURATION:    20–30 minutes
    SOURCES:     Psalms, Gospels (especially short pericopes), or chosen text
                  (a paragraph from Augustine, Merton, etc.)

THE JESUS PRAYER (Hesychast tradition)

    A continuous repetition of: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me."
    Coordinated with breath if helpful (in: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God"; out:
    "have mercy on me"). Practiced for fixed duration or as continuous practice
    throughout the day. Develops what The Way of a Pilgrim calls "prayer of the heart."

WEEKLY / MONTHLY PRACTICE LOG (light tracking)

    Date  | Practice form     | Duration | Showed up?  | Notes (one phrase)
    ------|-------------------|----------|-------------|----------------------
          |                   |          |             |
          |                   |          |             |

    NOTE: do not score quality. The practice is showing up; depth follows.

WHEN TO USE EACH FORM

    Form               | Best when…
    -------------------|---------------------------------------------------
    Centering Prayer   | Mind is busy with content; need silence beneath
    Lectio Divina      | Want narrative engagement; scripture-grounded
    Jesus Prayer       | During activity / movement; continuous practice
    Ignatian imaginative| Therapeutic / formative; engaging emotional layer
```

> **Operational notes:** Three disciplines. (1) Trust receptivity. The Western instinct is to "make it work" — to evaluate sessions, push for breakthroughs, optimize the practice. Contemplative tradition warns against this; the practice is consent, not achievement. The discipline is showing up regularly without grading. (2) Don't switch practices restlessly. Months of one form yields more than weeks of three. The contemporary spiritual marketplace tempts variety; the tradition recommends fidelity. (3) Find a director or retreat regularly. Subtle dynamics arise (consolation, desolation in Ignatian terms; aridity in Carmelite; emotional surfacing in any). A guide normalizes them and prevents misinterpretation. Fourth: contemplative practice doesn't compete with discursive theology, ethics, or community — it complements them. The fully-formed Christian life integrates all four (prayer, study, ethics, community); collapsing into one impoverishes the others.
