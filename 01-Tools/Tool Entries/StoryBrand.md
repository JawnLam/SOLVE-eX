# StoryBrand

**One-line summary:** A seven-element brand-narrative framework that casts the customer as the hero and the brand as the guide — character with a problem, met by a guide with a plan, called to action, with stakes of failure and success — used to clarify positioning, websites, and marketing.

**When to reach for it:** Brand positioning workshops, website rewrites where the page is "about us" instead of "about you," sales copy that lists features instead of speaking to customer pain, and any communication where the brand has accidentally cast itself as the protagonist.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Donald Miller's *Building a StoryBrand* (2017) compresses storytelling for marketing into a seven-element framework, **SB7**:

1. **A Character** — the customer, with a clear desire / want
2. **Has a Problem** — the obstacle to that desire (external + internal + philosophical layers)
3. **And Meets a Guide** — a credible figure with empathy and authority (the brand)
4. **Who Gives Them a Plan** — a clear, repeatable, low-friction next path
5. **And Calls Them to Action** — direct (buy now, sign up) and transitional (download, learn more)
6. **That Helps Them Avoid Failure** — the negative stakes if they don't act
7. **And Ends in Success** — the positive future state if they do

The non-obvious operational insight is that **almost all brand and marketing communication makes the brand the hero**, and this is the dominant cause of marketing under-performance. "We are innovative." "We have 20 years of experience." "We pioneered…" — these are hero-of-the-story moves. The audience does not enter such stories because they are not in them. The StoryBrand inversion is structural: cast the customer as the hero, and the brand as the *guide* (the mentor figure in Hero's Journey terms — the one with the wisdom and the tool, not the one taking the journey).

This is not just a marketing trick. It maps to a real cognitive pattern: people pay attention to stories *about themselves*. A communication where the audience is not present as protagonist must work much harder to earn attention. The StoryBrand framework imposes the inversion mechanically, which produces immediate readability gains in most brand copy.

The framework's three layers of problem (external, internal, philosophical) are also operationally useful:

- **External problem** — the surface issue (we need a CRM)
- **Internal problem** — how the external problem makes the customer feel (frustrated, disorganized, behind)
- **Philosophical problem** — why this matters in a larger sense (people deserve tools that respect their time)

Most marketing addresses only the external problem. Adding the internal and philosophical layers is what generates emotional commitment, which is what produces customers rather than browsers.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes StoryBrand prevents:

1. **The "we are the hero" failure.** "About Us" pages, founder narratives, and capabilities slides cast the brand as protagonist. The customer reads themselves out of the story and disengages. The fix: rewrite from the customer's perspective with the brand as guide.
2. **The features-without-stakes failure.** Marketing that lists what the product does without naming what failure looks like leaves the customer no urgency to act. The Failure element of SB7 (what bad thing does this prevent) is the discipline that surfaces stakes.
3. **The plan-less call to action.** "Contact us" or "Get started" without a clear, multi-step plan asks the customer to invent the path. They don't. The Plan element forces the brand to lay out the path explicitly so action feels low-friction.

For consulting and B2B work specifically, StoryBrand is a corrective for a recurring positioning failure: the consultant or firm leads with their methodology, their experience, and their pedigree (we are the hero) when the client wants to see themselves succeed (you are the hero, and we are the guide who gets you there). Inverting the position fixes the messaging.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the customer (the Character) — specifically. What do they want?         |
|      | (One sentence, customer's vocabulary, not yours.)                                |
|    2 | Name the Problem in three layers: external (the surface issue), internal (the    |
|      | feeling it produces), philosophical (why it matters).                            |
|    3 | Establish the brand as Guide — empathy ("I understand your problem") plus       |
|      | authority ("I know how to solve it"). Authority without empathy reads as cold;   |
|      | empathy without authority reads as weak.                                         |
|    4 | Articulate the Plan — three to four numbered steps the customer takes. Make it   |
|      | concrete and low-friction. Skip steps that are not necessary.                    |
|    5 | State the direct Call to Action — what should they do RIGHT NOW. (Buy / sign /  |
|      | book.) Plus a transitional CTA — a low-commitment next step (download / read).  |
|    6 | Name the Failure scenario — what bad thing does the customer avoid by acting.    |
|      | Be specific; vague stakes don't motivate.                                        |
|    7 | Paint the Success scenario — what does life look like after they have what the   |
|      | brand offers? Be concrete.                                                       |
|    8 | Audit: read your draft as the customer. Are YOU the hero, or am I (the brand)   |
|      | the hero? If me, rewrite.                                                        |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE SB7 BRAND SCRIPT

   1. CHARACTER (the customer):
      Specifically who? ____________________________________________
      What do they want (in one sentence, their words)?
       ___________________________________________________________

   2. PROBLEM:
      External problem (surface): _________________________________
      Internal problem (how it makes them feel): __________________
      Philosophical problem (why it matters in a larger sense):
       ___________________________________________________________
      Villain (optional — what is the problem personified as?):
       ___________________________________________________________

   3. GUIDE (the brand):
      Empathy ("we understand…"): _________________________________
      Authority ("we know how because…"): _________________________

   4. PLAN:
      Step 1: ___________________________________________________
      Step 2: ___________________________________________________
      Step 3: ___________________________________________________
      Step 4 (if needed): _______________________________________

   5. CALL TO ACTION:
      Direct (action right now): __________________________________
      Transitional (low-commitment next step): ____________________

   6. FAILURE (what they avoid by acting):
      _____________________________________________________________
      _____________________________________________________________

   7. SUCCESS (what life looks like after):
      _____________________________________________________________
      _____________________________________________________________

THE WEBSITE-WIREFRAME APPLICATION

   Above the fold:
       Header: One sentence stating what you offer + how it improves
                customer's life (success). NOT what your company is.
       Sub-header: One supporting sentence on why / how.
       CTA button: direct + transitional buttons visible.

   Below the fold:
       Section 1: Customer's problem (external / internal / philosophical)
       Section 2: You as guide (empathy + authority signals — case studies,
                                 testimonials, credentials)
       Section 3: The plan (3-4 step path)
       Section 4: Stakes (what failure looks like + what success looks like)
       Section 5: Direct CTA again, prominent.

   Test: visit the page. Within five seconds, can a stranger answer:
       (a) what does this brand offer?
       (b) how does my life get better?
       (c) what do I do next?

   If any answer is unclear, rewrite the failing section.

THE HERO-INVERSION TEST (the master diagnostic)

   Read your draft.

   Count: how many sentences are about the customer vs. about the brand?

   If brand-sentences > customer-sentences, you have inverted the story.
   Rewrite from the customer's perspective.

   Specific signs of inversion:
       - Pronouns dominated by "we / our / us"
       - Opening sentence about company history or capabilities
       - "About Us" page that doesn't mention the customer
       - Language that requires the reader to care about the brand before
         understanding why they should act

THE THREE-LAYER PROBLEM AUDIT

   Most marketing addresses only the external problem ("we need a CRM").

   To generate emotional commitment, add the internal layer:
       "…because our team feels disorganized and we're losing leads we
       can't see in time."

   To generate philosophical commitment, add the third layer:
       "…and a sales team's time is too valuable to spend reconstructing
       what they should already know."

   The philosophical layer is what makes brand stories durable across
   pricing pressure, competitive imitation, and category commoditization.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) The customer-as-hero / brand-as-guide inversion is the framework's load-bearing claim. Most StoryBrand applications fail not because the seven elements are wrong but because the inversion is incomplete — the brand still creeps back into protagonist position. Audit ruthlessly. (2) The three problem layers (external / internal / philosophical) are what separate StoryBrand from feature-list marketing. Skipping the internal and philosophical layers produces flat, swappable copy. (3) Donald Miller's framework popularizes long-standing direct-response and copywriting moves (Eugene Schwartz, John Caples, Gary Halbert) — treat StoryBrand as a deployable adaptation rather than original theory. The deployable form is its real contribution. (4) Pair with persona-shifting before drafting. Generic personas produce generic SB7 fills. The framework is most powerful when each of the seven slots is filled with concrete, specific language that could only come from one named customer's experience.
