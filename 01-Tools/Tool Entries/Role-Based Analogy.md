# Role-Based Analogy

**One-line summary:** A perspective-shift technique that asks "how would [specific person, institution, or industry] solve this?" — borrowing a different mental model wholesale to surface approaches that the native frame can't see.

**When to reach for it:** Strategic decisions where conventional thinking has stalled; design and product work needing fresh perspective; group brainstorming that's converging too quickly; teaching by analogy; coaching situations where reframing helps; and any context where pretending to be someone else with very different priorities and capabilities would generate new approaches.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Role-based analogy** asks "how would [X] solve this?" The structure:

1. **Identify the problem.**
2. **Choose a role** — a person, institution, industry, fictional character, or composite. Roles work best when they have strong, distinct characteristics that contrast with your current frame.
3. **Adopt the role's mental model.** What do they prioritize? How do they think? What capabilities and constraints?
4. **Apply the borrowed model to your problem.** Generate solutions consistent with the role.
5. **Translate back to your context.** Which role-generated ideas adapt to your actual situation?

The non-obvious operational insight is that **mental-model substitution generates options that mental-model adjustment can't.** Asking "how should we approach this differently?" usually produces variants within your current frame. Asking "how would [Steve Jobs / Toyota / NASA / a 5-year-old] approach this?" forces you to inhabit a different frame entirely, generating solutions that frame-internal thinking misses.

Common productive roles:

- **"How would Apple design this?"** — rigorous simplicity, focus, integration
- **"How would Toyota improve this?"** — kaizen, jidoka, just-in-time
- **"How would Disney engineer this experience?"** — guest journey, theming, attention to detail
- **"How would NASA approach this?"** — systems engineering, redundancy, mission-critical reliability
- **"How would a chef plate this?"** — visual hierarchy, balance, sensory layering
- **"How would the military operate this?"** — clear roles, command structure, decisive action
- **"How would a 5-year-old explain this?"** — simplification, visual metaphor, fundamental questions
- **"How would Bezos memo this?"** — narrative, customer obsession, long-term thinking
- **"How would Buffett invest in this?"** — moat, simplicity, long-term value

A second insight: **the role doesn't need to be perfectly accurate.** "Apple" as a role is a stylized composite of design philosophy, not a precise corporate description. The cartoon version of the role is often more generative than a nuanced one — the strong characteristics drive option generation.

A third insight: **multiple roles produce richer output than one.** Run the same problem through 3-5 different roles; the variants often complement, and the contrast between roles' approaches reveals dimensions you hadn't considered.

A fourth insight: **the framework is teachable and viral.** Once a team learns to ask "how would [X] approach this?", the move transfers across problems. It becomes a routine creative-thinking habit rather than a specialized technique.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "stuck in frame" stall.** Native mental models are exhausted; new options need a frame shift.
2. **The convergence trap.** Group brainstorming converges on the average view; role-substitution explicitly diverges.
3. **The "we're not creative" assumption.** Creativity often = perspective shift, not genius. Role-based analogy makes the shift accessible.

For strategists, designers, facilitators, coaches, teachers, and anyone running creative-thinking work, role-based analogy is one of the most accessible and productive techniques.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Articulate the problem clearly.                                                  |
|    2 | Choose 3-5 roles. Pick those with strong, distinct mental models that contrast |
|      | with your current frame.                                                          |
|    3 | For each role, briefly characterize their priorities, methods, and constraints.|
|    4 | Apply each role to the problem. Generate solutions consistent with the role.   |
|    5 | Across roles, notice patterns and contrasts. What dimensions are surfaced?     |
|    6 | Translate role-generated ideas back to your context. Which adapt cleanly?      |
|      | Which require modification?                                                       |
|    7 | Synthesize. The best solutions often combine elements from multiple roles.     |
|    8 | Use the strongest variants to inform your actual approach.                     |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE ROLE-SUBSTITUTION MOVE

   Standard problem-solving:
       Generate options within your usual mental frame.
       Output: incremental variants of native thinking.

   Role-substitution:
       Adopt frame of [Apple / NASA / chef / 5-year-old].
       Output: solutions consistent with that frame —
       often very different from native thinking.

   The frame substitution is the move. The borrowed
   mental model carries different priorities,
   methods, and constraints; applying it to your
   problem generates novel options.

THE ROLE PORTFOLIO

   COMPANIES / BRANDS:
       Apple — simplicity, design integration
       Amazon — customer obsession, long-term
       Toyota — kaizen, lean, jidoka
       Tesla — first-principles, vertical integration
       Disney — guest experience, theming
       Patagonia — values-led, sustainability
       SpaceX — bold engineering, fast iteration
       Costco — value, scale, employee economics

   PEOPLE (specific or composite):
       Steve Jobs — focused product perfectionism
       Warren Buffett — long-term value investing
       Elon Musk — ambitious physics-grounded
       Jeff Bezos — customer obsession, narrative memos
       Marie Kondo — minimalism, intentionality
       Charlie Munger — mental-models latticework

   ROLES / PROFESSIONS:
       Chef — visual hierarchy, sensory layering
       Military commander — clear orders, decisive action
       Surgeon — checklist, precision, training
       Therapist — listening, reflection
       Detective — evidence, deduction
       Stand-up comedian — timing, audience read
       Novelist — character, narrative, voice

   PERSPECTIVES:
       5-year-old — basics, why-questions
       Skeptic — what's the proof?
       Beginner — fundamental questions
       Outsider from another industry — naive eye

   FICTIONAL / HISTORICAL:
       Sherlock Holmes — observation, deduction
       Sun Tzu — strategic indirection
       Stoic philosopher — what's in your control?
       Da Vinci — cross-discipline imagination

   The richer the role's distinctive characteristics,
   the more generative the substitution.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — CUSTOMER-SUPPORT REDESIGN

   Problem: customer-support team is overwhelmed;
   tickets growing faster than headcount.

   ROLE 1: How would Apple design this?
       Self-service first; immaculate UX; elegant
       escalation when needed. Eliminate the ticket
       altogether by making support unnecessary.

   ROLE 2: How would Toyota approach this?
       Stop the line: every escalation triggers root-
       cause investigation. Pull-system: solve issues
       at source rather than scaling reactive support.

   ROLE 3: How would a 5-year-old approach this?
       "Why are people asking this?" Probe basic
       assumptions. Are we even asking the right
       questions about why customers contact us?

   ROLE 4: How would the military operate this?
       Clear command structure; tiered response by
       severity; pre-defined playbooks for common
       cases.

   ROLE 5: How would Disney engineer this?
       Customer-journey-mapped; touchpoint by
       touchpoint; theatrical staging of issue
       resolution. Magic moments where appropriate.

   Cross-role insights:
       Multiple roles converge on "reduce ticket
       volume at source" (Apple, Toyota).
       Roles differ on what to do with remaining
       tickets (Disney's experience-design vs.
       military's procedural efficiency).

   Synthesis: aggressive root-cause work to reduce
   volume (Toyota) + self-service UX (Apple) +
   procedural efficiency for required human contact
   (military) + experience design at moments of
   complaint (Disney).

   Without role-substitution, the team would have
   focused on "hire more support" — within their
   native frame.

THE ROLE-CONSTRUCTION DISCIPLINE

   Build roles strong enough to be generative:

   Articulate priorities (what does this role most care
   about?)
       Apple: design coherence, user experience, premium
       feel
       Toyota: zero defects, kaizen, employee respect
       NASA: mission-critical reliability

   Articulate methods (how do they work?)
       Apple: vertical integration, controlled
       experience
       Toyota: gemba (go and see), 5 whys, andon cord
       NASA: redundancy, simulation, peer review

   Articulate constraints (what won't they do?)
       Apple: won't compromise on integration
       Toyota: won't ship known defects
       NASA: won't fly without verification

   The richer the role characterization, the more
   generative the substitution.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. WEAK ROLES
        Generic / vague characterization; output
        indistinguishable from native frame.
        Recovery: rich, specific role profiles.

   2. ONE ROLE ONLY
        Single substitution; misses contrasts.
        Recovery: 3-5 roles per problem.

   3. ROLE-WORSHIP
        Treating role as authoritative ("Apple would
        do this; therefore we should"). Recovery:
        roles generate options; you decide.

   4. NO TRANSLATION
        Role-generated ideas applied without adapting
        to your context. Recovery: explicit
        translation step.

   5. PERFORMATIVE ROLE-PLAYING
        Going through motions without genuine
        substitution. Recovery: actually inhabit the
        role temporarily.

   6. FAMOUS-ROLE BIAS
        Defaulting to over-used roles (Apple, Disney);
        missing fertile less-used ones. Recovery:
        diverse role portfolio.

   7. NEAR-ROLE BIAS
        Picking roles too similar to your current
        frame; generates marginal variants. Recovery:
        choose contrasting roles.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Problem: ___________________________________________

   Roles selected (3-5, contrasting):
       Role 1: _______________________________________
       Role 2: _______________________________________
       Role 3: _______________________________________
       Role 4: _______________________________________
       Role 5: _______________________________________

   Per role:
       Role X priorities / methods / constraints:
           _________________________________________
       Role X solution to the problem:
           _________________________________________

   Cross-role patterns:
       Convergent insights: __________________________
       Contrasting insights: _________________________

   Translation to your context:
       Adaptable directly: ___________________________
       Requires modification: ________________________
       Not applicable: _______________________________

   Synthesis (best ideas combined):
       _________________________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Choose contrasting roles. The contrast with your native frame is what generates options. Roles too similar to your current thinking produce marginal variants. (2) Build rich role profiles. Generic "Apple would think differently" produces nothing; specific characterization (priorities, methods, constraints) produces options. (3) Use multiple roles. 3-5 roles per problem produces richer output than one; convergent and contrasting patterns are themselves insights. (4) Translate explicitly back to your context. Role-generated ideas often need adaptation; the borrowed frame doesn't transfer perfectly. The translation step is where ideas become usable.
