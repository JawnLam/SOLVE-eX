# BJ Fogg Behavior Model

**One-line summary:** Behavior occurs when motivation, ability, and a prompt converge — Fogg's formula B = MAP — with the practical implication that motivation is unreliable and the high-leverage design moves are making behaviors easier and engineering reliable prompts.

**When to reach for it:** Designing personal habit changes (exercise, sleep, learning), designing behavior-change products (fitness apps, learning platforms), workplace behavior design (compliance, safety, engagement), and any case where "I know I should but I don't" describes the gap.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

B.J. Fogg's behavior model, developed at Stanford and presented in *Tiny Habits* (2020), is a simple equation:

**B = MAP**: Behavior happens when **M**otivation, **A**bility, and a **P**rompt converge above a threshold.

- **Motivation** — how much you want to do the behavior (right now, in this moment)
- **Ability** — how easy the behavior is (right now, given current skill, time, resources)
- **Prompt** — a trigger that says "do it now"

If any of the three is missing or below threshold, the behavior doesn't happen. All three must align.

The non-obvious operational insight: **motivation is the least reliable of the three.** Motivation fluctuates with mood, energy, social context, and time of day; designing behavior change around motivation produces inconsistent results. The two reliable design levers are:

1. **Increase Ability** — make the behavior so easy that even low motivation suffices. Fogg's "tiny habits" approach takes this to its logical conclusion: shrink the behavior until it requires almost no effort. (Two pushups. One sentence. One sip of water.) The smallness is the design feature, not a temporary scaffolding.
2. **Engineer Prompts** — anchor new behaviors to reliable existing routines. Not "I'll meditate when I feel like it" but "After I pour my morning coffee, I'll sit for 60 seconds." Existing routines are reliable prompts; novel ones aren't.

A third Fogg insight is the **celebration move**: feel positive emotion immediately after performing the behavior. Even a small moment of "yes!" or a fist pump locks the behavior in faster than waiting for distant rewards. The brain encodes habits more strongly when reward is immediate — extrinsic rewards (achievements, streaks) often help; manufactured intrinsic positive feeling helps even more.

The framework's practical claim is that behavior change is **mostly an engineering problem, not a willpower problem.** People who fail at habits aren't lacking discipline; they've been designing changes incompatible with how behavior actually works (high ability barrier, weak prompt, motivation-dependent).

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The motivation-dependent design.** "I'll exercise when I feel motivated" predictably fails because motivation is unreliable. Fogg's model identifies the structural failure: motivation alone cannot produce consistent behavior; ability and prompts must do the work.
2. **The "be consistent" exhortation.** Telling people to "just be more consistent" without changing ability or prompts puts the burden on the unreliable component. Fogg's design moves restructure the system so consistency emerges from the design, not from willpower.
3. **The all-or-nothing failure.** Big behavior changes ("exercise 60 minutes daily") have high ability barriers; missing once breaks the streak; restart requires fresh motivation. Tiny habits invert this — so easy they always happen, so the streak is unbroken; ability stays high; consistency compounds.

For consultants, coaches, product designers, and self-changers, the framework is the discipline that converts "I should do X" intentions into "I actually do X" behaviors with high reliability.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the desired behavior. Be specific — not "exercise more" but "do five    |
|      | pushups" or "stretch for 60 seconds."                                            |
|    2 | Make it tiny. The behavior should be so small that motivation barely matters —   |
|      | doable on your worst day, when tired, sick, or busy. If you can't see yourself  |
|      | doing it on a bad day, it's not tiny enough.                                    |
|    3 | Anchor to an existing routine. "After [existing reliable behavior], I will [new  |
|      | tiny behavior]." The existing behavior is the prompt; pick one that happens     |
|      | reliably without your effort.                                                    |
|    4 | Celebrate immediately. Within 3 seconds of performing the behavior, do something|
|      | that signals positive emotion — fist pump, "yes!", smile. The brain encodes    |
|      | habits faster with immediate positive feedback.                                  |
|    5 | Practice the trio (anchor + behavior + celebration) until automatic. Usually    |
|      | 1-3 weeks, depending on how reliable the anchor is.                             |
|    6 | Once automatic, the tiny habit can grow naturally. People who reliably do five  |
|      | pushups often expand on their own — but the growth is voluntary and easy, not   |
|      | imposed.                                                                         |
|    7 | If a habit is failing, diagnose: is the anchor unreliable? Is the behavior too  |
|      | big? Is the celebration missing? Adjust the failing component.                  |
|    8 | Don't add multiple habits at once. One tiny habit at a time. Compounding works  |
|      | when each is locked in before adding more.                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE BEHAVIOR EQUATION

   B = M × A × P
       (Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt)

   For behavior to happen, all three must be present above threshold.
   Multiplicative: any zero kills the behavior.

   Design implications:
       - Don't design around M (unreliable, fluctuating)
       - Maximize A (make it easy)
       - Engineer P (anchor to reliable existing routines)

THE TINY-HABIT RECIPE

   "After [ANCHOR], I will [BEHAVIOR]."
   And then: "I celebrate by [CELEBRATION]."

   Example formulas:
       "After I pour my morning coffee, I will write one sentence
        in my journal. I celebrate by saying 'yes!'"

       "After I close my laptop at the end of work, I will do five
        pushups. I celebrate by smiling and nodding."

       "After I sit down on the toilet, I will do ten breaths of
        meditation. I celebrate by feeling proud."

   Rules:
   - ANCHOR must be a behavior that already happens reliably without
     your effort (existing strong habit).
   - BEHAVIOR must be tiny — doable on your worst day.
   - CELEBRATION must happen within ~3 seconds and feel positive.

THE TINY TEST

   For each candidate behavior, ask:
   - Can I do this on my worst day (tired, sick, depressed,
     traveling)? If no, it's not tiny enough.
   - Does it take less than 30 seconds? If no, can it be smaller?
   - Does it require any setup, special equipment, or schedule
     coordination? If yes, simplify or remove.

   Tiny habits feel almost embarrassingly small. That's the design,
   not a flaw.

THE ANCHOR-SELECTION CRITERIA

   A good anchor is:
   - Already reliable (you do it almost every day)
   - At a clean transition (after one activity, before another)
   - Time-correlated (similar time of day each occurrence)
   - In a stable location (same physical setting)

   Bad anchors:
   - Behaviors that depend on mood, motivation, or others
   - Behaviors that vary widely in timing or location
   - Behaviors you're also trying to change

   Strong anchor candidates: morning coffee, brushing teeth, sitting
   down at desk, parking the car, commuting transitions, putting
   children to bed.

THE CELEBRATION RESEARCH NOTE

   Fogg argues — and behavioral research broadly supports — that
   habit formation is faster with immediate positive feedback (within
   ~3 seconds) than with delayed reward.

   The brain's habit-encoding systems (basal ganglia + dopamine)
   reinforce behaviors associated with positive emotional state.
   Manufactured emotion (a deliberate "yes!" or visible smile)
   functions as feedback even though it's self-generated.

   Many habits fail in formation because they have no celebration
   step — the new behavior gets done but doesn't feel positive,
   so it doesn't lock in. Adding the celebration is a small
   structural fix with large effect.

THE GROWING-A-HABIT ARC

   Stage 1: Tiny habit (the design)
       Five pushups after closing laptop.

   Stage 2: Habit becomes automatic
       Four to twelve weeks. The behavior happens without conscious
       prompt — anchor triggers it directly.

   Stage 3: Voluntary growth
       Some practitioners expand naturally — five pushups becomes
       ten, then fifteen, then a full routine. Growth is voluntary,
       not imposed.

   Stage 4: Optional formalization
       If desired, the grown habit can become a scheduled practice.
       Many people stop here at "satisfying minimum" and don't grow,
       which is also valid.

   Critical: don't try to skip to a full routine in stage 1.
   Forcing growth before automaticity breaks the model.

THE FAILURE-DIAGNOSIS TEMPLATE

   If a habit isn't sticking, diagnose:

   Q1: Was the behavior tiny enough?
       If no: shrink it. Five pushups → two pushups → one pushup.

   Q2: Was the anchor reliable?
       If no: pick a different, stronger anchor.

   Q3: Was there a celebration?
       If no: add one immediately after the behavior.

   Q4: Did I try to start multiple habits at once?
       If yes: focus on one until it's automatic, then add the next.

   Q5: Was I in an unstable life context (travel, illness, major
   change)?
       If yes: pause the habit; restart when context stabilizes.
       Don't blame yourself; blame the conditions.

THE INSTITUTIONAL APPLICATION

   For organizational behavior change (compliance, safety, learning,
   engagement):

   - Don't rely on exhortation, training videos, or willpower
   - Reduce ability barriers — make the desired behavior the path
     of least resistance (defaults, simple processes)
   - Engineer prompts at decision points (signs, software cues,
     ritual moments)
   - Provide immediate positive feedback (small acknowledgments,
     not just quarterly reviews)

   The same B = MAP equation that governs personal habits governs
   organizational ones. Programs that ignore the equation and rely
   on motivation predictably underperform.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. NOT TINY ENOUGH
        The behavior is "small" by adult standards but still has
        ability barriers. Recovery: shrink further until the
        behavior is laughably easy.

   2. WEAK ANCHOR
        The anchor doesn't reliably trigger the behavior. Recovery:
        pick a stronger anchor or a more obvious transition point.

   3. NO CELEBRATION
        The behavior is done but doesn't feel positive. Recovery:
        explicitly celebrate, even theatrically.

   4. STARTING TOO MANY HABITS
        Multiple new habits compete for cognitive bandwidth.
        Recovery: one at a time; add the next when the first is
        automatic.

   5. SCALING UP TOO FAST
        Forcing growth before automaticity breaks the design.
        Recovery: stay tiny until the behavior is automatic, even
        if it feels insufficient. Growth comes after.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Make it tiny enough that motivation barely matters. The smallness is the design feature, not a temporary scaffolding. Tiny habits feel embarrassingly small; that's correct. (2) Anchor to existing reliable routines, not to schedules or motivation. The anchor is the prompt; an unreliable prompt produces unreliable behavior. Pick anchors from your strongest existing habits. (3) Celebrate immediately. Manufactured positive emotion within seconds of the behavior accelerates habit formation more than delayed rewards. The celebration is a small structural element with large effect. (4) One habit at a time. Multiple simultaneous new habits exceed cognitive bandwidth and produce zero adoption. Lock in one before adding the next; the compounding produces large change over months even when each individual habit is small.
