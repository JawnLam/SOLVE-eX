# Stakeholder Power-Interest Grid

**One-line summary:** 2×2 grid (Power × Interest) classifies stakeholders into four engagement strategies — Manage Closely, Keep Satisfied, Keep Informed, Monitor.

**When to reach for it:** Any initiative with multiple stakeholders whose support, indifference, or opposition could shape the outcome — change programs, project kickoffs, executive proposals, organizational redesign.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

The Power-Interest Grid forces explicit triage of stakeholder attention. Most initiatives fail not because the analysis was wrong but because effort was spent in the wrong places — heavy engagement with low-power critics while the actual decision-makers were undermanaged. The grid prevents that by separating two axes that are usually conflated:

1. **Power** — the stakeholder's ability to affect the outcome (formal authority, budget control, political capital, veto rights)
2. **Interest** — how much the outcome matters to them (whether they care enough to act)

The four resulting quadrants each get a distinct posture. High-power-high-interest stakeholders need active management; high-power-low-interest stakeholders need to be kept satisfied so they don't activate; low-power-high-interest stakeholders need to be informed so they become advocates; low-power-low-interest stakeholders just need monitoring in case they shift.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

People over-engage with stakeholders who are loud (high interest) regardless of whether they actually matter (power). The grid corrects for that bias. It also surfaces the dangerous quadrant most leaders miss: high-power-low-interest stakeholders. They look quiet, so they get ignored — until the initiative crosses their threshold of interest and they suddenly intervene with full power. "Keep Satisfied" isn't busywork; it's an early-warning investment.

For consulting and change work specifically, the grid produces an explicit allocation rule for the scarcest resource: senior leader attention. Naming each stakeholder's quadrant on a single page means follow-up disagreements become disagreements about *placement* (productive) rather than disagreements about *whether* to engage someone (political).

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=========================================================================================|
| Step |                                       Action                                            |
|======|=========================================================================================|
|    1 | List every stakeholder relevant to the initiative — individuals or groups.              |
|    2 | For each, score Power (Low / Medium / High) — what they can do to affect the outcome.   |
|    3 | For each, score Interest (Low / Medium / High) — how much the outcome matters to them.  |
|    4 | Plot each on the 2×2 grid (collapse Medium into nearest quadrant or use 3×3).           |
|    5 | Apply the per-quadrant strategy (see grid below).                                       |
|    6 | Define a specific engagement action per high-priority stakeholder.                      |
|    7 | Re-run after major events; positions shift as the initiative progresses.                |
|======|=========================================================================================|
```

> **Common failure mode:** Treating "Keep Satisfied" as "ignore." High-power-low-interest stakeholders are the most dangerous quadrant precisely because they look benign. Their interest can spike from a single trigger event — a board member's casual question, a competitor's announcement, a press article — and once they activate, they can override months of work. Allocate ~20% of stakeholder time to keeping this quadrant satisfied even though it feels unproductive.
