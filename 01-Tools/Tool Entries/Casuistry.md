# Casuistry

**One-line summary:** Case-based moral reasoning that starts from clear paradigm cases (where the moral verdict is uncontroversial) and reasons by analogy to harder cases — building moral knowledge from the bottom up rather than applying abstract principles top down.

**When to reach for it:** Medical ethics consultations, legal ethics analysis, business-ethics committees, complex applied-ethics situations where principles conflict, professional ethics in any field with case-based traditions, and any moral question where saying "the rule is..." doesn't quite resolve it.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Albert Jonsen and Stephen Toulmin's *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988) rehabilitated **case-based moral reasoning** after centuries of pejorative use ("casuistic" came to mean sophistical / dishonest, especially after Pascal's anti-Jesuit *Provincial Letters*). The framework's core method:

1. Identify **paradigm cases** where the moral verdict is uncontroversial (clear examples of right and wrong action).
2. Identify the **morally relevant features** of each paradigm case.
3. For the case in question, ask which paradigm it most resembles, on which dimensions.
4. Reason by analogy: if the new case shares features with a clear paradigm, the verdict tends in that direction.
5. Where the new case diverges from paradigms, articulate why and how the divergence affects the moral verdict.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **moral reasoning is often more reliably done bottom-up than top-down.** Top-down application of abstract principles (always tell the truth; maximize utility; act on universalizable maxims) frequently produces hard cases where principles conflict or yield counterintuitive answers. Casuistry handles hard cases by anchoring them to clearer cases, where moral judgment is less contested. The judgment in the new case follows from analogy, not from principle-derivation.

A second insight: **medical, legal, and business ethics are largely casuistic in practice.** A medical-ethics committee deciding about an end-of-life case doesn't typically derive an answer from utilitarianism or deontology; it consults case literature ("how have similar cases been resolved?") and reasons by analogy. The same is true of legal ethics (precedent), business ethics (case studies), and professional codes generally. Casuistry names what these practices are doing.

A third insight: **casuistry is compatible with theory but doesn't require it.** A casuist may use deontological or consequentialist reasoning to articulate why a paradigm case has its verdict, but the case itself is the anchor, not the theory. This is why casuistry can produce stable judgments across communities with different ethical theories — the cases provide a shared starting point.

The pejorative connotation comes from the practice's susceptibility to abuse: stretching cases to justify what the casuist already wants. Pascal's critique was largely accurate as applied to the worst Jesuit casuists. Modern casuistry (Jonsen, Toulmin) explicitly addresses this — disciplined casuistry requires honest case description and acknowledgment of disanalogies.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The principle-conflict paralysis.** When deontology says one thing and consequentialism says another, top-down theorizing gets stuck. Casuistry redirects: which paradigm case is this most like? The analogy often resolves where principle-comparison doesn't.
2. **The abstract-theory disconnection.** Abstract ethical theory often feels remote from concrete decisions. Casuistry brings the analysis to specifics: this case, those cases, the differences between them. Practitioners report it as more usable in real practice.
3. **The cross-theory communication failure.** Practitioners with different ethical commitments (deontologist, utilitarian, virtue ethicist) often can't agree on principles but can agree on case verdicts. Casuistry produces shared ground for collaborative judgment.

For ethics committees, professional ethics consultations, and any setting where moral judgments must be made jointly across people with different ethical theories, casuistry is the practical method most teams rely on, whether or not they call it that.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | State the case in question. What are the facts? Who's involved?                  |
|    2 | Identify paradigm cases on each side. What clearly-right and clearly-wrong       |
|      | analogous cases exist?                                                           |
|    3 | Articulate the morally relevant features of each paradigm. Why is the           |
|      | clearly-right case clearly right? Why is the clearly-wrong case clearly wrong?  |
|    4 | Compare the case in question to the paradigms. Which features does it share?    |
|      | Where does it diverge?                                                           |
|    5 | Reason by analogy. The verdict tends toward whichever paradigm the case more    |
|      | resembles on relevant dimensions.                                                |
|    6 | Articulate the disanalogies honestly. Where the case differs from the paradigm, |
|      | does the difference affect the moral verdict?                                    |
|    7 | Cross-check by considering competing analogies. Could a different paradigm be   |
|      | a closer match? Why or why not?                                                  |
|    8 | Issue a verdict, with explicit reasoning about which paradigm anchors it and    |
|      | which features are doing the moral work.                                        |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE CASUISTIC ANALYSIS TEMPLATE

   Case in question: __________________________________________

   Facts:
       Who: ____________________________________________________
       What: ___________________________________________________
       When/where: _____________________________________________
       Why: ____________________________________________________

   PARADIGM CASES (cases where the moral verdict is uncontroversial):

   Clearly-permissible paradigm:
       Description: ____________________________________________
       Why uncontroversial: ____________________________________
       Morally relevant features: ______________________________

   Clearly-impermissible paradigm:
       Description: ____________________________________________
       Why uncontroversial: ____________________________________
       Morally relevant features: ______________________________

   ANALOGICAL COMPARISON

   How does our case compare to clearly-permissible paradigm?
       Shared features: _______________________________________
       Different features: ____________________________________

   How does our case compare to clearly-impermissible paradigm?
       Shared features: _______________________________________
       Different features: ____________________________________

   Which paradigm does our case more closely resemble?
       _____________________________________________________

   Verdict (case in question): ________________________________
   Reasoning: which paradigm features carry weight and why:
       _____________________________________________________

THE COMPETING-ANALOGIES STRESS-TEST

   For any casuistic conclusion, ask: could a different paradigm
   be invoked as the closer match?

   Alternative paradigm: ______________________________________
   How our case resembles it: _________________________________
   Does this change the verdict? Y / N

   If yes: which paradigm fits better? Why?

   Honest casuistry acknowledges multiple plausible analogies and
   defends the choice. Sloppy casuistry picks the convenient
   analogy without acknowledging alternatives.

THE CASE-LIBRARY DISCIPLINE

   For casuistry to work in a domain, the field needs a stable
   library of paradigm cases — cases the relevant community
   recognizes as having clear moral verdicts.

   Examples:
       Medical ethics: Tarasoff (duty to warn), Karen Quinlan
       (right to die), case literature on informed consent
       Legal ethics: model cases on attorney-client privilege,
       conflict of interest
       Business ethics: Tylenol recall (handling crisis), Enron
       (accounting fraud), Volkswagen emissions (deception)

   Building a personal / institutional case library is part of
   becoming proficient in casuistic ethics. Reasoning is faster
   when the paradigm cases are immediately accessible.

THE PRINCIPLE-VS-PARADIGM RELATIONSHIP

   Casuistry doesn't reject principles; it relates differently
   to them.

   Principle-first:
       Start with abstract principles → derive case verdicts.
       Strengths: theoretical coherence.
       Weaknesses: principle conflict, abstraction from practice.

   Case-first (casuistry):
       Start with paradigm verdicts → reason by analogy.
       Strengths: practical traction, cross-theory agreement.
       Weaknesses: vulnerable to motivated case-stretching.

   The mature practice uses both: principles articulate why
   paradigms have their verdicts; paradigms anchor reasoning when
   principles conflict.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. CHERRY-PICKING PARADIGMS
        Choosing the paradigm that supports the desired verdict.
        Recovery: identify both supportive and opposing paradigms;
        compare honestly.

   2. INSUFFICIENT ATTENTION TO DISANALOGIES
        Treating analogies as identities. Recovery: explicit
        acknowledgment of differences and assessment of their
        moral relevance.

   3. NO STABLE CASE LIBRARY
        Inventing cases ad hoc rather than drawing on established
        paradigms. Recovery: in any domain, learn the canonical
        cases. They're the shared resource.

   4. PEJORATIVE-USE BAGGAGE
        Some audiences hear "casuistry" and think "sophistry."
        Recovery: use the term carefully; emphasize discipline
        and honesty in case-comparison.

   5. PRINCIPLE-AVERSION
        Treating casuistry as anti-theory. Recovery: theory
        articulates why paradigms have their verdicts; both
        principles and cases serve.

THE DISCIPLINED-CASUISTRY CHECKLIST

   For any casuistic argument:
   [ ] At least one supportive paradigm identified
   [ ] At least one opposing paradigm identified
   [ ] Morally relevant features of paradigms articulated
   [ ] Analogies and disanalogies explicit
   [ ] Competing analogies considered
   [ ] Verdict stated with reasoning anchored in paradigms

   Disciplined casuistry passes this checklist. Sloppy casuistry
   skips multiple items.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Identify paradigm cases on both sides. The most common casuistic failure is invoking only the favorable paradigm. Honest casuistry compares the case to both supportive and opposing paradigms before settling on a verdict. (2) Acknowledge disanalogies. Cases are rarely identical; the differences matter. The discipline is naming the differences and assessing their moral relevance, not pretending they don't exist. (3) Build a case library. In any domain, casuistry depends on shared paradigm cases the community recognizes. Building a personal / institutional case library is part of proficiency. (4) Combine with principle-based reasoning. Casuistry isn't anti-theory; it's a different starting point. Principles articulate why paradigms have their verdicts; paradigms anchor reasoning when principles conflict. The mature practice uses both.
