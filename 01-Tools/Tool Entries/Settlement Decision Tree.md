# Settlement Decision Tree

**One-line summary:** Quantitative tool for settlement valuation — probability of winning × expected damages = expected value at trial, compared to settlement offers plus cost of trial, factoring risk tolerance.

**When to reach for it:** Evaluating whether to accept a settlement offer or proceed to trial, especially in cases where the trial outcome is uncertain and the parties' risk tolerances may diverge from pure expected-value calculation.

## Purpose

The Settlement Decision Tree is a specific application of expected-value decision tree analysis to legal settlement decisions. Computation: probability of winning × expected damages if win = expected value at trial. Subtract estimated trial costs (legal fees, time) and discount for time-to-trial. Compare to the settlement offer. Critically: the result is the risk-neutral expected value; real plaintiffs and defendants have risk tolerances. Risk-averse plaintiffs (who can't afford a zero outcome) accept settlements below expected value; risk-tolerant plaintiffs hold out. Distinct from general expected-value decision trees: settlement-specific factors include the asymmetry of trial costs (paid even if lose), the time-value of waiting, the emotional cost of continued litigation, and the precedent-setting concern.

## How To Use

Build the decision tree for the case: probability of winning each major issue, expected damages range if won, expected costs (legal fees, expert fees, time), time to trial. Compute risk-neutral expected value at trial = P(win) × E[damages | win] − E[trial costs]. Compare to the settlement offer (net of legal fees to date). The numerical comparison gives the risk-neutral answer. Now adjust for risk tolerance: a risk-averse client may rationally accept settlement well below expected value; a risk-tolerant client may rationally reject offers above. Also factor non-monetary considerations: precedent, reputation, time, emotional toll. The 'walk-away number' (minimum acceptable settlement) emerges from this analysis. Used by trial attorneys advising clients and by mediators framing offers.
