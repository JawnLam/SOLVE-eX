# Schenkerian Analysis

**One-line summary:** Heinrich Schenker's method for analyzing tonal music — every piece reduces to a fundamental structure (Ursatz) elaborated through layers of voice-leading; analyst strips surface to reveal background.

**When to reach for it:** Analyzing tonal music (Western classical music from approximately Bach through early 20th c.) for its underlying structural architecture, where surface-level features must be related to deeper voice-leading patterns.

## Purpose

Heinrich Schenker (1868-1935) developed an analytical method that treats tonal music as elaboration of a fundamental structure (Ursatz) — typically a descent in the upper voice from 3, 5, or 8 to 1 over a bass arpeggiation I-V-I. Surface music is interpreted as layered elaborations: foreground (surface notes), middle-ground (intermediate structural levels), background (the Ursatz itself). The analyst progressively strips elaboration to reveal underlying structure. Distinct from set-theory analysis (used for atonal music) and from purely formal analysis. Foundational for Western music theory pedagogy; the method's claim is that great tonal music exhibits this layered coherence and that the layered analysis reveals the work's deep architecture.

## How To Use

(For a music theory student or analyst.) Begin with the score of a tonal work. Identify the home key. Begin foreground reduction: identify which notes are essential and which are decorative (neighboring tones, passing tones, suspensions). Strip the decorative. Identify the middle-ground voice-leading — what is the underlying line in the upper voice and bass? Continue stripping. Identify the background — the Ursatz. Notate the reduction graphically using Schenker's distinctive symbols (beams, slurs, stem direction). The graph displays the analytical claim about how the surface relates to deeper structure. Schenkerian graphs are dense; learning to read them takes substantial training. The analytical claim is then defended in prose. Used in music theory pedagogy; remains controversial in some circles (questions about cultural specificity, formalism, eurocentrism).
