# Sketchnoting

**One-line summary:** A visual-notetaking practice that combines words, simple icons, containers, and connectors to capture and structure ideas in real time — used to think through complexity, retain learning, and communicate with others.

**When to reach for it:** Listening to a complex talk or meeting, working through a problem alone, capturing a workshop in real time, summarizing a book or article into something you'll actually revisit, planning a project visually before writing it up, and any case where pure linear notes are losing the structure of the material.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Sketchnoting is the practice of taking notes that combine **typography, images, structure, containers, and connectors** rather than running prose. Mike Rohde formalized the modern practice in *The Sketchnote Handbook* (2012), drawing on a longer tradition of visual thinking (David Sibbet, the International Forum of Visual Practitioners) and on the cognitive observation that **dual-coded** material (words + images) is retained substantially better than words alone.

The non-obvious operational insight: **sketchnoting is a thinking tool, not an art form.** Rohde's deliberate framing — "ideas, not art" — is part of the methodology, not a humility move. The value of the practice comes from the structure that emerges as you decide what to draw, what to write, what to enclose in a container, and what to connect with an arrow. Drawing skill is irrelevant; legibility and consistent visual vocabulary are sufficient.

The five core elements:

1. **Typography** — varied lettering (size, weight, style) to mark hierarchy
2. **Icons / images** — simple drawings that stand for concepts (lightbulb = idea, person = stakeholder, gear = process)
3. **Containers** — boxes, clouds, banners, scrolls that group content
4. **Connectors** — arrows, lines, paths that show relationships
5. **White space** — deliberate unfilled area that lets the eye rest and structure breathe

The cognitive payoff has multiple sources. Real-time sketchnoting forces summarization (you can't write down everything, so you must decide what matters); spatial layout makes relational structure visible (which idea is upstream of which); dual coding aids retention; and the resulting artifact is dramatically more revisitable than linear text — a sketchnote is "scannable" in a way that a page of prose is not.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes sketchnoting prevents:

1. **The transcription trap.** Note-takers attempting to capture everything in linear prose end up with text dense enough that they will never re-read it. Sketchnoting's bandwidth limit (you cannot draw as fast as someone speaks) forces selection — and selection IS the cognitive work.
2. **The flat-structure problem.** Linear notes lose hierarchical and relational structure: which idea contains which, which precedes which, which contradicts which. Sketchnoting makes structure visible because spatial relationship encodes relational meaning.
3. **The non-revisited-archive problem.** Pages of bullet-pointed notes from talks accumulate in notebooks and apps unread. Sketchnotes are visually distinctive and dense in a way that re-reading them is fast and rewarding — they get revisited, where prose notes don't.

For consulting and knowledge work, sketchnoting is also a high-leverage *communication* tool: a one-page sketchnote of a complex meeting, recommendation, or workshop output is easier for executives to absorb than a multi-page write-up, and it forces the author to find the actual structure of the content (which often surfaces problems with the underlying thinking).

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Prepare: blank page, reliable pen (one weight is fine; two is plenty), no eraser. |
|      | Erasers slow you down and signal that mistakes are expensive — they aren't.       |
|    2 | Establish a frame: title at top, date / context at corner, source if relevant.    |
|    3 | Listen / read for the spine: what is the main thing the source is doing? Capture |
|      | that early, large, in the page's primary location.                                |
|    4 | As ideas come, decide for each: word or icon? Inside container or floating?      |
|      | Connected to what? The decisions are the work.                                   |
|    5 | Use containers (boxes, clouds, banners) to group related ideas. Use connectors   |
|      | (arrows, dashed lines) to show relationships between groups.                     |
|    6 | Vary type: bigger / bolder for important, smaller / lighter for supporting.      |
|      | Hierarchy through visual weight, not bullets.                                    |
|    7 | Leave white space deliberately. A sketchnote that fills every pixel is illegible. |
|    8 | After: review within 24 hours. Spend 5 minutes annotating, fixing, completing.   |
|      | This consolidation step is where the retention payoff actually accrues.           |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FIVE ELEMENTS (master these and the rest follows)

   1. TYPOGRAPHY
       - Plain print:        body text
       - BOLD CAPS:          section headers / emphasis
       - Outlined letters:   titles
       - Script / italic:    labels / captions
       Rule: 2-3 lettering styles per page is plenty. More = chaos.

   2. ICONS (build a personal vocabulary)
       Idea:        lightbulb
       Question:    question mark
       Important:   star, exclamation
       Person:      stick figure / circle-head
       Group:       multiple stick figures
       Process:     gear, arrow chain
       Time:        clock, calendar
       Money:       dollar sign, coin
       Risk:        warning triangle, skull
       Growth:      upward arrow, plant
       Decline:     downward arrow
       Decision:    diamond, fork
       Communication: speech bubble, envelope

       Rule: keep icons SIMPLE. A circle-with-two-dots-and-a-line for a face
       is fine. Anatomical correctness is unnecessary.

   3. CONTAINERS
       - Box:        ordinary group
       - Rounded box: softer / process group
       - Cloud:      uncertain / aspirational
       - Banner / scroll: title / important callout
       - Speech bubble: quote / dialogue
       Rule: containers create visual chunking. Without them the page is a
       crowded list.

   4. CONNECTORS
       - Solid arrow:         causal / sequential
       - Dashed arrow:        weaker / inferred relationship
       - Two-way arrow:       mutual influence
       - Branching tree:      decomposition
       - Bracket / curly:     grouping a sequence
       Rule: connectors carry as much meaning as boxes. Use deliberately.

   5. WHITE SPACE
       Approximately 30-40% of the page should be empty in a finished
       sketchnote. White space defines structure.

THE PAGE-LAYOUT TEMPLATES (pick one, then go)

   GRID (most reliable)
       +--------+--------+
       | topic1 | topic2 |
       +--------+--------+
       | topic3 | topic4 |
       +--------+--------+

   RADIAL (for centered topic with branches)
            topicB
              |
       topicA — CENTER — topicC
              |
            topicD

   TIMELINE (for sequential content)
       start ────►─── event1 ────►─── event2 ────►─── end

   PATHWAY (for argument or story arc)
       start ───→ ··· ───→ ··· ───→ end
                       ↑
                       branch / aside

   POPCORN (free-form, content drives layout)
       Whatever shape emerges. Hardest for beginners; most flexible.

   For first 20 sketchnotes, use Grid. For next 20, try Radial and Timeline.
   Popcorn is a graduate-level practice.

THE 24-HOUR REVIEW

   Within 24 hours of capture:
       [ ] Re-read; annotate any line that's now unclear
       [ ] Add icons or color to anything that should be more prominent
       [ ] Connect anything you missed connecting
       [ ] Add a one-sentence summary at the bottom: what was THIS really
            about?

   This step is what converts capture into retention. Sketchnotes without
   the review step are about as useful as prose notes without re-reading.

THE ANTI-PATTERNS

   - Spending more than 30 seconds on any single element. The bandwidth
     constraint is the point.
   - Erasing. Just keep going. The mistakes are part of the page.
   - Trying to be pretty. The goal is structure, not aesthetics.
   - Using more than 3 lettering styles, more than 5 colors, or more than
     a couple container types per page. Variety creates clutter.
   - Filling every pixel. Save the white space.

WHAT TO PRACTICE FIRST

   1. Build a personal icon vocabulary: 20-30 icons you can draw in 3
      seconds each. Practice them on a single page until each is reliable.
   2. Practice one layout template (Grid) on five sketchnotes before
      experimenting.
   3. Always do the 24-hour review. This is the discipline that produces
      payoff.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) The skill is selection, not drawing. The cognitive work happens when you decide what to capture, what to skip, where to place it, and how to connect it — drawing skill is incidental. Practitioners with no art training produce sketchnotes that are operationally equivalent to those of trained illustrators after the first ~50 pages of practice. (2) The 24-hour review converts capture into retention. Without it, sketchnoting is a very expensive way to take notes you won't re-read; with it, the retention curve is dramatically better than for linear notes. (3) Container and connector vocabulary carries as much meaning as the icons themselves. New sketchnoters under-use containers (everything floats) or over-use them (every word is in a box). Calibrate by intent: containers should carry meaning. (4) Sketchnotes are also a strong external-communication artifact, not just a personal tool. A one-page sketchnote of a complex topic is often more useful to an executive than a multi-page memo. The discipline of producing one forces structural clarity that multi-page prose can mask.
