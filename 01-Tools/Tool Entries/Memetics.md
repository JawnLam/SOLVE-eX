# Memetics

**One-line summary:** A framework that treats ideas, phrases, practices, and images as replicators undergoing variation, selection, and replication — used to analyze why some cultural units spread and persist while others die, and to design content and narratives that propagate.

**When to reach for it:** Brand and content strategy (why does some content spread and other content die?), political and policy communication (why does this slogan stick?), organizational change (why do some new practices propagate while others stay in pilot?), cultural-evolution analysis, and any case where an idea's spread or stagnation is the puzzle.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Richard Dawkins introduced the term **meme** in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) as a cultural analog to the gene: a unit of cultural transmission that propagates by being copied from mind to mind, undergoes variation in copying, and is subject to selection pressure (some memes get copied more than others; some die out). The framework treats ideas, phrases, melodies, fashions, religious practices, and behaviors as replicators competing for mental real estate — and analyzes their dynamics using selection-pressure language imported from biology.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **a meme's success is not about its truth or its value; it's about its replication fitness in the cultural environment.** Memes that are easy to remember, easy to retransmit, emotionally engaging, and that fit existing mental schemas spread faster than memes that are merely more accurate. This explains why slogans and stories often outcompete careful explanations, why simple narratives outcompete nuanced ones, and why some ideas persist long after they're shown to be false.

The three Dawkins criteria for replicator success:

1. **Fidelity** — how accurately the meme is copied. High-fidelity memes (slogans, exact phrases, ritualized practices) preserve their form across many transmissions. Low-fidelity memes (vague concepts, technical ideas) drift and may be lost.
2. **Fecundity** — how many copies the meme generates per host. Memes that prompt their hosts to share them (jokes, controversial claims, shocking images) outcompete memes that are kept private.
3. **Longevity** — how durable the meme is in any one host. Memes that get embedded (catchy songs, religious doctrines, foundational stories) persist for years; memes that fade after one exposure don't accumulate hosts.

A successful meme balances all three — high fidelity, high fecundity, high longevity. Most ideas are weak on at least one. Strategic communication design targets all three simultaneously.

The framework is **an analogy, not a literal biological claim.** Whether memes are "really" replicators in a strict biological sense is contested. The operational point is that the variation-selection-replication frame yields useful predictions about cultural spread even if the biological analogy is imperfect.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "if it's true / good, it'll spread" failure.** Substantive truth and substantive value are weakly correlated with replication fitness. Public-health communications, technical accuracy, and "important" warnings often fail to spread because they lack memetic fitness — too long, too nuanced, too unrewarding to retransmit. Memetics names the gap.
2. **The viral-by-luck mistake.** Marketers and communicators sometimes treat virality as random or charismatic. The framework reveals it as the result of structural properties — fidelity, fecundity, longevity — that can be designed for. Viral content is rarely lucky; it usually has those properties even when its creators don't articulate them.
3. **The narrative-resilience surprise.** Bad stories about an organization, brand, or person sometimes persist long after they've been factually rebutted. Memetics explains why: the rebuttal often lacks the original story's memetic fitness. Narrative damage requires narrative repair, not factual repair.

For consulting and communication work, memetics is the discipline that asks not "is this true?" but "is this *transmissible*?" — and recognizes that the second question often determines outcomes regardless of the answer to the first.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the meme. What is the unit you're analyzing — a phrase, a story, an    |
|      | image, a practice?                                                              |
|    2 | Map the cultural environment. Who are the hosts? What other memes compete for   |
|      | their attention? What memes already occupy the niche?                           |
|    3 | Score fidelity. How precisely is the meme transmitted? Slogans (high) vs.       |
|      | technical concepts (low).                                                       |
|    4 | Score fecundity. How likely is each host to retransmit? What's the explicit or  |
|      | implicit incentive to share?                                                    |
|    5 | Score longevity. How durable is the meme in any one host? Days, weeks, years?  |
|    6 | Diagnose: which of the three properties is the meme weakest on? That's the      |
|      | leverage point for design or intervention.                                      |
|    7 | Design (if you're trying to spread something): tighten the formulation for      |
|      | fidelity, add retransmission hooks for fecundity, embed in something durable    |
|      | for longevity.                                                                  |
|    8 | Counter (if you're trying to suppress something): find the opposite properties  |
|      | of the target meme, then deploy a counter-meme strong on those properties.      |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE THREE-PROPERTY SCORECARD

   Meme: ___________________________________________________________

   FIDELITY (how accurately is it transmitted?)
       LOW           MEDIUM            HIGH
       drifts        partial form      preserved exactly
       Examples:
       Low — academic concepts that lay people simplify on retelling
       High — exact phrases ("Just do it"), rituals, jingles

   FECUNDITY (how often does each host retransmit?)
       LOW           MEDIUM            HIGH
       kept private  shared sometimes  hosts spontaneously share
       Examples:
       Low — technical knowledge people don't volunteer
       High — jokes, gossip, shocking images, controversial claims

   LONGEVITY (how durable in any one host?)
       LOW           MEDIUM            HIGH
       forgotten     persists weeks    persists for years
       Examples:
       Low — news cycle memes
       High — childhood songs, brand jingles, foundational stories

   Composite fitness ≈ Fidelity × Fecundity × Longevity

   Diagnose by the weakest property; that's the leverage point.

THE MEMETIC-DESIGN CATALOG (for spreading)

   FIDELITY-BOOSTERS:
       - Tight verbal formulations (slogans, names, mnemonics)
       - Visual icons (logos, gestures, postures)
       - Ritualized formats (call-and-response, set phrases)
       - Round numbers and simple structures

   FECUNDITY-BOOSTERS:
       - Emotional payload (humor, anger, awe, fear)
       - Status signal (sharing this makes the host look good)
       - Group identity marker (in-group vs. out-group)
       - Tribal-affiliation signaling
       - Curiosity gaps (incomplete information demanding completion)

   LONGEVITY-BOOSTERS:
       - Embedding in larger memeplexes (ideologies, brands, religions)
       - Emotional anchoring (associated with significant life events)
       - Repetition / spaced exposure
       - Practical utility (the meme does work for the host)

THE COUNTER-MEME PLAYBOOK

   For suppressing a damaging meme:

   1. Don't repeat the original meme to debunk it.
      Repetition strengthens fidelity. The negation trap (see Frame
      Analysis entry) applies directly.

   2. Build a counter-meme with stronger fitness on the SAME
      properties.
      If the bad meme is high-fecundity (people love sharing it),
      your counter must also be high-fecundity. Dry corrections fail.

   3. Crowd the niche.
      A new meme on the same topic occupies the cognitive slot the
      bad meme is using. Multiple competing narratives often produce
      better outcomes than single-narrative dominance.

   4. Wait for context shift.
      Memes are environment-dependent. Cultural / political / market
      context changes can collapse memes that were entrenched. Time
      and patience are sometimes the right tool.

THE INTERNET-MEME APPLICATION

   Modern internet memes (image macros, video formats, viral phrases)
   are an accelerated version of the same dynamic. Specific patterns:

   - High-fecundity formats: image-with-caption, short videos, twitter
     rejoinders. Designed for retransmission.

   - Variation/selection at scale: thousands of variants compete; the
     fittest survive. Watching which variants spread reveals what the
     environment selects for.

   - Mutation rate: image macros tolerate high mutation; specific
     phrases tolerate less. Match formulation rigidity to the target
     mutation rate.

   - Niche specificity: memes that work in one community fail in
     another. Cultural environment is local; spread is local until
     a crossover meme emerges.

   For brand / political communication using internet memes, the move
   is to seed multiple variants and observe which one the environment
   selects, rather than betting heavily on a single formulation.

THE MEMEPLEX CONCEPT

   Some memes survive better in clusters than individually. A
   memeplex is a coordinated set of memes that mutually reinforce
   replication.

   Examples:
   - Religions (doctrines, rituals, stories, communities)
   - Ideologies (axioms, narratives, identity markers)
   - Brand worlds (logo, slogan, story, archetype, customer ritual)
   - Corporate cultures (values, language, ceremonies, hierarchy
     symbols)

   Memeplexes are more stable than individual memes because each
   component reinforces the others. Disrupting a memeplex requires
   attacking multiple components simultaneously; attacking one is
   usually absorbed.

   For organizational change: cultures are memeplexes. Single-meme
   interventions ("our new value is X") rarely shift culture; the
   surrounding memeplex absorbs them. Effective culture change
   coordinates multiple meme changes.

THE ETHICAL CAVEAT

   Memetics treats cultural transmission descriptively — what spreads,
   not what should spread. Designing high-fitness memes is ethically
   neutral; the use is not. High-fitness false memes (conspiracy
   theories, propaganda, viral disinformation) cause real harm.

   When using the framework offensively (designing memes to spread),
   maintain a substantive truth standard alongside the fitness
   standard. The framework can be used for honest communication or
   for manipulation; choose deliberately.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Replication fitness is not the same as truth or value. The most important conceptual move is recognizing that they're decoupled — and treating them as separable design problems. Truth without fitness fails to spread; fitness without truth produces propaganda. Aim for both. (2) Diagnose by the weakest of the three properties (fidelity, fecundity, longevity). That's the leverage point. Most failed-to-spread ideas are weak on fecundity (no incentive to share) or fidelity (drifts in transmission); strong-but-failed memes are usually short on longevity. (3) Don't repeat the bad meme to debunk it. The negation trap and the fidelity-strengthening effect both apply. Build a counter-meme on the same fitness dimensions instead of arguing against the original on its terms. (4) Memeplexes are more stable than individual memes. For culture change and brand strategy, the unit of analysis is usually the memeplex, not single memes. Coordinated changes outperform single-meme interventions, which the surrounding memeplex tends to absorb.
