# Consequentialism

**One-line summary:** A moral framework holding that the rightness of action depends entirely on its consequences — that one ought to choose whichever action produces the best overall outcome — with utilitarianism (greatest happiness for greatest number) as its dominant historical form.

**When to reach for it:** Policy analysis where aggregate effects matter (public health, regulation, resource allocation), cost-benefit decisions, situations comparing multiple paths by their probable outcomes, and as one lens among several in any serious ethical analysis.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Consequentialism holds that **the rightness or wrongness of an action depends entirely on its consequences.** Classical utilitarianism — Bentham (1789), Mill (1861) — operationalizes this as: the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people affected. Modern variants substitute different value currencies (preference satisfaction, well-being broadly, capabilities, agency) but share the structural commitment: outcomes are what matter morally; rules and intentions are instrumentally good only insofar as they produce good outcomes.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **consequentialism forces honest calculation.** When asked "is this policy good?" deontology asks about rules; virtue ethics asks about character; consequentialism asks "good for whom, by how much, with what probability, over what time?" The forced calculation surfaces effects that intuition or rule-following misses. For policy and large-scale decision-making, this is often where consequentialism is most useful — its discipline of explicit accounting catches systematic harms that deontological reasoning can permit by adherence to rules.

A second insight: **consequentialism has well-known counterexamples.** The classic objections (organ harvesting, framing the innocent man to prevent riot, utility monsters) all have the structure: a consequentially-better outcome is achievable via what intuitively seems clearly wrong. Consequentialists handle these via rule consequentialism (rules with good aggregate effects should be followed even when individual exceptions would gain), or by enriching the value calculation (long-term effects on trust, on institutional integrity, on agent character).

A third insight: **consequentialism depends on uncertain prediction.** Real consequences are not fully knowable in advance. The framework's calculations therefore have epistemic gaps that opponents exploit; defenders argue that all moral frameworks face uncertainty and that explicit calculation under uncertainty beats hidden calculation.

The framework spans **act consequentialism** (each act assessed by its specific consequences) and **rule consequentialism** (follow the rules whose general adoption would produce the best consequences, even when an individual act-deviation would seem to produce locally better outcomes).

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The rule-fetishism trap.** Pure deontology can produce conclusions that ignore catastrophic consequences. Consequentialism's explicit calculation prevents the "rules over outcomes" pathology by forcing the question "what does this actually produce?"
2. **The hidden-tradeoff failure.** Many policies have aggregate effects that are obvious in consequentialist analysis and invisible in rule-based reasoning (whom does this help, whom does this hurt, by how much). The framework's discipline surfaces these.
3. **The intuition-only failure.** "It just feels wrong" is sometimes a moral intuition tracking real harm and sometimes status-quo bias dressed up as morality. Consequentialism's calculation forces the intuition to be cashed out in terms of who is affected and how — making it inspectable.

For policy analysts, regulators, and executives making allocation decisions, consequentialism is one of the four primary lenses (with deontology, virtue ethics, contractualism). Where it diverges from the others, the divergence is informative.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the action / policy / decision being evaluated.                         |
|    2 | Identify everyone affected, directly and indirectly. Be inclusive.               |
|    3 | For each affected party, identify the relevant consequences (well-being,         |
|      | preferences, opportunities, harms).                                              |
|    4 | Estimate magnitudes and probabilities. Numbers needn't be precise; ordinal       |
|      | rankings often suffice.                                                          |
|    5 | Aggregate. Total consequences across affected parties; compare alternatives.    |
|    6 | Stress-test. Are there long-term effects you missed? Effects on trust, on      |
|      | institutions, on character, on precedent? Add them.                             |
|    7 | Cross-check with deontology and virtue ethics. Where they diverge from           |
|      | consequentialist conclusion, the divergence is the substantive question.        |
|    8 | Decide, acknowledging uncertainty. Consequentialism's predictions are           |
|      | probabilistic; the framework doesn't claim certainty.                            |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE CONSEQUENTIALIST CALCULATION TEMPLATE

   Action / policy: _________________________________________________

   AFFECTED PARTIES (be inclusive — direct, indirect, future):

   Party              | Effect       | Magnitude  | Probability
   -------------------|--------------|------------|--------------
   ___________________| __________   | _________  | ___________
   ___________________| __________   | _________  | ___________
   ___________________| __________   | _________  | ___________
   ___________________| __________   | _________  | ___________
   ___________________| __________   | _________  | ___________

   Aggregate sense (overall positive or negative? roughly how large?):
       _________________________________________________________

   Compared to alternatives: better / worse / similar?
       _________________________________________________________

THE FOUR-DIMENSION CALCULATION

   For more thorough analysis, decompose effects across:

   1. WHOM (who is affected)
       Direct: ________________________________________________
       Indirect: ______________________________________________
       Future: ________________________________________________

   2. HOW MUCH (magnitude per party)
       Use ordinal ranking if numbers aren't available:
       severe / moderate / minor / negligible / positive

   3. HOW LIKELY (probability of effect)
       Certain / likely / possible / unlikely / negligible

   4. OVER WHAT TIME (immediate vs. long-term)
       Immediate: ___________________________________________
       Medium-term: ________________________________________
       Long-term: __________________________________________

   Long-term and second-order effects are most often missed.
   Common: a policy that's net-positive immediately but
   net-negative over a decade as institutional incentives shift.

THE ACT VS. RULE DISTINCTION

   ACT CONSEQUENTIALISM:
       Evaluate each individual action by its specific consequences.
       Strengths: maximally responsive to context.
       Weaknesses: justifies many counterintuitive cases (organ-
                    harvesting, framing innocents, etc.).

   RULE CONSEQUENTIALISM:
       Follow the rules whose general adoption produces the best
       consequences, even when an individual exception would
       seem to produce locally better outcomes.
       Strengths: closer to common moral intuitions; preserves
                   trust, institutions, character.
       Weaknesses: can collapse to deontology in practice; less
                    responsive to genuine special cases.

   For most policy / institutional ethics, RULE consequentialism
   is the operationally relevant variant. The act-rule distinction
   matters most in hard cases where they diverge.

THE COMMON COUNTEREXAMPLES (and responses)

   1. ORGAN HARVESTING
        Five patients need organs; one healthy person could supply
        them all. Act consequentialist arithmetic favors harvesting.
        Response: rule consequentialism objects (general rule
        produces catastrophic loss of trust in medicine); long-term
        effects matter; agent-relative considerations.

   2. UTILITY MONSTER
        A person who derives vastly more pleasure from any resource
        than anyone else; pure utility-maximizing favors giving
        everything to them. Response: implausible empirically;
        modern variants weight well-being more carefully than crude
        Benthamite hedonism.

   3. FRAMING THE INNOCENT
        Frame innocent person to prevent riot; net lives saved.
        Response: rule consequentialism; long-term effects on
        justice system; integrity-preservation.

   4. EXPERIENCE MACHINE
        Plug into a machine that gives you any subjective experience
        you choose. Pure hedonic calculation favors plugging in.
        Response: most people don't choose this, suggesting we value
        more than just experience-quality. Modern preference- and
        capability-based variants accommodate this.

   These cases are well-known in ethics; consequentialists have
   responses. Use them as stress-tests of your reasoning, not as
   refutations of the framework.

THE LONG-TERM-EFFECT CHECKLIST

   For any consequentialist analysis:
   [ ] Have I accounted for effects on trust?
   [ ] Have I accounted for effects on institutional integrity?
   [ ] Have I accounted for effects on character / virtue?
   [ ] Have I accounted for precedent and similar cases?
   [ ] Have I accounted for incentive effects on future behavior?
   [ ] Have I accounted for distribution (not just aggregate)?

   Most consequentialist failures are missed long-term effects —
   short-term good that produces longer-term bad. The checklist
   catches them.

THE DISTRIBUTION QUESTION

   Pure aggregate utilitarianism treats 100 utility split (50, 50)
   the same as (99, 1). Most modern variants reject this and weight
   distributional concerns:
       Prioritarianism: weight benefits to worse-off more heavily
       Egalitarianism: penalize inequality of outcomes
       Sufficientarianism: prioritize getting everyone above a
                          threshold

   For policy work, the distributional weighting often matters more
   than the aggregate. Ask: how is the welfare distributed, not
   just how large is the total?

THE QUICK-USE TEMPLATE

   Action: __________________________________________________

   Affected: ________________________________________________
   Net effect (aggregate): ___________________________________
   Distributional concerns: __________________________________
   Long-term / institutional effects: ________________________
   Probability of effects materializing: ____________________

   Verdict (consequentialist): permissible / impermissible /
   ambiguous

   Cross-check with other frameworks before final decision.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Be inclusive about affected parties. The most common consequentialist failure is missing affected parties — typically those who are distant in space, time, or social position. The framework's value depends on the inclusiveness of the calculation. (2) Account for long-term and institutional effects. Short-term aggregate good that produces longer-term institutional damage often dominates the long-run calculation. The framework's blind spot is usually here. (3) Use rule consequentialism for most institutional / policy work; act consequentialism for unique-case analysis. The variants converge in most cases; their divergence is the heart of hard cases. (4) Cross-check with deontology and virtue ethics. Where they converge, confidence is high. Where they diverge, the divergence is the substantive moral question — neither framework alone is sufficient.
