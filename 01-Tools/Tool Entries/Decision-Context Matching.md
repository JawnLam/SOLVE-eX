# Decision-Context Matching

**One-line summary:** A meta-tool for choosing decision processes by matching to context dimensions — stakes, reversibility, time pressure, expertise, pattern familiarity — synthesizing insights from Klein's RPD, Bezos's doors framework, and decision-science to ensure the process fits the decision rather than applying one approach universally.

**When to reach for it:** Designing decision rights and processes in organizations; coaching individuals on decision-making variability; before any significant decision (to choose the right approach); training decision-makers; and any context where over-processing low-stakes decisions or under-processing high-stakes decisions costs significant value.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Decision-context matching** picks decision process by context. The dimensions:

1. **Stakes** — low / medium / high. Magnitude of impact if right or wrong.
2. **Reversibility** — one-way (irreversible) or two-way (reversible). Bezos's framework.
3. **Time pressure** — immediate / short / long horizon. Forced speed of decision.
4. **Expertise level** — novice / intermediate / expert. Pattern library available.
5. **Pattern familiarity** — routine / novel. Has this situation been seen before?

The framework prescribes processes:

- **High stakes + one-way + low time pressure:** deliberate analysis with multiple stakeholders, pre-mortem, careful structure (maximum-discipline path).
- **High stakes + one-way + high time pressure:** fast structured decision (OODA-style) with senior decision-maker; document for later review.
- **Low stakes + two-way:** fast individual decision; experiment-and-learn.
- **Routine + expert:** RPD-style pattern recognition; act fast.
- **Novel + high stakes:** pause if possible; engage analytical tools; seek diverse input.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **a single decision-making approach is wrong for all decisions.** Organizations that always require committee approval over-process low-stakes; organizations that never use formal processes under-process high-stakes. Matching process to context produces the speed-quality balance that uniform approaches can't.

A second insight: **the dimensions interact.** A decision can be high-stakes but two-way (high-volume hiring with probation) — the reversibility moderates the stakes. Or low-stakes but one-way (small public statement) — irreversibility elevates importance. The matching considers all dimensions together, not each in isolation.

A third insight: **the framework empowers delegation.** Junior decision-makers can be empowered for two-way decisions; senior approval reserved for one-way / high-stakes / novel. This is the operating mechanism of "leverage" in scaled organizations.

A fourth insight: **the framework requires self-awareness about expertise.** Believing yourself an expert when you're not produces RPD-style fast decisions in novel situations — confident but wrong. Honest assessment of pattern-library coverage matters.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "uniform process" trap.** All decisions go through the same process; speed-quality balance is wrong for most.
2. **The "everything goes to senior" bottleneck.** Senior decision-makers process low-stakes decisions that juniors should make.
3. **The "individual decisions on high-stakes" failure.** Junior decisions on irreversible high-stakes situations without senior oversight.

For executives, organizational designers, decision coaches, and anyone responsible for decision rights / processes, decision-context matching is foundational meta-tool.

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FIVE-DIMENSION MATRIX

   STAKES:
       Low: limited impact if wrong
       Medium: meaningful impact
       High: substantial impact

   REVERSIBILITY:
       Two-way: easy to reverse if wrong
       One-way: hard or impossible to reverse

   TIME PRESSURE:
       Immediate: act now
       Short: hours to days
       Long: weeks to months available

   EXPERTISE:
       Novice: no relevant pattern library
       Intermediate: some patterns
       Expert: deep pattern library; this is well-
       trodden ground for me

   PATTERN FAMILIARITY:
       Routine: this has been seen many times
       Variant: variation on familiar pattern
       Novel: genuinely new

   The combination of dimensions produces the
   appropriate process.

THE PROCESS RECOMMENDATIONS

   HIGH STAKES + ONE-WAY + LOW TIME PRESSURE:
       Maximum discipline:
       Multi-stakeholder analysis
       Pre-mortem
       Structured frameworks (TOWS, Five Forces)
       Senior decision-maker(s)
       Documented reasoning
       Don't rush.

   HIGH STAKES + ONE-WAY + HIGH TIME PRESSURE:
       Fast structured decision:
       Senior decision-maker
       Time-boxed analysis (use the time available)
       Document for retrospective review
       (Crisis decisions; emergency response; military)

   HIGH STAKES + TWO-WAY:
       Moderately structured:
       Smaller group decision
       Pilot / experiment to test
       Plan for reversal if needed
       Less ceremony than full one-way process

   LOW STAKES + TWO-WAY:
       Fast individual decision:
       Junior empowered
       Try it; reverse if needed
       Bias for action

   LOW STAKES + ONE-WAY:
       Quick check; sometimes formal:
       Junior with awareness of irreversibility
       Sometimes senior consultation
       Document if precedent-setting

   ROUTINE + EXPERT:
       RPD-style fast pattern recognition:
       Act on pattern match
       No need for analytical detour

   NOVEL + ANY STAKES:
       Slow down. Engage analytical tools.
       Seek diverse perspectives.
       Build new pattern via this case.

THE WORKED EXAMPLES

   DECISION A: hire a junior engineer (multiple
   candidates).
       Stakes: medium (good hire vs. bad hire matters)
       Reversibility: two-way (probation period)
       Time pressure: short
       Expertise: hiring manager has done many similar
       hires
       Pattern familiarity: routine
       Process: hiring manager + 2-3 interviewers;
       structured but not exhaustive.

   DECISION B: acquire a competitor for $200M.
       Stakes: high
       Reversibility: one-way
       Time pressure: long (months of diligence)
       Expertise: experienced M&A team
       Pattern familiarity: variant (each acquisition
       is somewhat unique)
       Process: extensive diligence; multiple advisors;
       pre-mortem; board approval; senior decision-
       maker (CEO + board).

   DECISION C: respond to social-media crisis (1 hour
   to public statement).
       Stakes: high
       Reversibility: one-way (statement is archived)
       Time pressure: immediate
       Expertise: communications team
       Pattern familiarity: variant (each crisis has
       specifics)
       Process: fast structured; senior comms +
       legal + CEO sign-off in 1 hour; document.

   DECISION D: try a new email subject-line variant.
       Stakes: low
       Reversibility: two-way
       Time pressure: short
       Expertise: marketer
       Pattern familiarity: routine (A/B testing)
       Process: marketer decides; A/B test; iterate.

   Each decision gets a different process matched to
   its context.

THE EXPERTISE-CALIBRATION DISCIPLINE

   Honest expertise assessment is hard. Self-rated
   experts are often less expert than they think.

   Calibration check:
       Have I made similar decisions before?
       Did they go well?
       Was outcome dominated by skill or luck?
       Do I get reliable feedback in this domain?

   Without calibration, "expert RPD" produces fast
   confident wrong decisions in domains where you're
   not actually expert. The slow analytical path is
   the corrective.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. ONE-SIZE-FITS-ALL PROCESS
        Same approach for all decisions. Recovery:
        match per decision.

   2. WRONG DIMENSION ASSESSMENT
        Mis-assessing stakes / reversibility / etc.
        Recovery: explicit assessment per dimension.

   3. EXPERTISE OVERESTIMATE
        Thinking you're expert when you're not;
        applying RPD inappropriately. Recovery:
        calibration check; default to slower in
        novel situations.

   4. NO SENIOR ESCALATION FOR ONE-WAY HIGH-STAKES
        Junior decisions on high-stakes irreversible.
        Recovery: explicit decision rights.

   5. SENIOR BOTTLENECK FOR ROUTINE
        Senior involved in low-stakes decisions.
        Recovery: empowerment for two-way decisions.

   6. NO RETROSPECTIVE ON PROCESS
        Process used; never assessed for fit.
        Recovery: periodic review.

   7. CONFLATING DIMENSIONS
        Treating high-stakes as automatically one-way
        (often two-way exist). Recovery: assess each
        dimension independently.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Decision: __________________________________________

   Five-dimension assessment:
       Stakes: low / medium / high
       Reversibility: one-way / two-way
       Time pressure: immediate / short / long
       Expertise: novice / intermediate / expert
       Pattern familiarity: routine / variant / novel

   Process matched:
       Decision-maker: ______________________________
       Speed: _______________________________________
       Discipline: __________________________________
       Stakeholder breadth: _________________________

   Documentation: Y / N
   Retrospective: Y / N

   Calibration check:
       Honest about expertise level? Y / N
       Honest about pattern familiarity? Y / N
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Assess all five dimensions. Single-dimension matching (just stakes; just reversibility) misses the others. The full picture produces the right process. (2) Match process to context. Uniform process for all decisions is the dominant organizational failure mode. The matching produces both speed (where appropriate) and discipline (where needed). (3) Calibrate expertise honestly. Self-rated experts are often less expert than they think. RPD-style fast decisions in non-expert domains produce confident wrong outcomes; honest assessment is the corrective. (4) Empower for two-way; reserve discipline for one-way. The framework's organizational value is enabling junior decisions on reversible matters while protecting irreversible matters with senior involvement.
