# Required-Resource Check

**Also known as:** Capability Gate (field manual)

**One-line summary:** A pre-action gate that asks the actor to surface, explicitly, whether the resources required to execute an intended move are currently controlled — turning the unspoken question "can I act?" into a binary that produces either action or a generative sub-cycle.

**When to reach for it:** At the threshold of any action that crosses irreversibly into the world (a memo sent, a hire announced, a price changed, a confrontation initiated, a deal signed); when forward momentum is building and the conversation has skipped the question of capacity; when an actor is feeling pressure to "do something" before having checked what they have to spend; and in Phase 5–6 transitions where the user is about to commit to a path. Especially valuable when commitment feels obvious — the obviousness is when assumptions are at their most invisible.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Required-Resource Check** is the gate the ADAPT Loop places between the planning zone and the consequence zone. The decision discipline is honest: do I have enough resources — everything spendable, including cash, time, attention, political capital, relationships, credibility, information — to make this move (Field Manual p. 14)?

The gate has three honest answers, not two:

1. **Yes** — the actor proceeds to weigh options and act.
2. **No, but I can rescope** — shrink the objective until it fits available resources. Both this and the next move "loop back into the planning funnel. Neither is failure. The failure mode is to plow ahead anyway, hoping that resources will materialize once the action is under way. They generally do not." (Field Manual p. 15.)
3. **No, but I can rebuild** — delay action and gather more resources first. Required resources become **new desired resources added to the agenda**, opening a sub-cycle whose work is procurement of the missing resource (Lam 2020 p. 224, step 9; see also `Nested Sub-Cycle Discipline`).

The non-obvious framing is that **a No at the gate is not the framework's failure state — it is its most-used and most-generative move.** The dissertation's saturation tests across Charles, Jim, Nathan, Sarah, and Rusty all open sub-cycles on resource-check failure; the discipline is the cycle stack, not the immediate action. Treating No as failure is what produces the canonical premature-action error pattern.

A second insight: **the resources the gate audits are not the resources the actor thinks they audit.** Money and time are obvious; political capital, credibility, attention bandwidth, and relationship-cost are the resources that compound and that the gate most often catches as missing (see `9-Resource Portfolio Diagnostic` for the full taxonomy).

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Plow-ahead optimism.** The actor commits, expecting resources to materialize once the action is under way; they do not, and the move executes at the level of what was actually available, not what was promised.
2. **Treating No as failure.** The actor abandons the interest entirely when the gate returns No, when the strategically right move is to open a sub-cycle and procure the missing resource.
3. **Mis-naming the missing resource.** The actor identifies the resource as money or time when the actual gap is credibility, attention, or coalition support. The mis-naming produces a sub-cycle aimed at the wrong target.

For executives, founders, and any actor at the threshold of an irreversible move, the gate is the discipline that distinguishes capable action from optimistic motion.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

The check is a short, honest pause — not a long-form planning exercise.

1. **Name the intended action.** Write it down in one sentence. Vague intent ("push harder on the deal") makes the gate impossible to run; the gate needs an action specific enough that its resource cost can be estimated.
2. **List the resources the action will spend.** Audit across all nine resource types — Money, Hard Assets, Credibility, Attributes, Legitimacy, Information, Access, Title, Tribe — not just money and time. (See `9-Resource Portfolio Diagnostic`.)
3. **For each resource, ask: do I control enough of it now to spend what this action requires, with margin for the cycle's adverse alternative cost?** Margin matters because the adverse alternative is a real possible outcome (see `Decision Matrix Construction`).
4. **Form the gate verdict.** Yes / Rescope / Rebuild. If Yes, proceed to action and Phase 6 execution. If Rescope, return to the funnel and shrink the objective. If Rebuild, identify the missing resource and open a sub-cycle to procure it.
5. **If Rebuild: name the sub-cycle target.** The missing resource is now a new interest. Who controls it, and what would move them to make it available? This re-enters the planning funnel with the new interest at the top.

A useful test: if the user cannot state, in one sentence, what they would do if the gate returned a clean No, the gate has not actually been run.

## Worked Example

A founder is closing a Series A. Her interest: secure capital to scale go-to-market over 18 months. Her objective: close on favorable terms with a lead investor whose presence on her cap table strengthens rather than constrains her. The intended action is to sign the first term sheet received, from Pinemark Partners.

Running the gate:
- **Money:** nine months of runway — adequate margin to walk away if needed. ✓
- **Time:** the term sheet has a 14-day no-shop. ✓ (with discipline)
- **Credibility:** strong product traction, two other interested firms. ✓
- **Information:** familiar with Pinemark partner, three prior meetings. ✓
- **Tribe (advisors / board):** seed-stage board is two friendly investors and herself; no senior CFO or experienced corporate counsel. **— missing.**
- **Access:** Pinemark is the only fully active term sheet; Stelladin signaled informally. ✓ (thin)

The gate returns a qualified Yes: she can act, but she must act with the awareness that her negotiating leverage is real but thin. The qualified verdict reshapes the matrix: signing as-is locks in the cost of an under-resourced negotiation; countering preserves room to mature the negotiation while a parallel option warms. The qualified gate is what disqualifies "sign as-is" before the matrix is even run. (Adapted from Field Manual ch. 8, Cathna / Anna Pelham, pp. 26-27.)

## Common Failure Modes

- **Asking only about money and time.** The most common political-capital depletion is invisible from a money-and-time audit. Use the 9-resource taxonomy.
- **Skipping the margin question.** "I have enough resources for the preferred position" is not the same as "I have enough resources for the adverse alternative." The gate audits margin against AA, not headline.
- **Treating Rescope as defeat.** Rescoping is one of two legitimate responses to a No; it is not failure. The failure mode is plowing ahead anyway.
- **Naming the wrong missing resource.** The actor identifies "I need more time" when the actual gap is "I need a sponsor with standing to push this." Time is the comfortable answer; the political-capital answer is uncomfortable but real.
- **Running the gate after the action is irreversible.** The gate exists to catch errors before the planning zone closes. Once the action enters the world, the gate has no remedial function — only the perception gap audit (see `Perception and Actual Effect Gap Audit`) remains.
