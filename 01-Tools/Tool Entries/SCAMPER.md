# SCAMPER

**One-line summary:** A creative-thinking checklist that prompts seven systematic transformations of an existing product, process, or idea — Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other uses, Eliminate, Reverse — generating large variant lists from which promising candidates emerge.

**When to reach for it:** Product redesign and brand-extension brainstorming; service-improvement workshops; idea-generation when conventional brainstorming has stalled; teaching creative-thinking technique; quick-and-light innovation workshops where TRIZ is overkill; and any context where systematic prompts produce more variants than free brainstorming.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**SCAMPER** is a checklist of seven prompts applied to an existing thing. The acronym:

1. **S — Substitute** — what can be substituted? Materials, components, processes, people, places, times.
2. **C — Combine** — what can be combined? With other products, ideas, processes, technologies?
3. **A — Adapt** — what can be adapted? From another context, industry, era?
4. **M — Modify (Magnify, Minimize)** — what can be made larger, smaller, stronger, lighter?
5. **P — Put to other uses** — what new uses are possible? Different markets, applications, contexts?
6. **E — Eliminate** — what can be removed? Parts, steps, features, costs?
7. **R — Reverse (Rearrange)** — what can be reversed or rearranged? Sequence, polarity, roles?

The non-obvious operational insight is that **systematic prompts produce more variants than free brainstorming.** Free brainstorming defaults to first-thought variants and exhausts quickly; SCAMPER's seven distinct prompts each open a different transformation space, producing 5-10 variants per prompt = 35-70 candidates total. Most are weak; some are good; rare ones are excellent.

A second insight: **SCAMPER is lighter than TRIZ but covers similar territory.** TRIZ's 40 principles include several SCAMPER moves (segmentation = eliminate parts; merging = combine; inversion = reverse). SCAMPER's seven prompts trade depth for accessibility — easier to teach, faster to apply, less rigorous about contradiction abstraction.

A third insight: **the framework works on processes and services, not just products.** Original applications were product-design; modern use applies SCAMPER to business processes (substitute steps, combine roles, eliminate approvals), services (adapt formats, reverse customer flow), even strategic plans (modify scope, put to other uses).

A fourth insight: **the discipline is generating quantity before evaluating quality.** SCAMPER produces many variants; the temptation is to filter as you go. Better practice: generate freely through all seven prompts; evaluate after.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "first-thought brainstorming" exhaustion.** Free idea generation runs out of variants quickly; SCAMPER's seven distinct lenses keep generating.
2. **The "stuck on what is" trap.** Mental fixation on the current form blocks alternatives; systematic transformation prompts break the fixation.
3. **The "no quantity, no quality" failure.** Creative work usually requires generating many candidates before finding excellent ones; SCAMPER produces quantity.

For product designers, brand strategists, process improvers, innovation facilitators, and anyone running creative-thinking sessions, SCAMPER is foundational toolkit.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the subject — product, process, idea, service.                         |
|    2 | Run through each SCAMPER prompt. Generate 5-10 variants per prompt without      |
|      | filtering.                                                                        |
|    3 | Don't evaluate during generation; quantity first.                                |
|    4 | Total list: typically 35-70 variants across all prompts.                        |
|    5 | Evaluate after generation. Filter for feasibility and impact.                   |
|    6 | Cluster related variants. Sometimes patterns emerge.                            |
|    7 | Select top 3-5 candidates for prototyping.                                       |
|    8 | Iterate; SCAMPER can be re-applied to the candidates themselves.               |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE SEVEN PROMPTS

   S - SUBSTITUTE
       What can be replaced?
       Materials, components, processes, locations,
       roles, sources.
       Example: traditional shoe + leather → vegan
       leather → plant-based fiber.

   C - COMBINE
       What can be merged?
       Two products, two ideas, two markets, two
       technologies.
       Example: phone + camera → smartphone with
       camera.

   A - ADAPT
       What can be borrowed from elsewhere?
       Other industries, other eras, other contexts,
       other species.
       Example: Velcro adapted from burdock burrs;
       trade-show booth design adapted from theater
       sets.

   M - MODIFY (Magnify, Minimize)
       What can be enlarged, reduced, changed in
       proportion?
       Larger, smaller, stronger, lighter, faster,
       slower.
       Example: minimize a desktop into a laptop;
       magnify a phone into a tablet.

   P - PUT TO OTHER USES
       What new applications?
       Different markets, different segments, different
       use cases, different price points.
       Example: WD-40 for waterproofing; bicarbonate
       of soda for cleaning.

   E - ELIMINATE
       What can be removed?
       Parts, features, steps, costs, packaging.
       Example: minimalist watch eliminates numbers,
       hands; stays time-telling.

   R - REVERSE / REARRANGE
       What can be inverted, sequenced differently?
       Roles, polarity, order, perspective.
       Example: customer-as-supplier (UGC platforms);
       reverse customer journey design.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — COFFEE SHOP REDESIGN

   Subject: traditional coffee shop service.

   SUBSTITUTE:
       - Substitute beans with alternative crops
         (chicory, mushroom)
       - Substitute milk with plant-based options
       - Substitute baristas with self-service kiosks
       - Substitute physical store with delivery

   COMBINE:
       - Coffee + co-working space
       - Coffee + book shop
       - Coffee + breakfast / meal subscription

   ADAPT:
       - Adapt subscription model from streaming
         services
       - Adapt loyalty program from airlines
       - Adapt drive-through from fast food

   MODIFY:
       - Magnify: roastery + multiple cafes
       - Minimize: kiosk-only format
       - Modify: pop-up locations

   PUT TO OTHER USES:
       - Coffee shop as event venue
       - Coffee shop as photography studio
       - Coffee shop as community gathering space

   ELIMINATE:
       - Eliminate seating (grab-and-go only)
       - Eliminate menu (single signature drink)
       - Eliminate cash (mobile payment only)

   REVERSE:
       - Customers serve themselves (reverse server
         role)
       - Reverse hours (overnight cafe)
       - Reverse direction: bring shop to customers
         (food truck)

   Total: ~25 candidates. Some weak, some strong.
   Evaluate; prototype best.

THE DISCIPLINE OF QUANTITY-FIRST

   Generation rules:
       Don't filter during generation; output everything
       Don't worry about feasibility yet
       Combine prompts (e.g., substitute + eliminate)
       for further variants
       Build on others' ideas in group settings

   Evaluation rules (after generation):
       Feasibility: can this actually be done?
       Impact: would this matter if done?
       Differentiation: does it create distinctive
       value?
       Cost: implementation effort?

   The two phases stay separate. Filtering during
   generation kills variants prematurely.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. SKIPPING PROMPTS
        Treating SCAMPER as menu; using only some.
        Recovery: forced run through all seven.

   2. PREMATURE FILTERING
        Evaluating during generation. Recovery:
        explicit two-phase: generate then evaluate.

   3. SHALLOW APPLICATION
        One variant per prompt; not pushing for many.
        Recovery: 5-10 per prompt minimum.

   4. NO PROMPT INTERACTION
        Each prompt applied separately; never combined.
        Recovery: try combinations (substitute +
        eliminate; combine + reverse).

   5. SUBJECT-MISMATCH
        SCAMPER applied to a problem too abstract or
        too narrow. Recovery: scope subject
        appropriately.

   6. NO PROTOTYPE
        Lists generated; nothing tested. Recovery:
        select top candidates; prototype.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Generate then evaluate. The two-phase discipline produces quantity that becomes the basis for quality. Filtering during generation kills weak ideas that might combine into strong ones. (2) Run all seven prompts. Each prompt opens a different transformation space; skipping prompts misses variants in those spaces. (3) Push for 5-10 per prompt. First-thoughts exhaust quickly; depth comes from continuing past the obvious. (4) Combine prompts for further variants. Substitute + eliminate, combine + reverse, etc. The Cartesian product of prompts opens additional generative space.
