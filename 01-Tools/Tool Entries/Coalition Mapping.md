# Coalition Mapping

**One-line summary:** List who must back the decision, mark each as Champion / Supporter / Neutral / Skeptic / Opponent, identify what would move each non-supporter, and sequence the conversations to avoid early no's.

**When to reach for it:** A decision needs more than one person's approval and the support isn't yet locked in. Board votes, executive committee decisions, cross-functional initiatives, major procurement, organizational redesigns, M&A approvals.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Decisions in organizations are rarely binary moments. They are the visible surface of a longer political process in which a coalition is assembled, doubts are addressed, and opponents are either neutralized or routed around. Coalition Mapping is the operational version of that process — explicit enough to manage, granular enough to act on.

The tool integrates three things into a single plan:

1. **The required coalition** — who must back this for it to pass, given the formal and informal voting/veto structure.
2. **Current state per stakeholder** — Champion (will advocate), Supporter (will vote yes), Neutral (no opinion), Skeptic (leaning no but persuadable), Opponent (will vote no, may actively block).
3. **Movement plan** — for each non-supporter, what specific concern, interest, or exchange would move them, and the order in which to engage.

The order matters more than people expect. Early conversations create reference points: a high-status early "no" gives others permission to say no without much thought; a high-status early "yes" makes later refusal more costly. The map exposes this and lets you sequence deliberately.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Most failed initiatives aren't defeated; they're starved. They run out of political oxygen because the decision-maker count was wrong (more vetoes than expected), the movement plan was vague ("we'll talk to them"), or the sequence created an avoidable early no.

Coalition Mapping prevents the four most common failures:

1. **Underestimating the required coalition** — assuming "the CEO can just decide" when there are actually four veto points.
2. **Confusing politeness with support** — a Neutral who said "interesting" is not a Supporter.
3. **Pitching to everyone the same way** — the Skeptic CFO needs different evidence than the Skeptic head of engineering.
4. **Talking to the easy yeses first** — collecting endorsements that would have come anyway, while the actual swing votes harden.

For consulting and change work, this tool is what lets you tell a sponsor "we're at 7 of 11 votes, and here are the four conversations that close it" instead of "we have momentum, I think it'll pass." The first form lets the sponsor allocate their political capital; the second form is just hope.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Define the decision precisely. What is being approved, by whom, by when?        |
|    2 | List the required coalition — every person whose support, abstention, or        |
|      | non-opposition is necessary. Include formal voters AND informal vetoes.         |
|    3 | Mark current position for each: Champion / Supporter / Neutral / Skeptic /      |
|      | Opponent. Be honest. If you don't know, mark Unknown and plan a read-out.       |
|    4 | For each non-Supporter, identify movement levers: what specific concern,        |
|      | interest, identity, or exchange would shift them by one position?               |
|    5 | Identify each person's primary influencer (use the Influence Sociogram).        |
|      | Sometimes the path to A is through B, not A.                                    |
|    6 | Plan the sequence. Convert easy Skeptics to Neutrals first to build a "this     |
|      | is moving" narrative. Save the hardest conversations for after you have momentum|
|    7 | Re-map weekly while the campaign is active. Positions move; assumptions decay.  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

> **Sequencing principle (the core operational insight):** A high-status early "no" is far costlier than a low-status late "no." Early no's become reference points others coordinate around; late no's are isolated dissents. So the engagement sequence usually goes: easy upgrades (Skeptic → Neutral) first to generate momentum, then peer-pressure the harder Skeptics, and only then approach Opponents — by which point either their no doesn't matter, or you've gathered enough support to make their no expensive for them.

> **Champions vs. Supporters:** The distinction matters. A Champion will make calls on your behalf; a Supporter will only confirm if asked. Most coalition campaigns lose because they had Supporters where they needed Champions. Specifically ask each Supporter: "Would you raise this with X?" — if the answer is yes, they're a Champion. If it's "let me think about it," they're a Supporter, and you still need a Champion for that branch of the network.

> **The honesty discipline:** This artifact only works if you mark positions truthfully — including ones you'd prefer were different. The temptation to mark a wishful Neutral as a Supporter is the same temptation that makes campaigns fail. If you can't be honest on paper with yourself, you can't be strategic.
