# Cognitive Bias Checklists

**One-line summary:** A debiasing tool — formalized by Kahneman, Lovallo & Sibony — that applies an explicit checklist of common cognitive biases to a decision in progress, prompting decision-makers to ask whether each bias is operating and to surface its effects before committing.

**When to reach for it:** Major strategic decisions; investment / acquisition decisions; hiring and promotion choices; long-horizon plans (where forecasting biases matter); decisions made under emotional or political pressure; and any context where decision-makers are aware of bias risk and willing to slow down to check.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Cognitive bias checklists** make implicit bias-checking explicit. The structure:

1. **Recognize that decisions are subject to systematic biases.** Affect, anchoring, overconfidence, planning fallacy, groupthink, and many others.
2. **Apply a checklist** to the decision-in-progress, asking each question: is this bias operating here?
3. **Identify which biases likely apply.** Not all biases apply to all decisions; pick the relevant ones.
4. **Adjust the decision or process** to mitigate the identified biases.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **bias awareness alone doesn't prevent bias.** Decades of behavioral-economics research show that even people who know about biases continue to exhibit them — including the researchers themselves. Checklists work where awareness alone fails because they make the check explicit and routine, like a pilot's pre-flight checklist.

The Kahneman-Lovallo-Sibony 12-question checklist (published in HBR 2011, "Before You Make That Big Decision"):

1. Self-interest distortion (proposers vs. reviewers)
2. Affect heuristic (emotional reaction overriding analysis)
3. Groupthink (dissent suppressed)
4. Salience bias (vivid analogies overweighted)
5. Confirmation bias (insufficient consideration of disconfirming evidence)
6. Availability bias (recent / memorable events overweighted)
7. Anchoring (initial number distorting subsequent ones)
8. Halo effect (one good attribute generalized)
9. Sunk cost / endowment effect
10. Overconfidence / planning fallacy
11. Disaster neglect (low-probability tail risks ignored)
12. Loss aversion / risk aversion mismatch

A second insight: **the checklist works best as collaborative review, not self-application.** Self-applying biases is hard because the same biases distort the self-application. Two teams (proposers + reviewers) using the checklist on each other's work produces stronger debiasing than either team self-applying.

A third insight: **the checklist is a starting point, not a complete solution.** Decisions still require judgment after the checklist; the checklist surfaces issues, doesn't resolve them. Use as one debiasing layer among several (pre-mortem, red teaming, base-rate reasoning).

A fourth insight: **bias-awareness fatigue is a real risk.** Applying a 12-item checklist to every decision is impractical. Reserve for decisions where stakes justify the time, or develop shorter context-specific checklists for routine decisions.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "we're rational here" trap.** Belief that your team is exempt from biases. Empirically, no team is.
2. **The "implicit awareness" failure. **Knowing about biases doesn't prevent them; explicit checking does.
3. **The post-hoc rationalization risk.** Without pre-decision bias check, biases shape the decision and then rationalization defends it. Pre-decision check intervenes earlier.

For leaders, decision-makers, investment committees, hiring committees, and any team making high-stakes decisions vulnerable to systematic bias, checklists are practical debiasing.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the decision. Articulate it specifically.                              |
|    2 | Choose the checklist. Kahneman-Lovallo-Sibony 12-question, or domain-specific  |
|      | (investment checklist, hiring checklist).                                        |
|    3 | Walk through each question. For each: does this bias likely apply here?         |
|    4 | Identify the high-likelihood biases. Often 3-5 of the 12 apply meaningfully    |
|      | to a given decision.                                                              |
|    5 | Adjust the decision or process. Specific mitigations: outside view, pre-mortem,|
|      | external review, structured comparison, base-rate analysis.                     |
|    6 | Decide with awareness of residual bias. Some bias remains; explicit            |
|      | acknowledgment is better than denial.                                            |
|    7 | Document for institutional learning. Which biases recur in your decision      |
|      | environment?                                                                      |
|    8 | Update the checklist. Domain-specific patterns may emerge that justify         |
|      | customization.                                                                    |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE 12-QUESTION CHECKLIST (Kahneman-Lovallo-Sibony)

   Apply each to the decision under review:

   1. SELF-INTEREST
      "Is there reason to suspect motivated errors or
      errors driven by the self-interest of the
      recommending team?"

   2. AFFECT
      "Have the people making the recommendation
      fallen in love with it?"

   3. GROUPTHINK
      "Were there dissenting opinions within the
      recommending team? Were they explored?"

   4. SALIENCE
      "Is the diagnosis overly influenced by an
      analogy to a memorable success?"

   5. CONFIRMATION
      "Were credible alternatives considered?"

   6. AVAILABILITY
      "If we had to make this decision again in a
      year, what information would we want? Can we
      get more of it now?"

   7. ANCHORING
      "Do we know where the numbers came from? Could
      they be unsubstantiated extrapolations or
      anchors?"

   8. HALO
      "Is the team assuming that a person, organization,
      or approach that is successful in one area will
      be just as successful in another?"

   9. SUNK COST / ENDOWMENT
      "Are the recommenders overly attached to a past
      decision?"

   10. OVERCONFIDENCE
      "Is the base case overly optimistic?"

   11. DISASTER NEGLECT
      "Is the worst case bad enough?"

   12. LOSS AVERSION
      "Is the team overly cautious because of past
      losses?"

   Each question is yes/no/maybe. The maybes and yeses
   are where mitigation effort goes.

THE COMMON BIAS-MITIGATIONS

   For each identified bias:

   SELF-INTEREST:
       Independent review; structurally separate
       proposer from approver

   AFFECT:
       Pre-mortem; mandatory consideration of
       alternatives; cool-off period

   GROUPTHINK:
       Devil's advocate; private writing before
       discussion; junior voices first

   SALIENCE:
       Reference-class forecasting; outside view;
       quantitative base rates

   CONFIRMATION:
       Steel-man alternatives; deliberate seeking of
       disconfirming evidence

   AVAILABILITY:
       Use base rates instead of recent examples;
       consult written records, not memory

   ANCHORING:
       Multiple independent estimates; check anchor
       sources

   HALO:
       Decompose into specific claims; evaluate each
       independently

   SUNK COST:
       Frame as new decision: "If we hadn't started,
       would we start now?"

   OVERCONFIDENCE:
       Wide confidence intervals; distribution of
       outcomes; planning-fallacy correction
       (multiply estimates by 1.5-2)

   DISASTER NEGLECT:
       Stress-test worst case; pre-mortem; tail-risk
       hedging

   LOSS AVERSION:
       Consider expected value, not just downside;
       balanced framing

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — ACQUISITION DECISION

   Decision: acquire competitor for $200M.

   Checklist application:

   Self-interest: ✓ The team championing the deal
   gets bigger scope; risk of motivated errors.
   Mitigation: independent diligence team.

   Affect: ✓ Multiple participants emotionally
   committed (CEO has been pushing for this).
   Mitigation: pre-mortem; consider null option
   explicitly.

   Confirmation: ✓ Diligence team reports favorable
   data; little disconfirming. Mitigation: red team
   explicitly attacks deal thesis.

   Anchoring: ✓ $200M was originally floated by the
   target's CEO; subsequent discussion anchored on
   that. Mitigation: independent valuation.

   Overconfidence: ✓ Synergy estimates are at the
   high end of comparable deals. Mitigation: apply
   base-rate haircut (typical synergy realization is
   60-70% of plan).

   Sunk cost: ✗ No prior commitment to overcome.

   Other biases: less applicable.

   Adjustments to process:
       - Independent valuation
       - Red team attack on thesis
       - Conservative synergy assumption (50% of plan)
       - Pre-mortem on integration
       - Cool-off: revisit decision in 2 weeks

   The decision proceeds, but the bias-aware version
   has structurally different inputs than the
   bias-blind version would have.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. CHECKLIST AS CEREMONY
        Run through the questions; check boxes; nothing
        changes. Recovery: each yes/maybe gets specific
        mitigation.

   2. SELF-APPLICATION ONLY
        Same team that's biased applies the checklist
        and concludes "no bias here." Recovery:
        cross-team or independent application.

   3. CHECKLIST FATIGUE
        Applied to every small decision; team starts
        skipping. Recovery: reserve for high-stakes
        decisions; lighter version for routine.

   4. ASSUMING COMPREHENSIVENESS
        12 biases ≠ all biases. Recovery: domain-
        specific additions; awareness that the
        checklist is partial.

   5. POST-HOC APPLICATION
        Checklist after decision is made; serves as
        rationalization. Recovery: pre-decision
        application is essential.

   6. KNOW-IT-ALL FATIGUE
        Senior decision-makers feel the checklist is
        below their level. Recovery: peer-applied
        checklist (each team applies to other team's
        recommendations) levels the playing field.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Decision under review: ____________________________

   Checklist application:
       Q1 Self-interest: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: __
       Q2 Affect: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: ________
       Q3 Groupthink: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: ____
       Q4 Salience: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: ______
       Q5 Confirmation: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: __
       Q6 Availability: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: __
       Q7 Anchoring: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: _____
       Q8 Halo: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: __________
       Q9 Sunk cost: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: _____
       Q10 Overconfidence: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation:_
       Q11 Disaster neglect: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation:_
       Q12 Loss aversion: Y / N / Maybe — Mitigation: __

   High-likelihood biases (3-5): _____________________

   Process adjustments:
       _________________________________________________

   Decision proceeds with explicit acknowledgment of
   residual bias: ___________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Apply pre-decision, not post. The checklist's value is in shaping the decision, not in rationalizing it after. Run it before commitment. (2) Cross-team application beats self-application. Same biases that distort the decision distort the self-applied checklist. Have a different team review with the checklist. (3) Reserve for high-stakes decisions. Twelve-question checklist for every decision is impractical and produces fatigue. Use the full version where stakes justify; develop lighter context-specific versions for routine decisions. (4) The checklist surfaces; mitigation acts. Identifying biases isn't enough; each yes/maybe needs a specific mitigation (independent review, pre-mortem, base-rate adjustment, etc.). Without follow-through, the exercise is ceremony.
