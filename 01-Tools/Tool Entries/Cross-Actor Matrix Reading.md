# Cross-Actor Matrix Reading

**One-line summary:** A perspective-shift lens that runs the ADAPT funnel on another actor — asking what their agenda is, what their resource state is, and where their observation node is pointed — so that what looked from the single-actor view like "uncontrollable reality" can be recognized as the precise output of another actor's matrix.

**When to reach for it:** When an outcome has surprised the actor and their first attribution is to randomness, luck, or "the situation"; when a counterparty is behaving in a way that the actor cannot make sense of within the actor's own frame; when a competitive situation requires anticipating how another actor will respond to a move; when reviewing past cycles where the actor's perception gap was unusually large (often a sign that the actual effect was produced by another actor's overlay, not by the actor's own move); when modeling timing — running this on the other actor reveals when their observation node will be available to register the actor's move.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Cross-Actor Matrix Reading** is the perspective-shift lens that recovers the most important strategic insight in iterated political contexts: other actors are running their own ADAPT cycles, and what appears to a single-actor view as randomness or "the situation" is usually another actor's matrix output. The dissertation captures this in the Charles case Coda (Lam 2020 p. 109): the CFO independently operated his own political logic overlaid on Charles' execution; the surprising 4-week-vs-12-month timeline gap that Charles experienced was not random — it was the CFO's matrix's precise output.

The field manual operationalizes the lens into three diagnostic questions (Field Manual p. 39):

> "What is this actor's agenda? Not their stated objective; their long-running, rarely-articulated ambition. Watch what they protect when under pressure; that is the agenda.
>
> What is the actor's resource state right now? Is their capability gate likely to return Yes or No on the move you are watching them consider? An actor with depleted resources will rescope; an actor with abundant resources can afford the frontal move.
>
> Where is the actor's observation node pointed? An actor whose attention is occupied elsewhere — by a crisis, a major project, a personal matter — is running a degraded cycle. They will be slow to update interests and slow to respond to your moves. This is one of the most under-exploited insights in competitive situations: timing your move to your opponent's observation state, rather than to your own readiness, is often what makes a move land."

The non-obvious operational property: **the three diagnostic questions correspond to the three loci where ADAPT cycles consume political capital.** Agenda is the strategic invariant; resource state determines capability-gate verdicts; observation node determines whether the cycle's learning loop is running cleanly. Mapping another actor's position across these three is enough to predict their next move with substantially more accuracy than reading their stated public position.

A second insight: **the lens transforms attribution.** What an actor would attribute to "bad luck" or "an unreasonable response" is usually the other actor's matrix output. The Cresterly Lessons section (Field Manual p. 25) summarizes this:

> "What looked, from a single-actor perspective, like uncontrollable reality (joint governance, the leash, the review), was, in the two-actor view, the precise output of Vale's matrix. There is rarely as much noise as it feels like there is."

A third insight: **the lens applies to allies, not just adversaries.** Running the cross-actor reading on supportive parties — sponsors, allies, neutral observers — surfaces what they are deciding underneath their cooperation, when their resource state might shift, and what would cause them to update.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Attribution to randomness.** Treating another actor's matrix output as "luck" or "the situation" cuts off the strategic information that the gap between intended and actual outcomes contains.
2. **Stated-position naïveté.** Reading other actors at the level of their stated public objective rather than their underlying agenda produces predictions that miss the political reality.
3. **Single-actor timing.** Timing a move to the actor's own readiness without considering the target's or third parties' observation states means a move can land into an inattentive room — or, worse, into an attentive third party's reactive cycle.

For executives, strategists, founders, and any actor operating in conditions where political outcomes are produced by multiple overlapping decision processes, the lens is what recovers the visibility into other actors' contributions.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

The lens is a structured three-question pass per other actor, typically done in 15-30 minutes per actor. The discipline is to run the pass before forming a strategic theory of the actor's likely behavior, not after.

1. **Select the actor.** A specific person or named coalition. "The market" or "the competition" is not an actor; run the lens on a specific player within that surround.
2. **Question 1: What is their agenda?** Their long-running, rarely-articulated ambition — not their stated objective. Indicators: what do they protect when under pressure? What do they consistently allocate scarce time toward? Whose interests do they consistently advance? Run `Actor-Agenda Decomposition` on them.
3. **Question 2: What is their resource state?** Use the 9-resource taxonomy (see `9-Resource Portfolio Diagnostic`). Specifically: where is their capability gate likely to be passable, and where is it likely to be returning No? Recent depletions are especially informative — an actor who just spent significant political capital is operating with a constrained gate for the next several cycles.
4. **Question 3: Where is their observation node pointed?** What is currently consuming their attention? Crises, major projects, personal matters, ongoing investigations — anything that occupies attention degrades the cycle and slows interest-updating. An actor with a starved observation node is slow to register and slow to respond.
5. **Assemble the cross-actor model.** Combine the three answers into a one-paragraph theory of what the actor's likely next move is, what would trigger it, and what would delay it.
6. **Test against observable evidence.** Recent behavior should be consistent with the model. If it is not, one of the three answers is wrong; revise.
7. **Use the model to time and shape the actor's own move.** A move into another actor's starved-observation window lands differently than the same move into their attentive window. The lens's strategic value is in the timing implication.

A useful test: if the actor's theory of the other party reduces to "they will do whatever serves their stated objective," the lens has not been run. The lens's value is in the gap between stated objective and underlying agenda, and in the resource and observation modulations that the gap is filtered through.

## Worked Example

A regional VP at Cresterly Industrial has run Cycle 1 of an analytics-coverage initiative. The CEO approved her pilot — but with a steering committee chaired by the CTO. From the VP's single-actor view, this looked like the CEO hedging her bets. Running the cross-actor reading on the CTO:

- **Agenda:** consolidate technology authority across the firm; specifically, retain unified governance of data infrastructure regardless of business-unit pressure. Visible in the CTO's consistent defense of platform integrity when challenged.
- **Resource state:** depleted. He has just initiated a major cloud-infrastructure migration that is consuming most of his attention bandwidth and significant political capital. His capability gate is currently returning No on most contested moves.
- **Observation node:** pointed at the migration. Specifically, he is no longer watching the analytics-pilot board on which the VP is moving. His Cycle 1 observation was sharp; his Cycle 2 observation is starved.

The cross-actor model: the CTO is in a constrained cycle. His preferred position (block the analytics-group split) is unaffordable; his selected option will be an adverse-alternative move (concede the applied layer; entrench on the platform; extract a procedural review). The joint-governance outcome that surprised the VP was not the CEO's hedging — it was the precise output of the CTO's matrix in Cycle 1 (Field Manual ch. 7, pp. 23-25). The VP's strategic implication: Cycle 3 becomes available not when her resources rebuild, but when his observation node remains pointed at the migration. Time the next move to his observation state. (Adapted from Field Manual ch. 7, Cresterly Cycles 2-3, pp. 23-25; underlying dissertation pattern: Charles case Coda, p. 109.)

## Common Failure Modes

- **Reading the actor's stated objective.** The stated objective is the surface; the agenda is the strategic invariant. Reading only the stated objective produces predictions that miss what the actor will actually defend under pressure.
- **Skipping the resource-state question.** Without the resource-state read, the lens cannot distinguish an actor who will pursue their preferred position from an actor who will be forced into adverse-alternative moves by gate failures.
- **Static observation-node assessment.** The observation node moves over time — a major project ends, a crisis resolves, attention re-aligns. A model that does not anticipate observation-node shifts will mis-time the actor's own next move.
- **Reading only adversaries.** Allies, sponsors, and neutral observers also have agendas, resource states, and observation pointers. Their cycles affect the actor's outcomes; running the lens only on adversaries leaves valuable signal unread.
- **Cross-actor reading as gossip.** The lens is strategic, not psychological. The discipline is to read the actor's agenda from what they protect, not from what they reveal in their personality.
- **Single-pass reading.** The lens needs to be re-run when conditions shift. An assessment locked at one moment misses the actor's own cycle-updating.
