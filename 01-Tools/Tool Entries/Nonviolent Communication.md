# Nonviolent Communication / NVC

**One-line summary:** Marshall Rosenberg's communication framework — Observation + Feeling + Need + Request — that distinguishes evaluations from observations, feelings from thoughts, universal human needs from strategies, and specific requests from demands, producing dialogue that doesn't trigger defensiveness.

**When to reach for it:** Conflict resolution; mediation; parenting; therapy; partnerships under stress; communications across difference (cultural, ideological, personal); contexts where conventional speaking patterns trigger defensiveness; and any situation where being heard requires a structurally different way of speaking.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Nonviolent Communication (NVC)** structures dialogue around four elements:

1. **Observation** — what specifically happened, without evaluation. "When you arrived 30 minutes after the agreed time" — not "When you were late."
2. **Feeling** — what you feel in response. "I feel frustrated" — not "I feel that you don't respect me" (which is a thought disguised as a feeling).
3. **Need** — the universal human need underneath the feeling. "Because I need consideration / partnership / clarity."
4. **Request** — a specific, doable, present-tense request. "Would you be willing to text me if you'll be more than 10 minutes late?" — not "Would you be more considerate?"

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most communication failures stem from confusing categories.** Evaluations sound like observations ("You were late" sounds factual but is evaluative). Thoughts sound like feelings ("I feel attacked"). Strategies sound like needs ("I need you to apologize" — apology is a strategy; the underlying need might be respect or being heard). Demands sound like requests. NVC's discipline is keeping each element pure.

A second insight: **needs are universal; strategies are specific.** Two people in conflict often have compatible needs (both want respect, autonomy, consideration) but incompatible strategies (one wants apology, the other wants problem-solving). Identifying needs underneath strategies often reveals workable resolutions.

A third insight: **the framework is structurally different from typical speech.** Most dialogue uses evaluation, judgment, demand. NVC's pattern is unfamiliar; speaking it feels stilted initially. With practice, it becomes natural and produces dramatically different conversational outcomes.

A fourth insight: **the framework works in both expression and listening.** Speaking NVC: state your observation, feeling, need, request. Listening NVC: hear past the other's evaluations to their feelings and needs. Both halves are needed.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "evaluation triggers defense" trap.** Statements that mix observation with evaluation produce defensiveness; pure observation invites engagement.
2. **The "thoughts-as-feelings" muddle.** "I feel attacked" is a thought; "I feel angry" is a feeling. Conflating them produces argument over interpretations.
3. **The "demand-disguised-as-request" failure.** Requests with implicit threat aren't requests; they fail to produce genuine cooperation.

For mediators, parents, therapists, partners, and anyone in dialogue across difference or under stress, NVC is foundational structural discipline.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the situation requiring difficult communication.                       |
|    2 | Articulate the OBSERVATION — what specifically happened, without evaluation.    |
|    3 | Identify your FEELING — emotion, not thought. Use feeling vocabulary.          |
|    4 | Identify the NEED underneath the feeling. Universal human needs (respect,      |
|      | autonomy, consideration, etc.).                                                  |
|    5 | Formulate a REQUEST — specific, doable, present-tense, accepts no as answer.   |
|    6 | Speak: "When I observe X, I feel Y, because I need Z; would you be willing    |
|      | to A?"                                                                           |
|    7 | Listen NVC-style: hear past their evaluations to their feelings and needs.    |
|    8 | Practice. The framework feels stilted initially; gets natural with practice.  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FOUR ELEMENTS

   1. OBSERVATION (without evaluation):
      What's observable; verifiable.
      Bad: "You're always late."
      Good: "You arrived at 6:30 instead of 6:00 the
      last three times we've met."

   2. FEELING (not thought):
      Pure emotion.
      Feeling vocabulary: angry, sad, frustrated,
      hurt, anxious, joyful, grateful, lonely.
      Not feelings: "I feel that you don't care"
      (thought); "I feel attacked" (interpretation);
      "I feel like leaving" (impulse).

   3. NEED (universal, not strategy):
      Universal human needs:
          Connection, belonging, love
          Respect, dignity, recognition
          Autonomy, choice, self-determination
          Safety, security, stability
          Growth, learning, competence
          Contribution, meaning, purpose
          Joy, play, beauty
          Honesty, integrity, transparency
      
      Not needs (these are strategies):
          "I need you to apologize."
          "I need you to be on time."
          "I need to leave."
      
      Underneath strategies are needs (respect; trust;
      space).

   4. REQUEST (specific, doable, present):
      "Would you be willing to ____?"
      Specific: "text me if running late" — not "be
      more considerate."
      Doable: actionable now.
      Present: about future behavior, not past.
      Accepts "no": if the other can't say no, it's
      a demand, not a request.

THE FOUR-STEP STATEMENT

   "When I [observe / hear / see] [observation],
   I feel [feeling],
   because I need [need].
   Would you be willing to [request]?"

   Example:
   "When you arrived 30 minutes after our agreed time
   without texting, I felt anxious, because I need
   consideration and clarity in our planning. Would
   you be willing to text me if you're going to be
   more than 10 minutes late?"

   Compare to:
   "You're always late and inconsiderate. You don't
   respect my time."

   Same situation; very different conversational
   outcome.

THE LISTENING-NVC TECHNIQUE

   When listening:

   The other person's words may be evaluative,
   judgmental, demanding. Hear past them:

   "You're so selfish."
   → They're feeling _____ because they need _____.

   Possible interpretation: feeling lonely, needing
   connection.

   Reflect: "It sounds like you're feeling lonely and
   needing more connection? Is that right?"

   This deflects the evaluation (which would
   trigger defense) and addresses the underlying
   feelings and needs (which is what the speaker is
   actually expressing, even if poorly).

   Powerful in conflict and mediation.

THE STRATEGY-VS-NEED DISCIPLINE

   Most conflicts are between incompatible strategies.
   Underneath the strategies are usually compatible
   needs.

   Example:
   Manager: "I need you to come into the office 5
   days/week." (strategy)
   Employee: "I need to work from home." (strategy)
   
   Underneath:
   Manager: collaboration, visibility, team cohesion
   (needs)
   Employee: focus, work-life balance, autonomy
   (needs)
   
   Compatible needs; incompatible strategies. Other
   strategies might satisfy both:
       Hybrid (3 in, 2 home)
       Defined collaboration days + flex
       Cameras-on for synchronous work
       Async collaboration norms
   
   Without need identification, the strategies
   collide. With it, alternative strategies become
   findable.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. EVALUATIONS DISGUISED AS OBSERVATIONS
        "You were rude." Recovery: pure observation
        ("you said X").

   2. THOUGHTS DISGUISED AS FEELINGS
        "I feel that you don't respect me." Recovery:
        "I feel hurt; I'm thinking you don't respect
        me; I'm not sure if that's true."

   3. STRATEGIES DISGUISED AS NEEDS
        "I need you to apologize." Recovery: identify
        the underlying need (respect; acknowledgment).

   4. DEMANDS DISGUISED AS REQUESTS
        "Would you be willing to..." with implicit
        consequences. Recovery: genuinely accept "no";
        consequences become separate negotiation.

   5. STILTED MECHANICAL DELIVERY
        Following the formula without the spirit.
        Recovery: practice; integrate.

   6. NO LISTENING SIDE
        Speaking NVC; not hearing NVC. Recovery:
        practice both halves.

   7. JUDGMENTAL FRAMING OF NVC
        Treating the framework as morally superior;
        making others feel "wrong" for not using it.
        Recovery: NVC is a tool; not a moral demand
        on others.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Situation: __________________________________________

   Observation (without evaluation):
       _________________________________________________

   My feeling (pure emotion):
       _________________________________________________

   My need (universal, not strategy):
       _________________________________________________

   My request (specific, doable, present):
       Would you be willing to _____________________?

   Speech: "When I observe ___, I feel ___, because I
   need ___. Would you be willing to ___?"

   Listening:
       Other's words: ________________________________
       Underlying feeling (your guess): ______________
       Underlying need: ______________________________
       Reflective check: "Are you feeling ___ because
       you need ___?"

   Strategy alternatives that might serve both needs:
       _________________________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Keep the categories pure. Observations not mixed with evaluations; feelings not mixed with thoughts; needs not mixed with strategies; requests not disguising demands. The discipline of pure categories is the framework's mechanism. (2) Identify needs underneath strategies. Most conflicts are strategic; the underlying needs are usually compatible. Surfacing needs reveals alternative strategies. (3) Practice both halves. NVC is a way of speaking and a way of listening. Hearing past evaluations to underlying feelings and needs is as important as speaking that way yourself. (4) Adopt the spirit, not just the formula. Mechanical delivery feels stilted and produces resistance. The framework works when integrated as orientation, not just script.
