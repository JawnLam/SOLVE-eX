# Ericsson Deliberate Practice

**One-line summary:** K. Anders Ericsson's research-based framework for skill development: not hours alone but specific characteristics — clear goal at edge of ability, full focus, immediate feedback, repetition with refinement, coaching for established skills — distinguish ordinary practice (which plateaus) from deliberate practice (which continues to produce improvement).

**When to reach for it:** Personal skill development; professional learning; coaching others toward mastery; designing training programs; understanding why long-tenured practitioners aren't always experts; identifying why effort isn't producing improvement; and any context where skill plateau matters and the question is "why isn't more practice working?"

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Ericsson's deliberate practice** specifies the conditions for continued skill improvement. The five characteristics:

1. **Clear specific goal at the edge of current ability** — not too easy (no challenge), not too hard (no progress). Just beyond current performance.
2. **Full focused attention** — distracted practice doesn't count. Total engagement.
3. **Immediate feedback** — performance assessment delivered close in time, with signal about what to adjust.
4. **Repetition with refinement** — practice the specific skill many times, modifying based on feedback.
5. **Coaching for skills with established methods** — when prior masters have figured out how to do it, learn from them; coaching dramatically accelerates.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most "practice" isn't deliberate practice.** Going through the motions, repeating what you already know, working without feedback — this is ordinary practice and produces plateau, not progress. Deliberate practice is uncomfortable (you're at the edge of ability) and effortful (full focus); it's what produces continued improvement past the plateau.

Ericsson's distinction with Gladwell's "10,000 hours":

- **Gladwell:** 10,000 hours of practice → mastery
- **Ericsson:** 10,000 hours of *deliberate* practice → mastery (and the deliberate qualifier is essential)

Many practitioners with 10,000+ hours never reach mastery because their practice was ordinary, not deliberate.

A second insight: **the framework explains skill plateau.** Most people improve quickly at a new skill, then plateau. The plateau is reached when current ability is sufficient for typical performance demands; ordinary use no longer pushes them. Continued improvement requires deliberate stretching past the plateau — which is uncomfortable and feels unnecessary, so most people don't do it.

A third insight: **the framework prescribes coaching for established skills.** Tennis, music performance, chess, surgery — these have known methods developed by prior masters. Working alone, you might rediscover them slowly; a coach transmits them quickly. For genuinely novel domains (frontier research), coaching can't help in the same way; but for established skills, it's nearly always advisable.

A fourth insight: **the framework is uncomfortable.** Deliberate practice doesn't feel like you're "doing what you're good at"; it feels like working at what you're not yet good at. Most people prefer comfortable repetition; deliberate practice requires choosing discomfort.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "more hours" assumption.** Putting in time without specifying practice characteristics produces plateau, not improvement.
2. **The comfortable-repetition trap.** Practicing what you can already do feels productive; doesn't develop skill.
3. **The "self-taught alone" inefficiency.** When established methods exist, learning solo is often slower than coached learning.

For musicians, athletes, surgeons, software engineers, executives, and anyone trying to improve at a skill where ordinary practice has plateaued, deliberate practice is essential framework.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the skill to improve. Specific.                                          |
|    2 | Identify current performance level. What can you reliably do?                  |
|    3 | Define a goal just beyond — at the edge. Specific and observable.              |
|    4 | Design practice sessions: full focus; specific exercise targeting the goal.    |
|    5 | Build feedback loops: immediate (during session), reflective (after session).  |
|    6 | If skill has established methods, find a coach.                                  |
|    7 | Practice with refinement. Adjust based on feedback.                             |
|    8 | Periodically reset the goal as you improve. Edge of ability moves.             |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FIVE CHARACTERISTICS (CHECKLIST)

   For your practice session, verify:

   [ ] Clear specific goal? (not "practice the piece"
       but "play measure 12 with the trill at tempo")
   [ ] At edge of ability? (not too easy, not too hard;
       you should fail sometimes)
   [ ] Full focused attention? (no distractions; total
       presence)
   [ ] Immediate feedback? (you can tell whether you
       did it right)
   [ ] Repetition with refinement? (many attempts; each
       one informed by prior)
   [ ] Coaching available where applicable?

   If all five → deliberate practice.
   If some missing → ordinary practice; less
   improvement expected.

THE SKILL-PLATEAU EXPLANATION

   Initial period: rapid improvement.
   Adequate-for-use period: plateau.
   Past-plateau improvement: deliberate practice
   required.

   Most learners stop at "adequate for use" because:
       Daily use doesn't push them
       Ordinary repetition feels like practice
       Past-plateau practice is uncomfortable
       Past-plateau practice requires explicit goals
       beyond current ability

   The plateau is normal; passing it is exceptional
   and requires deliberate practice characteristics.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — TENNIS

   Skill: forehand topspin

   Ordinary practice:
       Hit forehands during practice match.
       Some go in, some don't.
       No specific focus on topspin technique.
       → Plateau at intermediate level.

   Deliberate practice:
       Goal: 80% of forehands cross the net with
       topspin (verified by ball machine output).
       Focus: only on grip + swing path producing
       topspin.
       Feedback: ball machine + coach + video review.
       Repetition: 100 forehands per session, with
       grip / swing path tweaked between sets.
       Coaching: weekly 1:1.

   The deliberate version is harder, takes more
   discipline, but produces measurable improvement
   the ordinary version doesn't.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — SOFTWARE ENGINEERING

   Skill: writing concise, clear technical documents

   Ordinary practice:
       Write documents during work.
       Send for review.
       Reviewers might note small issues.
       → Plateau at "adequate."

   Deliberate practice:
       Goal: write documents that lead to faster
       decisions (measured by time to review +
       approval).
       Focus: specific aspects (lead with conclusion;
       structured argument; concise prose).
       Feedback: review reading time + reviewer
       feedback; before/after comparison.
       Repetition: rewrite past documents using new
       structure; track time-to-decision delta.
       Coaching: senior writer mentor; feedback on
       drafts.

   Most engineers plateau at adequate; few do
   deliberate practice on writing skill.

THE COACHING ARGUMENT

   For skills with established methods:

   Coach knows what mastery looks like
   Coach knows the typical errors
   Coach knows the path
   Coach can identify your specific gaps

   Without coaching:
       You may practice the wrong thing
       You may build bad habits
       You may not see your own errors
       You may take longer to discover what coaches
       teach in weeks

   For genuinely novel domains, coaching may not
   exist; expert peers / honest feedback are next-best.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. ORDINARY PRACTICE MISLABELED
        Calling repetition "deliberate" when it's not.
        Recovery: apply checklist.

   2. NO FEEDBACK LOOP
        Practicing without knowing how it went.
        Recovery: build feedback (recording, coach,
        objective metric).

   3. COMFORT-ZONE GOALS
        "Goal" is what you can already do. Recovery:
        explicitly target edge.

   4. UNFOCUSED ATTENTION
        Practicing while distracted (phone, music,
        social context). Recovery: full focus blocks.

   5. NO COACH WHEN AVAILABLE
        Self-teaching when coaching exists. Recovery:
        invest in coaching for established skills.

   6. INSUFFICIENT REPETITION
        One attempt per session. Recovery: many
        attempts with refinement.

   7. NO GOAL ADVANCEMENT
        Goal stays static; you've improved past it.
        Recovery: reset to new edge.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Skill: _____________________________________________
   Current performance level: _________________________
   Goal (at edge): ____________________________________

   Practice session plan:
       Specific exercise: _____________________________
       Duration: ______________________________________
       Repetitions: ___________________________________
       Focus discipline: ______________________________

   Feedback loop:
       Immediate (during): ____________________________
       Reflective (after): ____________________________

   Coaching:
       Available? Y / N
       Source: _______________________________________

   Checklist:
       [ ] Clear specific goal at edge
       [ ] Full focused attention
       [ ] Immediate feedback
       [ ] Repetition with refinement
       [ ] Coaching for established skills

   Goal advancement:
       Hit goal → reset to new edge
       Schedule check: weekly / monthly
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Apply the five-characteristic checklist. Not all practice is deliberate; verify each session. Missing characteristics produce diminished improvement. (2) Practice at the edge. Comfort-zone repetition feels productive but plateaus. The edge of ability is uncomfortable; the discomfort is the indicator of growth. (3) Build feedback loops. Without immediate feedback, you can't refine; refinement is the engine of improvement. Recording, metrics, and coaching all serve. (4) Find coaches for established skills. Self-teaching from scratch is slower than learning from those who already know. Investment in coaching pays off many times over for skills with established mastery paths.
