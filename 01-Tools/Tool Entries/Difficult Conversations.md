# Difficult Conversations

**One-line summary:** Stone, Patton & Heen's framework — from Harvard Negotiation Project — that recognizes any difficult conversation contains three simultaneous conversations (What Happened, Feelings, Identity) and shifts the goal from delivering a message to mutual learning.

**When to reach for it:** Performance feedback; relationship discussions where emotions run deep; family conflicts; conversations where the surface topic masks deeper identity stakes; situations where you've avoided a conversation for a long time; and any context where "having it out" hasn't worked because the conversation isn't really about what it seems.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Difficult Conversations** identifies three simultaneous conversations:

1. **The What Happened conversation** — competing stories about what occurred, who's right, who caused what.
2. **The Feelings conversation** — what each party feels (often suppressed or unexpressed).
3. **The Identity conversation** — what this means about us (am I competent? am I a good person? am I lovable?).

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most conversation failures happen because parties only address the surface (What Happened) while the real difficulty is in the other two layers.** Two managers debating who's responsible for a missed deadline are also unconsciously navigating: how does this make them feel? what does this say about their competence?

The framework's prescription:

1. **Shift from "message delivery" to "learning conversation."** Don't go in to convince; go in to learn what they're seeing that you're not.
2. **Acknowledge all three layers.** Surface the feelings; address the identity stakes.
3. **Move from blame to contribution.** Instead of "who's at fault?" ask "how did we each contribute to this situation?"
4. **Move from positions to interests.** What does each party actually want underneath their stated position?

A second insight: **the framework's biggest move is the goal shift.** Conversations focused on "winning" (proving the other wrong) almost always fail. Conversations focused on "learning" (understanding both perspectives) usually succeed. The shift in goal changes everything else.

A third insight: **the identity conversation is usually the hardest layer.** Stakes there can be high — "if this is true about me, who am I?" Skilled difficult-conversation practitioners help themselves and the other navigate identity threats explicitly.

A fourth insight: **the framework complements Crucial Conversations.** Crucial Conversations gives the dialogue mechanics (STATE skills); Difficult Conversations gives the deeper psychological structure. Used together: structural understanding (DC) + practical skills (CC).

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "surface-only" trap.** Discussing what happened while ignoring feelings and identity; conversation feels stuck.
2. **The "win the argument" failure.** Goal of convincing produces argument, not understanding.
3. **The "blame-vs-contribution" misframe.** "Whose fault?" produces defensiveness; "what did each contribute?" produces insight.

For managers, partners, parents, friends, and anyone facing difficult conversations whose surface treatment hasn't worked, the three-conversations framework is foundational diagnostic.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the conversation as difficult. Strong feelings; conflicting views;     |
|      | avoidance.                                                                        |
|    2 | Map the three layers. What's the What Happened question? What feelings? What   |
|      | identity stakes (yours and theirs)?                                              |
|    3 | Shift goal from "deliver message" to "learn what they're seeing."              |
|    4 | Move from blame to contribution. How did each of us contribute?                |
|    5 | Surface feelings — yours and theirs — explicitly.                                |
|    6 | Acknowledge identity stakes — for both of you.                                   |
|    7 | Have the conversation: share your story; learn theirs; find shared              |
|      | understanding.                                                                    |
|    8 | Move to action: what do we agree on; what's next.                                |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE THREE CONVERSATIONS

   1. THE WHAT HAPPENED CONVERSATION:
      Competing facts and stories about what occurred.
      "He didn't return my email."
      "She sent it to the wrong address."
      "We agreed on Tuesday."
      "We didn't agree."

   2. THE FEELINGS CONVERSATION:
      What each party feels.
      "I felt dismissed."
      "I felt overwhelmed."
      "I'm angry but pretending I'm not."

      Often suppressed: "Emotions don't belong here."
      Reality: they're operating regardless.

   3. THE IDENTITY CONVERSATION:
      What this means about us.
      "Am I competent?"
      "Am I a good colleague?"
      "Am I respected?"
      "Am I respected as a parent?"

      Identity stakes are often the deepest layer;
      they make the conversation feel "loaded."

   All three are happening simultaneously. Failed
   conversations usually only address #1.

THE LEARNING-CONVERSATION SHIFT

   STANDARD APPROACH (message delivery):
       Goal: convince other of my view
       Tactics: marshal evidence, prepare counter-
       arguments, prove rightness
       Result: argument, defensiveness, gridlock

   LEARNING-CONVERSATION APPROACH:
       Goal: understand what each of us is seeing
       Tactics: share my view AND ask about theirs;
       genuinely curious
       Result: shared understanding, possibility of
       resolution

   The shift is hard. Internal pressure to convince is
   strong. The discipline: notice the impulse to
   "make them see"; redirect to "understand what they
   see."

THE BLAME-TO-CONTRIBUTION MOVE

   BLAME:
       "Whose fault is this?"
       Produces defensiveness; everyone protects.
       Future-oriented? No — past-oriented.

   CONTRIBUTION:
       "How did each of us contribute to this
       situation?"
       Produces insight; both parties can examine.
       Future-oriented: contribution illuminates how
       to prevent recurrence.

   Notice: "contribution" doesn't dilute the larger
   contributions of one party. If one is 80%
   responsible, the contribution analysis says so.
   But it also captures the 20% the other contributed,
   which is where their leverage lies for prevention.

THE FEELINGS-SURFACING DISCIPLINE

   Skill: name feelings explicitly.

   "I feel hurt that you didn't include me in that
   meeting."
   "I'm uncertain whether to bring this up; afraid
   you'll dismiss it."
   "I feel respected when you check in like this."

   For yourself: own the feeling.
   For other: ask about feelings.
   "I imagine you might be feeling X. Is that right?"

   Suppressing feelings doesn't make them go away;
   they leak out as snippy comments, withdrawal, or
   confusion about why the conversation isn't
   working.

THE IDENTITY-CONVERSATION TECHNIQUES

   Recognize: most difficult conversations have
   identity stakes for both parties.

   For yourself:
       What identity is at stake here?
       (Am I competent? Am I a good X? Am I respected?)
       Acknowledge it; don't suppress.
       Adopt complex self-image: "I'm capable AND
       sometimes make mistakes."

   For the other:
       Recognize their identity stakes.
       Avoid attacks that threaten core identity.
       Help them maintain dignity even when correcting
       behavior.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — PERFORMANCE CONVERSATION

   Surface: missed deadlines.

   What Happened conversation:
       Manager: "Three deadlines slipped this month."
       Employee: "The Y deadline was extended; the Z
       was always tight; X had unexpected dependencies."
       Both have facts; their stories differ.

   Feelings conversation:
       Manager (unsaid): frustrated; worried about
       team performance.
       Employee (unsaid): anxious; resenting feeling
       blamed; possibly feeling unsupported.

   Identity conversation:
       Manager: am I competent if my team is missing
       deadlines?
       Employee: am I competent? am I valued?

   Standard approach: just discuss the deadlines.
   Result: argument or grudging compliance.

   Difficult-conversations approach:
       Acknowledge all three layers.
       "I want to talk about deadlines, and I also
       want to acknowledge this conversation feels
       loaded for me — I'm worried about the team and
       wondering how this reflects on me as a manager.
       I imagine it might feel similar for you."
       
       Surface feelings; engage identity; then
       discuss what happened with both layers
       acknowledged.

   The conversation goes deeper, faster, more
   productively than surface treatment alone.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. SURFACE-ONLY TREATMENT
        Only the What Happened conversation.
        Recovery: surface feelings and identity.

   2. WIN-MINDSET
        Goal of convincing rather than learning.
        Recovery: explicit shift to learning.

   3. BLAME-FOCUS
        "Whose fault?" Recovery: contribution analysis.

   4. SUPPRESSED FEELINGS
        Pretending emotions aren't operating. Recovery:
        explicit naming.

   5. IDENTITY ATTACKS
        Statements that threaten core identity ("you
        always..."; "you're the kind of person who...").
        Recovery: separate behavior from identity.

   6. NO ACKNOWLEDGMENT
        Acting as if your view is the only sensible
        one. Recovery: acknowledge their story exists
        and is sensible from their position.

   7. SKIPPING SAFETY-CHECK
        Diving into hard content before relational
        safety. Recovery: establish safety first.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Conversation: ______________________________________

   Three-layer mapping:
       What Happened: differing stories?
           Mine: __________________________________
           Theirs (anticipated): __________________
       Feelings:
           Mine: __________________________________
           Theirs (anticipated): __________________
       Identity:
           My identity at stake: __________________
           Their identity at stake: _______________

   Goal:
       Shift from message-delivery to learning? Y / N

   Contribution analysis:
       My contribution to the situation: __________
       Their contribution: ________________________

   In-conversation:
       Surface feelings explicitly? Y / N
       Acknowledge identity? Y / N
       Listen for their story? Y / N

   Outcome: shared understanding; agreed actions
       _________________________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Recognize all three layers. The What Happened conversation is the surface; feelings and identity are the depths. Failed conversations almost always neglect the deeper two. (2) Shift goal from convincing to learning. The goal change is the largest single intervention; everything else follows. (3) Move from blame to contribution. Contribution analysis is constructive; blame is defensive. The shift produces insight and forward action. (4) Surface feelings and identity stakes. Suppressed feelings leak; ignored identity stakes derail. Naming them explicitly — for self and for other — is uncomfortable but productive.
