# Virtue Ethics

**One-line summary:** A moral framework holding that ethics is about character — the development of virtues like practical wisdom, courage, justice, and temperance — and that right action is what a person of virtue would do, with the ultimate goal being eudaimonia (human flourishing).

**When to reach for it:** Long-term character development, mentorship and coaching, situations where rule-based ethics seems hollow ("technically allowed but feels wrong"), professional ethics formation (medicine, law, education, leadership), and as one lens among several in any serious ethical analysis.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (~350 BCE) gave Western ethics its most thoroughly developed virtue-based framework. The core claim: ethics is fundamentally about **character (arete)**, not about rules or outcomes. Right action follows from being a person with the right virtues; the central question is not "what rule applies?" or "what produces the best outcome?" but **"what would a person of practical wisdom do here?"**

The non-obvious operational insight is that **virtue ethics is agent-centered rather than action-centered.** Deontology and consequentialism evaluate actions; virtue ethics evaluates the agent who performs them. This shift matters because two people performing the same external action can be morally different — one acting from courage, one from recklessness; one from justice, one from rigid rule-following. The character behind the action is the moral fact.

The classical Aristotelian virtues:

- **Practical wisdom (phronesis)** — the master virtue of knowing how to apply other virtues to specific situations
- **Courage (andreia)** — appropriate response to fear; the mean between cowardice and recklessness
- **Temperance (sōphrosynē)** — appropriate response to pleasure; the mean between deficiency and excess
- **Justice (dikaiosynē)** — giving each their due
- **Magnanimity, generosity, friendliness, truthfulness** — others in Aristotle's list

A second insight is the **doctrine of the mean**: virtues are typically the appropriate middle between two extremes. Courage is the mean between cowardice (deficiency) and recklessness (excess). Generosity is the mean between stinginess and prodigality. The mean is not arithmetic average; it's the appropriate response to the situation, which requires practical wisdom to determine.

A third insight is **virtue as habituation**: virtues are developed through practice, not acquired by argument. We become courageous by doing courageous acts; we become just by doing just acts. The implication for ethics development is that practice — and the institutions that structure practice (apprenticeship, professional formation, repeated decision-making in role) — matters more than reading ethical theory.

The framework's modern revival — Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) — argues that deontology and consequentialism have failed to produce stable moral consensus, and that we should return to virtue-based reasoning. The revival doesn't displace the other frameworks but supplements them.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "technically allowed" pathology.** Rule-based ethics permits actions that comply with the letter of rules but violate their spirit. Virtue ethics asks "what would a person of integrity do?" — catching cases where rule-compliance is itself problematic.
2. **The action-without-agent failure.** Consequentialism and deontology evaluate actions as if any actor could perform them equivalently. Virtue ethics recognizes that the same action by different actors has different moral weight; character matters.
3. **The short-term-rule-following trap.** Following rules in the moment without developing the underlying character produces brittle ethics that breaks under pressure. Virtue ethics's emphasis on habituation and long-term character development produces more robust ethical practice.

For mentors, leaders, coaches, and individuals committed to long-term character development, virtue ethics provides the framework that other ethical frameworks lack — the developmental, identity-formation dimension of being a good person, not just doing right actions.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | State the situation. What's at stake; what's the proposed action?               |
|    2 | Identify the virtues at play. Courage? Honesty? Justice? Practical wisdom?       |
|      | Multiple virtues usually apply.                                                  |
|    3 | Ask: "What would a person of [virtue] do here?" If multiple virtues, ask for     |
|      | each.                                                                            |
|    4 | Apply the doctrine of the mean. What's the appropriate response — neither too   |
|      | much nor too little? The mean isn't average; it's situational.                  |
|    5 | Cross-check: is this action consistent with the kind of person I aspire to be? |
|      | Does it strengthen or weaken the relevant virtues?                              |
|    6 | Consider character development. Will repeating this action habituate me toward  |
|      | virtue or away from it?                                                          |
|    7 | Cross-check with deontology and consequentialism. Where they converge,          |
|      | confidence is high. Where they diverge, the divergence reveals the structure   |
|      | of the dilemma.                                                                  |
|    8 | Decide and act, with awareness that virtue is built over time and that          |
|      | individual decisions accumulate into character.                                  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE VIRTUE INVENTORY (Aristotelian + extensions)

   Classical (Aristotelian):
       Practical wisdom (phronesis) — judgment in particular cases
       Courage — appropriate response to fear
       Temperance — appropriate response to pleasure
       Justice — giving each their due
       Magnanimity — appropriate sense of one's worth
       Generosity — appropriate giving
       Friendliness — appropriate sociality
       Truthfulness — accurate self-presentation

   Christian / Religious additions:
       Faith, hope, love (charity)
       Humility (a Christian addition; Aristotle viewed differently)

   Modern professional / civic virtues:
       Integrity, honesty, fairness
       Loyalty, civic responsibility
       Compassion, empathy
       Industry, perseverance

   Use the inventory to identify which virtues a situation engages.
   Most situations engage 2-4 virtues; tension among them is common.

THE DOCTRINE-OF-THE-MEAN TEMPLATE

   For each relevant virtue:

   Virtue: ____________________________________________

   Deficiency (too little): ___________________________
   Excess (too much): _________________________________
   Mean (appropriate response in this situation): _____

   The mean is situational, not formulaic. Practical wisdom is
   what tells you where it is in this case.

   Examples:
       Courage: cowardice ←→ courage ←→ recklessness
       Generosity: stinginess ←→ generosity ←→ prodigality
       Truthfulness: dishonesty ←→ truthfulness ←→ tactlessness
       Friendliness: surliness ←→ friendliness ←→ obsequiousness

THE "WHAT WOULD A PERSON OF VIRTUE DO" PROMPT

   Identify a model person — real or composite — who exemplifies
   the virtues relevant to this situation. Then ask:

       What would [model person] notice in this situation that
       I might miss?
       What would they prioritize?
       What action would they take?
       What would they refuse to do, even at cost?
       How would they balance competing considerations?

   The model can be a mentor, a historical figure, a fictional
   character, or a composite. The point is not imitation but
   inhabiting the perspective long enough to see the situation
   from a virtue-developed view.

THE CHARACTER-DEVELOPMENT QUESTION

   Beyond "what should I do here?", ask:

       What does this action habituate me toward?
       Does this make me more virtuous or less?
       What kind of person does someone who repeatedly does this
       become?

   Virtue is built through accumulated action. Single decisions
   matter not just for their direct effect but for the character
   they shape.

THE PRACTICAL-WISDOM RECOGNITION

   Phronesis — practical wisdom — is the master virtue. It's the
   capacity to:

   1. Recognize what's at stake morally in a situation
   2. Identify which virtues apply
   3. Locate the mean for each
   4. Balance competing virtues
   5. Act decisively without certainty

   Phronesis develops through:
   - Practice in particular cases
   - Mentorship from those further along
   - Reflection on outcomes
   - Building a stock of cases over time

   Cannot be acquired by reading alone. The framework is
   developmental — it predicts what a process of becoming virtuous
   looks like, not what virtue's content is by argument.

THE INSTITUTIONAL-FORMATION APPLICATION

   Professional ethics often rely heavily on virtue ethics:
       Medicine — virtues of compassion, truthfulness, beneficence
       Law — virtues of justice, fidelity, prudence
       Education — virtues of patience, fairness, wisdom
       Military — virtues of courage, honor, loyalty
       Leadership — virtues of integrity, courage, judgment

   The "code of ethics" of most professions is more virtue-based
   than rule-based — it specifies the kind of person the
   profession requires, not just the rules they must follow.

   Implication: ethics formation in professions should focus on
   habituation (apprenticeship, mentorship, practice in particular
   cases) more than on rule-memorization.

THE COMMON OBJECTIONS (and responses)

   1. CULTURAL RELATIVISM
        Different cultures have different virtues. Response:
        Aristotelian and modern virtue ethicists argue some virtues
        (honesty, justice, courage) are nearly universal; cultural
        variation is mostly in expression, not in core virtues.

   2. WHICH VIRTUES?
        Lists vary. Response: practical reflection on what makes
        humans flourish over time produces convergent (not identical)
        lists.

   3. CIRCULAR DEFINITION
        "Right action is what a virtuous person does; virtuous
        person is one who does right action." Response: the
        framework is developmental, not definitional. Becoming
        virtuous through practice produces the capacity to
        identify right action; the circle is virtuous (no pun).

   4. NO CLEAR DECISION PROCEDURE
        Doesn't tell you what to do in hard cases. Response:
        practical wisdom is supposed to be developed, not
        derived. The framework's value is in formation, not in
        producing algorithms.

THE QUICK-USE TEMPLATE

   Situation: __________________________________________________

   Virtues at play (top 2-4): __________________________________

   For each virtue, what's the mean in this situation?
       Virtue 1: __________ Mean: ____________________________
       Virtue 2: __________ Mean: ____________________________
       Virtue 3: __________ Mean: ____________________________

   Where do virtues conflict?
       _____________________________________________________

   "What would [admired model] do?"
       _____________________________________________________

   Character implications: does this build or erode virtue?
       _____________________________________________________

   Verdict (virtue ethics): action consistent with / inconsistent
   with the kind of person I'm trying to become.

   Cross-check with other frameworks.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Virtue ethics is developmental, not algorithmic. The framework's value is in long-term character formation, not in producing decision procedures for hard cases. Use it as a complement to rule-based and outcome-based reasoning, not as a substitute. (2) Identify the virtues at play in any situation; usually multiple apply. The doctrine of the mean is situational — what counts as courage in one context counts as recklessness in another. Practical wisdom is what tells you the difference. (3) Character is built through accumulated action. Single decisions matter for character even when their direct effects are small. The question "what does this make me?" complements "what does this produce?" (4) Use mentorship and case practice for ethics formation. Virtue ethics predicts that ethics development requires apprenticeship-style learning, not just argument. Build the institutional structures (mentoring, case discussion, supervised practice) that produce virtue.
