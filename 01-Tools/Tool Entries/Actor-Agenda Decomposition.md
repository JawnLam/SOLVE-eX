# Actor-Agenda Decomposition

**Also known as:** Agenda Funnel (field manual)

**One-line summary:** A structural decomposition that resolves a vague strategic want into six ordered layers — Actor → Agenda → Interest → Objective → Goal → Target — so that the layer at which a situation is actually getting misread can be located before any decision matrix is run.

**When to reach for it:** When a user describes a desire that resists concrete planning ("I want more influence," "We need to win this account," "He needs to leave the team"), when an actor's behavior is producing surprises that present analysis cannot explain, when a stakeholder conversation keeps shifting register without resolving, and at the start of any Phase 2 work where the destination needs to be sharpened from ambition into a target. Especially useful when the strategic situation feels intractable until the actor's actual agenda is named.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Actor-Agenda Decomposition** is the structural funnel that turns a long-running ambition into a specific person or institution the actor must move. The six layers, top to bottom:

1. **Actor** — the person or group capable of decisions. Naming the actor correctly is the precondition for everything below; "a company" rarely makes decisions, a particular executive does (Lam 2020 p. 150; Field Manual p. 11).
2. **Agenda** — the long-running ambition that organizes everything else. Rarely stated openly, often misidentified by observers. An executive's agenda is rarely "do my job well"; it is more likely "be the person who gets to choose what gets done" (Field Manual p. 12). Get the agenda wrong and every prediction below it drifts.
3. **Interest** — what the agenda produces at the moment. Interests are situational and changeable; losing an interest is recoverable; threatening an agenda is not (Lam 2020 p. 154; Field Manual p. 12).
4. **Objective** — a concrete aim derived from the interest. The first layer that can be written down without ambiguity. "Have more influence" is not an objective; "win the seat on the steering committee" is (Field Manual p. 12).
5. **Goal** — the specific measurable result that satisfies the objective. Where an objective can be described, a goal can be measured. "Be one of the seven named members in the charter when it is published next month" is the goal (Field Manual p. 12).
6. **Target** — the thing or person the actor must move to reach the goal. Targets are often the part of the situation an actor avoids naming clearly, because acknowledging a target forces a confrontation with the political reality of the goal (Field Manual p. 12).

The non-obvious property is that **the funnel runs only one direction.** You cannot work backward from a target to an agenda; you cannot pick a goal and reverse-engineer the interest (Field Manual p. 13). When an actor's behavior surprises you, the explanation almost always lies above the level of behavior you are observing — at the objective, interest, or agenda layer. This makes the funnel a tool for asking the right question: at what level am I getting this wrong?

A second insight: **a misread of agenda produces wrong predictions about every objective and goal the actor pursues; a misread of objective produces wrong predictions only about the current move.** Distinguishing the two saves enormous strategic effort. Most conversations that feel "stuck" are stuck because two parties are reasoning at different levels of the funnel without realizing it.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Skipping the funnel.** Acting from interest without naming objective, goal, and target produces motion without direction — the most common form of strategic vagueness (Field Manual p. 39, "Common failure modes").
2. **Agenda-objective conflation.** Treating an objective like an agenda makes losses feel catastrophic when they should be recoverable; treating an agenda like an interest makes a real threat get under-prepared.
3. **Avoiding target identification.** Naming the target forces the actor to confront political reality. Skipping the layer is psychologically protective but strategically expensive.

For executives, strategists, and any actor planning a political move, decomposing the funnel before deciding is the discipline that converts ambition into a specific, addressable move.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

The decomposition is a slow conversation, not a fill-in form. Each layer surfaces when the prior layer is clear.

1. **Name the Actor.** Who is the actor — specifically? If the user says "the company" or "our team," push until a named person or coalition of named participants emerges. Coalitions are valid; vague collectives are not.
2. **Surface the Agenda.** What ambition organizes this actor's behavior across the last several years? Watch for what they protect when under pressure — that is the agenda. The agenda is rarely the actor's stated mission statement.
3. **Locate the Interest.** What does the agenda produce as a current concern? Interests update quarterly. If the interest is more than 18 months old, it may actually be a sub-agenda.
4. **Articulate the Objective.** Convert the interest into a concrete aim — one sentence, one verb. "Have more influence" is interest-shaped; "secure operations as the primary internal customer of analytics" is objective-shaped.
5. **Define the Goal.** What measurable result, by when, would settle whether the objective was achieved? The goal is the objective's success criterion.
6. **Identify the Target.** Who or what must move for the goal to materialize? If the target is "the organization," push harder — the target is a person who can grant or deny.

After the funnel is complete, ask: at which layer would I be most wrong, and how would I know? The answer is the diagnostic value of the tool.

## Worked Example

A regional VP at a $4B industrial manufacturer feels strategically blocked. She describes her situation as "I'm not getting credit for the operational improvements my team has delivered."

Running the funnel:
- **Actor:** the regional VP, not her team.
- **Agenda:** become the company's center of operational gravity (a trajectory toward COO, then CEO) — surfaced after pressing on what she protects when under pressure.
- **Interest:** establish a credible claim that operations is the home of analytical decision-making in the firm.
- **Objective:** secure a defensible operating relationship with the central analytics group, short of full absorption.
- **Goal:** a chartered joint pilot in which her function and analytics co-deliver a margin improvement to one business unit.
- **Target:** the CEO's eventual decision to recognize operations as analytics' primary internal customer.

The original framing ("I'm not getting credit") was interest-level reasoning that had skipped the objective and target. The funnel surfaces that she has no defined target. The strategic conversation that follows is now about the CEO and the conditions under which she will form that judgment — not about who-deserves-credit. (Adapted from Field Manual ch. 7, Cresterly Industrial / Diana Reyes, p. 21.)

## Common Failure Modes

- **Naming a collective as the Actor.** "Our team," "the organization," "the leadership" — these do not act. A particular person or named coalition does. Push until a name emerges.
- **Stating an objective at agenda altitude.** "Be a transformational leader" is an agenda; it cannot be written down as a one-sentence objective. If the proposed objective is multi-decadal, it is the agenda — go back up.
- **Skipping target identification.** Treating "the company" or "the market" as the target conceals that some specific person controls the resource the goal requires. The target layer is uncomfortable for a reason.
- **Reverse-engineering down the funnel.** Picking a target ("I'll go talk to the CFO") and back-fitting an objective is an inversion of the funnel's logic. The CFO conversation may be the right move, but only if the funnel built from agenda confirms it. If the funnel was skipped, the move is interest-driven motion without strategic anchor.
