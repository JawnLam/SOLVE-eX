# Seven Generations Thinking

**One-line summary:** A decision discipline rooted in Haudenosaunee constitutional tradition: any significant decision must be evaluated for its impact on seven generations forward (~140-200 years), forcing consideration of consequences invisible at shorter time horizons.

**When to reach for it:** Decisions with multigenerational consequences (environmental, infrastructure, social policy, institution design, family legacy, asset allocation, climate); leadership contexts where the cultural default is quarterly or annual horizons; planning that requires distinguishing short-term reversible costs from long-term irreversible consequences; cross-cultural strategy that incorporates Indigenous knowledge frameworks.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

The principle comes from the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy's *Great Law of Peace* (Kaianere'kó:wa), one of the oldest continuous democratic traditions in the world. Article 28: leaders deliberating on the people's behalf must consider how their decisions will affect *the seventh generation*. Calculating roughly 20-30 years per generation, this is a 140-200 year horizon for every major decision.

The non-obvious operational claim: **the time horizon you adopt shapes which features of a decision become visible.** At quarterly horizon, downstream consequences are invisible; at multigenerational horizon, they dominate. A decision that appears optimal at a 3-year horizon may appear catastrophic at a 150-year horizon — and the reverse: investments that seem unjustifiable at short horizons (long-lived infrastructure, deep institutional design, ecological restoration) become obvious necessities at multigenerational ones.

The practice forces three specific moves:

1. **Name the seventh generation.** Imagine specific descendants — your great-great-great-great-grandchildren. Living where, doing what, inheriting what conditions?
2. **Trace the decision's consequences along that timeline.** What does each year, each generation, look like as a function of this decision?
3. **Account for accumulation.** Not just "what is the consequence in 150 years" but "what compounds along the way" — environmental loads accumulate, institutional habits ossify, intergenerational poverty perpetuates.

A second insight: **the discipline requires identifying which costs are reversible and which are not.** A short-term cost that reverts is bearable; a permanent cost that compounds is catastrophic. Seven-generations thinking emphasizes irreversibility detection — destruction of an ecosystem, loss of a language, soil exhaustion — over short-term magnitude.

A third insight: **the framework is intentionally not future-discounting.** Standard economics applies a discount rate (typically 3-5%) that makes consequences 100 years out functionally invisible. The Haudenosaunee tradition rejects this on ethical grounds: the seventh-generation descendant is a *real person* with full moral standing, not a discounted abstraction.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Discount-rate blindness.** Decisions that look good at modern discount rates but disastrous in absolute multigenerational terms. The framework refuses the discounting.
2. **Reversible/irreversible conflation.** Treating all costs as fungible. The framework foregrounds irreversibility.
3. **Generational externalization.** Pushing costs onto descendants who have no voice in the decision. The framework gives them voice by imagining them concretely.

The framework's contemporary uptake is substantial: Long Now Foundation, Indigenous-led environmental governance (UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues), some climate ethics frameworks (Stephen Gardiner's *Perfect Moral Storm*), and growing corporate-governance interest in "stakeholder capitalism" that includes future-people.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|  1   | Frame the decision. What is being decided? What are the options?                |
|  2   | Identify the seventh generation: ~140-200 years forward (year:        ).        |
|  3   | Imagine specific descendants. Not "humanity in general" — particular people     |
|      | living in particular places. Name them if helpful.                              |
|  4   | Trace each decision option through time. What does each generation's            |
|      | experience of the consequence look like? 1st, 2nd, 3rd, ... 7th.                |
|  5   | Identify ACCUMULATING vs ONE-TIME costs. Which compound? Which dissipate?       |
|  6   | Identify IRREVERSIBLE consequences. Mark them. They have higher moral weight   |
|      | regardless of size.                                                             |
|  7   | Compare options as if the seventh-generation descendants had a vote. Which      |
|      | option would they ratify?                                                        |
|  8   | Make the decision, with explicit acknowledgment of any costs you're imposing    |
|      | on the future.                                                                  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
Seven Generations Worksheet

DECISION (with options):
   ____________________________________________________

CURRENT YEAR:          _____
TARGET (7 generations): _____ (current + ~140-200 years)

THE DESCENDANT (imagine specifically):
   Name (optional):  _________________________________
   Year of life:     _____ (~Year you'll reach)
   Geography:        _________________________________
   Conditions they inherit: __________________________

GENERATIONAL TIMELINE (per option):

Option A: _________________________________________

   Generation 1 (~yrs 0-25 from now):
     Experience: ______________________________
     Costs/benefits: ___________________________

   Generation 2 (~yrs 25-50):
     Experience: ______________________________
     Costs/benefits: ___________________________

   Generation 4 (~yrs 75-100):
     Experience: ______________________________
     Costs/benefits: ___________________________

   Generation 7 (~yrs 150-200):
     Experience: ______________________________
     Costs/benefits: ___________________________

   ACCUMULATING costs in option A: _________________
   REVERSIBLE? _____  IRREVERSIBLE? _____

Option B: _________________________________________
   [repeat the timeline]

THE DESCENDANT'S VOTE:
   Which option would the seventh-generation descendant ratify?
   _________________________________________________
   On what grounds? ________________________________

DECISION:
   Option chosen: __________________________________
   Costs imposed on future (named, not euphemized):
   _________________________________________________
   Mitigations or compensations: ____________________
```

> **Operational notes:** (1) **Imagining specifically beats imagining abstractly.** "Future generations" is too vague to generate intuitions. Naming a great-great-great-great-grandchild who lives in a specific place forces concreteness. (2) **The discipline is harder than it sounds** because modern institutional structures (electoral cycles, quarterly reporting, lifetime asset allocation) actively suppress multigenerational thinking. Expect resistance from collaborators trained in short-horizon decision modes. (3) **The framework is most powerful for irreversibility detection.** Many decisions that *look* like quarterly choices are actually 150-year commitments (infrastructure, treaty entry, institutional design, ecological alteration). The framework's gift is surfacing this. (4) **Cross-cultural use carries responsibility.** The framework is rooted in living Haudenosaunee constitutional tradition; non-Indigenous use is appropriate but should acknowledge the lineage and avoid New Age dilution.
