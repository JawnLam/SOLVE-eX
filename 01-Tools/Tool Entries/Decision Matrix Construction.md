# Decision Matrix Construction

**Also known as:** ADAPT decision matrix; PP/AA matrix; Adverse-Alternative Scoring

**One-line summary:** A forcing-function matrix that scores every option in a decision on both Preferred Position (what success looks like) and Adverse Alternative (what failure costs), each across Benefit, Cost, and Utility — converting the option-comparison question from "which upside is most attractive?" to "which preferred-position upside is most attractive given what its adverse-alternative downside would cost?"

**When to reach for it:** When an actor faces two or more genuinely live options and the inclination is to pick the one whose upside feels most vivid; when the option set is emotionally weighted (an offer received, a confrontation contemplated, a deal on the table); when "go big" reasoning is competing with workhorse reasoning and the discipline to score both AA and PP would discriminate; at the threshold of Phase 5 commitment. The matrix is most valuable when the emotional stakes are high — that is when intuition has biased toward writing only the preferred position.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Decision Matrix Construction** is the structural discipline at the heart of the ADAPT Loop's Decide beat. Every move has two possible futures — the **preferred position (PP)**, the outcome the actor is pushing for, and the **adverse alternative (AA)**, the outcome that obtains if the preferred position fails. The matrix asks the actor to score each option on both, across three measures:

- **Benefit (B)** — value the actor gains in that scenario
- **Cost (C)** — value the actor spends in that scenario
- **Utility (U)** — net difference, B − C

This produces at least four components per option — B(PP), C(PP), B(AA), C(AA) — and derived utilities U(PP) and U(AA). For a three-option decision the matrix has eighteen cells (Field Manual p. 15). The shorthand on the master diagram compresses these; the discipline of the tool is to decompress them.

The non-obvious operational insight: **the right reading of the matrix is to compare the U(PP) / U(AA) pair across options, not to pick the option with the highest U(PP).** The matrix's discipline is in the adverse alternative, not the preferred position (Field Manual p. 25, Cresterly Lessons). A practitioner who scores only preferred positions will systematically over-bet — selecting bets in situations that require workhorses (see `Bet vs Workhorse Discrimination`).

A second insight: **the multi-PP hierarchy sub-discipline.** Actors can have ranked preferences within preferred positions. The dissertation's Rusty case (Lam 2020 saturation Tables 17–21) operates with P1 = "Todd leaves voluntarily" preferred over P2 = "Rusty fires Todd," both preferred over X = "Todd stays." Each preferred position has its own resource cost and adverse-side spillover; collapsing them into a single PP throws away the discrimination the actor most needs to make.

A third insight: **the matrix functions as a forcing function before it functions as an analytic tool.** "The result of running the matrix is the selection — the single move the actor commits to. The matrix is therefore not a decision aid in the soft sense; it is a forcing function. It requires the actor to articulate what failure looks like before committing to action — which is the discipline that separates running the cycle from improvising it" (Field Manual p. 16).

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Inflating PP, deflating AA.** Actors consistently under-score the AA cost (it is hypothetical) while over-scoring the PP benefit (it is emotionally vivid). Skipping the AA columns is selecting bets, not plans (Field Manual p. 39).
2. **Picking highest U(PP).** Reading the matrix as "which option has the best upside" rather than "which option's upside is most attractive given its downside cost" produces option-selection that converges on bets even when workhorses are available.
3. **Collapsing multi-PP hierarchy.** Treating "Todd leaves voluntarily" and "I fire Todd" as a single preferred position erases the distinction between two options with very different costs and political spillover.

For executives, founders, and any actor at the threshold of a meaningful commitment, the matrix is the discipline that converts intuition into a defensible, articulated selection.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

The matrix is built in five steps. Skipping the AA columns is the canonical violation; the discipline is that the matrix is not run until both columns are written.

1. **Enumerate the options.** Two minimum, typically three to five. If only one option is in view, the matrix cannot run — return to Phase 4 (Vision) to generate alternatives.
2. **For each option, write the Preferred Position.** A one-sentence description of what success looks like — the future the actor is pushing for in that option.
3. **For each option, write the Adverse Alternative.** A one-sentence description of what obtains if the preferred position fails — the fallback that defines what failure costs. **This is the step practitioners skip.** It is what the matrix is for.
4. **Score B(PP), C(PP), B(AA), C(AA) for each option.** Numerical scoring is useful (a 0–10 scale per cell with explicit unit notation works) but the values matter less than the act of writing each cell explicitly. Once written, U(PP) and U(AA) derive as B − C.
5. **Read the matrix as pairs.** For each option, look at U(PP) and U(AA) together. The right reading is which option's preferred-position utility is most attractive given the cost its adverse alternative would impose. An option with high U(PP) and catastrophic U(AA) is a bet; an option with modest U(PP) and tolerable U(AA) is a workhorse; mature actors favor workhorses.

A useful test: if the actor selects the option with the highest U(PP), they should be able to defend the choice in terms of the AA's tolerability, not just the PP's attractiveness. If they cannot, they are selecting bets.

## Worked Example

A regional VP at Cresterly Industrial faces three options for advancing her interest in establishing operations as the primary internal customer of the central analytics group. The matrix she runs (numerical scoring on a 0–10 scale per cell, simplified for illustration):

| Measure | A: Frontal request | B: Pilot proposal | C: Quiet coalition building |
|---|---|---|---|
| B(PP) | 9 | 6 | 4 |
| C(PP) | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| **U(PP)** | **+5** | **+4** | **+3** |
| B(AA) | 0 | 3 | 2 |
| C(AA) | 8 | 3 | 4 |
| **U(AA)** | **−8** | **0** | **−2** |

Read as PP-only, Option A wins (highest U(PP) = +5). The matrix's discipline is in the AA row. Option A's adverse alternative — frontal refusal by the CEO, public labeling as territorial, the CTO's position strengthened — is catastrophic. Option C has acceptable downside but no upside motion. Option B has a modest preferred-position payoff and an essentially neutral adverse-alternative outcome. Option B is the workhorse; she selects B. (Adapted from Field Manual ch. 7, Cresterly / Diana Reyes, p. 22.)

The matrix did not pick the option with the highest U(PP). It picked the option whose pair of futures was most defensible — and the defensibility was in the AA, not the PP.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Skipping the AA columns.** The most common failure. Without explicit AA scoring, the actor is selecting bets, not plans. The matrix has not been run.
- **Single-PP collapse.** Treating multiple ranked preferred positions as one PP erases the distinction the actor most needs to make. Multi-PP hierarchy is a sub-discipline of the tool, not an optional refinement.
- **Picking highest U(PP).** The matrix's right reading is U(PP) / U(AA) pair, not U(PP) alone. A high U(PP) with catastrophic U(AA) is a bet; mature actors favor workhorses.
- **Inflating PP via emotional vividness.** Recent good news about an option, social warmth from a counterparty, or a vivid imagined success scenario will inflate the PP score. The discipline is to write the cost column first, before the benefit column.
- **Running the matrix after commitment.** The matrix is a forcing function before commitment. Run after commitment, it becomes a rationalization. If the user is already mid-execution, the matrix's value has shifted to the perception gap audit (see `Perception and Actual Effect Gap Audit`).
