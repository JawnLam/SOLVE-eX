# Frame Analysis

**One-line summary:** Identifies the cognitive frames a piece of communication activates — the structures of interpretation that determine what counts as a fact, a problem, an actor, and a solution — and lets the analyst either expose, exploit, or replace them.

**When to reach for it:** Political and policy communication, brand and category positioning, crisis statements, change-management messaging, stakeholder analysis where the same facts mean different things to different audiences, and anywhere "we agree on the data but disagree on what it means" describes the situation.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

A **frame** is a cognitive structure that organizes interpretation — the schema that tells a person what kind of situation they are in, who the actors are, what counts as evidence, and what would resolve the situation. Goffman (1974) called frames "schemata of interpretation" and showed that ordinary social life depends on people answering, automatically and continuously, "what is going on here?" Lakoff (1996, 2004) extended the analysis to political language: every word activates a frame, and once activated the frame brings with it a set of presupposed roles and relationships.

The operationally important Lakoff move is *negation does not deactivate*. "I am not a crook" activates the crook frame. "Don't think of an elephant" activates the elephant frame. Refusing a frame by negating it strengthens it. The only way out of a frame is to substitute a different one — and the substitution must be done with different vocabulary, not with the original frame's vocabulary in negative form.

The non-obvious operational insight: **most policy and strategy disagreements are frame disagreements masquerading as fact disagreements.** Two people looking at the same numbers can land on opposite conclusions because their frames assign different roles to the actors and different valences to the outcomes. Adding more data does not resolve the disagreement; it gets reabsorbed into whichever frame is already active.

Frames operate at multiple levels:

- **Surface frames** — single words and phrases ("tax relief" frames tax as an affliction; "death tax" frames estate tax as something inflicted on death; "freedom fighters" vs "rebels" vs "terrorists" frame the same group three different ways)
- **Deep frames** — the moral / metaphorical structures that organize whole worldviews (Lakoff's "Strict Father" vs "Nurturant Parent" models in U.S. politics)
- **Episode frames** — what kind of event we are in ("crisis," "negotiation," "celebration," "trial") — Goffman's primary territory

For consulting and communication work, the practical move is: name the frame the audience is currently in, decide whether to operate within it or switch it, and design the language accordingly.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three classes of failure that frame analysis exposes which content-only analysis misses:

1. **Negation amplification.** A leader rebuts a critic by repeating the critic's frame in negative form ("we are NOT abandoning quality") and unintentionally strengthens the critic's narrative. Audiences remember the activated frame, not the negation.
2. **Vocabulary capture.** Adopting an opponent's vocabulary even with the intent to fight from inside it concedes the underlying frame. The frame's presuppositions become the unspoken ground of debate, and the opposition's strongest arguments become unsayable.
3. **Frame mismatch in negotiation.** One party is in a "problem-solving" frame (joint discovery, integrative bargaining) while the other is in a "competition" frame (zero-sum, distributive bargaining). The same offer reads as cooperation to one and as weakness to the other. Naming the frame mismatch is the move that resolves the asymmetry.

For change management specifically, frame analysis catches the failure mode where the change leader keeps describing the change in the operational/managerial frame ("efficiency," "alignment," "synergies") while the workforce is in the moral/identity frame ("respect," "fairness," "what we stand for"). The leader is not wrong on the operations and the workforce is not wrong on the values — they are speaking different frames at the same data, and the gap looks like resistance when it is mistranslation.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | State the situation in plain language. What is the communication trying to do?  |
|    2 | Audit the current vocabulary. Which words / phrases are doing frame work?       |
|      | (Hint: any word the audience reacts to emotionally is carrying a frame.)        |
|    3 | For each loaded term, name the frame: who are the actors, what are the roles,   |
|      | what is the implied resolution, what is the moral valence?                      |
|    4 | Identify the audience's current frame. Is it the same as the speaker's?         |
|      | If not, where does the gap live?                                                |
|    5 | Decide: operate within the audience's frame, or shift them to a new frame?      |
|      | Within-frame is easier and faster; cross-frame is slower but sometimes the only |
|      | option (e.g., when the existing frame structurally excludes the speaker's       |
|      | preferred resolution).                                                          |
|    6 | If shifting frames: design the substitute frame with new vocabulary, new actor  |
|      | roles, and new metaphors. Do NOT negate the old frame — substitute it.          |
|    7 | Pre-test the language with audience members or proxies. Frames are easier to    |
|      | spot from the outside than from inside. Listen for "you mean…" rephrasings —    |
|      | those reveal the frame the audience is hearing.                                 |
|    8 | Deliver. After delivery, audit by asking: which frame did the audience walk     |
|      | away holding? Compare to the intended frame.                                    |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FRAME AUDIT (apply to any draft communication)

   1. List the loaded terms. Mark every word in the draft that carries
      emotional or moral weight beyond its dictionary meaning.

   2. For each loaded term, fill in:

      Term: ____________________________________
      Frame activated: __________________________
      Actors implied (who is in what role): _____
      Resolution implied (what counts as a "win"): _____
      Moral valence: ____________________________
      Audience-side reaction (who hears this how): _____

   3. Composite question: across all loaded terms, what coherent frame
      does this draft live inside? Is it the frame I want?

THE FOUR FRAME-WORK MOVES

   1. NAME the frame.
      Once you can articulate "you are using the X frame," you can step out
      of automatic reaction and decide whether to operate inside it.

   2. EXPOSE the frame.
      In analysis or critique, naming someone's frame to a third party
      undermines its automatic operation. (Lakoff's "Strict Father / Nurturant
      Parent" exposure of US conservative / liberal frames is the canonical
      example.)

   3. OPERATE WITHIN the frame.
      When the audience is committed to a frame and switching costs are too
      high, the move is to make your case inside their frame. Concedes the
      ground but earns the audience.

   4. SUBSTITUTE the frame.
      When the existing frame structurally excludes your preferred outcome,
      the move is to introduce a different frame with different vocabulary,
      different actor roles, different metaphors. Substitution is slow,
      expensive, and the only option when other moves cannot work.

THE NEGATION TRAP (the single most common failure)

   "I am NOT a crook."     →  activates the crook frame
   "We are NOT cutting     →  activates the cutting-quality frame
     quality."
   "This is NOT a layoff." →  activates the layoff frame

   Diagnostic: if your defense begins with "we are not..." and uses the
   opposition's vocabulary, you are reinforcing their frame even as you
   reject it.

   Fix: say what the situation IS, with new vocabulary. Do not restate the
   accusation in any form, positive or negative.

LEVELS OF FRAME (what to attack at each level)

   SURFACE (words / phrases) — fast, tactical, message-discipline territory
       Tools: word substitution lists, phrase audits, talking-point reviews

   EPISODE (what kind of event are we in?) — medium-term, event-design
       Tools: meeting design, situation labeling, ceremonial design
       Goffman's territory: footings, keyings, fabrications

   DEEP (worldview / moral structure) — slow, strategic, multi-year
       Tools: brand narrative, organizational story, cultural change
       Lakoff's territory: moral politics, conceptual metaphor

   For change management: the surface and episode levels are where
   short-term wins live; the deep level is where lasting change lives.

CROSS-FRAME DIAGNOSIS (when you and the audience disagree)

   Question 1: are we using the same words for the same things?
       (vocabulary check)

   Question 2: are we assigning the same roles to the same actors?
       (role check — the most common mismatch)

   Question 3: are we treating the same outcomes as wins?
       (valence check — moral / identity territory)

   Question 4: are we in the same kind of event?
       (episode check — negotiation vs. competition vs. crisis)

   Most "they are unreasonable" experiences are role-check or episode-check
   failures. The other side is reasoning correctly within a frame the
   speaker has not yet identified.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) The negation trap is the discipline most often violated. Audit every defensive statement for whether it restates the accusation. If it does, rewrite from positive ground. (2) Frames are sticky for cognitive reasons, not because the audience is uninformed. Adding facts to a contested situation generally entrenches existing frames rather than dissolving them. The fix is reframing, not data. (3) Substitution is expensive — it requires new vocabulary, new metaphors, sustained repetition. Most communication work should operate within the audience's existing frame; substitution is reserved for cases where the existing frame structurally excludes the desired outcome. (4) Frame-spotting is easier from the outside than from inside your own work. Build a critic / outside-reader practice into any high-stakes communication, and listen specifically for "what they meant was…" rephrasings — those expose the frame the audience activated, which is often not the one the speaker intended.
