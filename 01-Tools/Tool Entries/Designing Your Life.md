# Designing Your Life

**One-line summary:** A design-thinking-based methodology for life and career planning that treats your own life as a design problem — building a compass, prototyping multiple possible futures, and iterating through small experiments rather than committing to a single plan.

**When to reach for it:** Career transitions, mid-life or post-college reorientation, retirement planning, role changes within an organization, helping a coachee or family member who's "stuck," and any situation where the question is "what should I do with my life?" rather than "how do I do this specific thing?"

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Bill Burnett and Dave Evans, both Stanford d.school faculty with backgrounds in product design, applied **design-thinking methodology** to the problem of life and career planning. Their *Designing Your Life* (2016) is the operational synthesis. The core insight: **most life-planning approaches treat the future as a problem to be solved with the right answer; design thinking treats it as a wicked problem that requires reframing, prototyping, and iteration.**

The framework's key moves:

1. **Build a compass** — write a "workview" (what work means to you, why you work) and "lifeview" (what you believe matters, what's important about life). Coherence between these is the design constraint.
2. **Good Time Journal** — log activities, noting which produce engagement, energy, and flow. This is empirical data about what life designs would actually fit.
3. **Three Odyssey Plans** — design three very different 5-year futures: (a) what you're already planning, (b) what you'd do if your current path disappeared, (c) what you'd do if money / image weren't constraints. The diversity is the design move; reading them back reveals what you actually value.
4. **Prototype** — for each candidate path, generate small experiments: conversations with people in that path, short trials of related activities, exploratory projects. Don't commit; explore.
5. **Iterate** — based on prototype results, refine paths. Choose only when prototypes have produced enough information to decide; commitment without prototyping is generally premature.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **the right question is not "what should I do?" but "what would I want to test?"** The reframe converts paralyzing decisions into tractable experiments. People who can't decide between Path A and Path B can usually decide what experiment would distinguish them.

A second insight is the **dysfunctional-belief diagnosis**. Many people are stuck not because they lack options but because they hold beliefs that close off options. "I should already know what I want." "If I'm passionate about it, I shouldn't have to work hard." "It's too late to change." Burnett and Evans catalog these and reframe each. The reframing is sometimes more valuable than the workshop process — naming a belief breaks its hold.

A third insight is **Plan B / C as protection**. Designing only one future is risky; designing three protects against any one failing. The discipline of generating three substantially different paths surfaces options that single-path thinking misses.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "find your passion" trap.** "Find what you're passionate about and make it your career" produces paralysis when no single passion is obvious — which is most of the time. Design thinking allows multiple paths to be valid; passion can be cultivated through engaged work, not just discovered in advance.
2. **The premature-commitment failure.** Choosing Path A before testing it produces buyer's remorse when reality differs from imagination. Prototyping reveals what each path actually contains before commitment.
3. **The single-imagined-future failure.** Most career planning works with one envisioned future. Failure of that future feels catastrophic. Three Odyssey Plans creates redundancy; failure of one is failure of one path among several.

For consultants helping clients with career transitions, executives reorganizing teams, and individuals at life inflection points, *Designing Your Life* provides a structured process that resists the dual failures of paralysis and premature commitment.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Write your Workview (~250 words). What is work for? What does it mean to you?   |
|      | What makes work good or bad? How does work relate to a life well-lived?          |
|    2 | Write your Lifeview (~250 words). What is your view of the world? What gives      |
|      | meaning? What's the relationship between self and others, individuals and        |
|      | larger groups?                                                                   |
|    3 | Identify coherence / incoherence between Workview and Lifeview. Where do they    |
|      | align? Where do they conflict? The conflicts are design constraints.            |
|    4 | Keep a Good Time Journal for 2-3 weeks. Track activities, noting engagement,    |
|      | energy gain/drain, and any flow experiences.                                     |
|    5 | Mine the journal: what activities consistently produce engagement / energy /    |
|      | flow? These point to design ingredients.                                         |
|    6 | Draft three Odyssey Plans, each describing a substantially different 5-year     |
|      | path. (a) current path extended; (b) alternative if current path disappeared;   |
|      | (c) wild card if money / image weren't issues.                                   |
|    7 | For each plan, identify the "questions" it raises and the "prototypes" that     |
|      | would test them. Prototypes are conversations with people in the path or small  |
|      | trial activities, not life-changing decisions.                                   |
|    8 | Run the prototypes. Iterate the plans based on what you learn. Commit only      |
|      | after prototyping yields enough information.                                    |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE WORKVIEW PROMPT

   Write 250 words on each:
       What is work for?
       What does work mean?
       What makes work worthwhile / good / fulfilling?
       What is the relationship between work, the individual,
       money, others, and society?
       Where does success in work come from?

THE LIFEVIEW PROMPT

   Write 250 words on each:
       Why are we here?
       What is the meaning / purpose of life?
       What is the relationship of self to others?
       Where does meaning / fulfillment come from in life?
       What is the role of family, community, country, world?
       What is the role of joy, sorrow, love, money, success,
       failure?
       What constitutes a life well-lived?

THE COHERENCE CHECK

   Read Workview and Lifeview side by side.

       Where do they align?     ____________________________________
       Where do they conflict?  ____________________________________
       What does work do for the kind of life you want?
                                ____________________________________
       What does life give back to the kind of work you want?
                                ____________________________________

   The conflicts and tensions are design constraints — paths
   chosen must navigate them, not ignore them.

THE GOOD TIME JOURNAL TEMPLATE

   Daily log (2-3 weeks):

   Date:        ___________________
   Activity:    ___________________
   Engagement:  ☐ Low  ☐ Medium  ☐ High
   Energy:      ☐ Drain  ☐ Neutral  ☐ Boost
   Flow:        ☐ No  ☐ Some  ☐ Full
   Notes:       ___________________

   At end of period, review:
       Activities consistently producing high engagement: _________
       Activities consistently producing energy boost:    _________
       Activities consistently producing flow:            _________
       Activities producing energy drain (and why):       _________

   These observations feed into design ingredient inventory.

THE ODYSSEY-PLAN TEMPLATE

   Three plans, each covering 5 years. For EACH plan, complete:

   PLAN ___ (1, 2, or 3)
   Title: _____________________________________________________

   Drawing of timeline (years 1-5; 2-3 milestones each year):

   |---|---|---|---|---|
   Y1   Y2   Y3   Y4   Y5

   At each milestone, what's happening? Where do you live? Who's
   in your life? What's your work? What's your typical week?

   Self-assessment dashboard:
       Resources (financial, time, network):    Low / Med / High
       Likeability (how appealing is this?):     Low / Med / High
       Confidence (you can pull it off):         Low / Med / High
       Coherence (matches Workview / Lifeview):  Low / Med / High

   Three questions this plan raises:
       1. _____________________________________________________
       2. _____________________________________________________
       3. _____________________________________________________

   Plan 1: extension of current path
   Plan 2: alternative if current path disappeared
   Plan 3: wild card if money / image weren't issues

THE PROTOTYPE DESIGN

   For each of the questions raised by your plans, design a prototype:

   PROTOTYPE TYPE 1: PROTOTYPE CONVERSATIONS
       Who in this path can I talk to?
       What questions would I ask them?
       (Goal: not advice, but stories of their experience)

   PROTOTYPE TYPE 2: PROTOTYPE EXPERIENCES
       What small activity could I do that's a sample of this path?
       Volunteer, shadow, take a class, side project.
       (Goal: lived experience, not imagined)

   Rules:
   - Prototypes are time-bounded (1 day to a few months max)
   - Prototypes don't require commitment
   - Prototypes generate information you couldn't get otherwise
   - Run multiple prototypes per plan; one isn't enough

THE DYSFUNCTIONAL-BELIEF CATALOG

   Common dysfunctional beliefs Burnett & Evans flag:

   "I should already know what I want."
        REFRAME: Most people don't. Design is iterative.

   "If I'm passionate about it, I shouldn't have to work hard."
        REFRAME: Passion is cultivated through engagement, not
        discovered before it.

   "It's too late to change."
        REFRAME: Design works at any age; many great careers
        start in mid-life.

   "I have to be the best at what I do or it's not worth doing."
        REFRAME: Engagement and meaning don't require being the
        best; they require honest practice.

   "If I make the wrong choice, I'll ruin my life."
        REFRAME: Almost no choices are irreversible. Iteration
        is built in.

   "I should have a 'true calling' that I find."
        REFRAME: Most people have multiple legitimate paths.
        The question is which to test, not which to find.

   Audit your own beliefs about life / career; reframe the
   dysfunctional ones before designing.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. SKIPPING THE COMPASS
        Going straight to plans without Workview / Lifeview.
        Recovery: write the compass first.

   2. DESIGNING ONE PLAN
        "I just need to figure out my one path." Recovery:
        force three substantially different plans.

   3. PROTOTYPING TOO BIG
        "I'll quit my job to test the new path." Recovery:
        prototypes are conversations and experiments, not
        commitments.

   4. NOT ITERATING
        Treating the first plan draft as final. Recovery: design
        is iterative; expect 2-4 cycles.

   5. ANALYSIS PARALYSIS
        Endless plan refinement without prototyping. Recovery:
        prototypes generate the information needed to decide;
        more thinking doesn't.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Three Odyssey Plans, not one. The diversity is the design move; reading them back reveals what you actually value. Single-plan thinking misses options that multi-plan thinking surfaces. (2) Prototype before committing. Conversations with people in the path and small experiences are dramatically more informative than imagined futures. Most life-planning failures come from imagining a path without testing what it actually contains. (3) Reframe dysfunctional beliefs first. Many people are stuck because they hold beliefs that close off options ("too late," "should already know"). Identifying and reframing these is sometimes the entire intervention. (4) Iterate. Design is iterative by nature; expect 2-4 cycles between plan and prototype. Treating any single plan as final misses the point of the framework — the value is in the iteration, not the first draft.
