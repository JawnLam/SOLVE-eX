# Moral Foundations Disgust Probe

**One-line summary:** A diagnostic worksheet that asks whether a strong aversion you're feeling is *moral disgust* (a purity-foundation signal) vs *contempt* (community/hierarchy signal) vs *anger* (autonomy/fairness signal) — separating the felt aversion from the moral reasoning that often gets attached to it post-hoc.

**When to reach for it:** When you find yourself strongly opposed to something but can't articulate the harm; when a policy or behavior triggers a "this is gross" reaction; in moral disagreements where the parties seem to be talking past each other; in therapy, debate prep, or political analysis when disentangling moral emotions matters.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory and Rozin's disgust research established two important findings:

1. **Disgust is a moral emotion, not just a physical one.** The same neural-physiological response (the curled-lip face, the rejection impulse) gets triggered by spoiled meat, by incest, by certain political opponents, and by violations of one's sacred values. The body doesn't distinguish; the mind constructs the moral story after the felt-signal.
2. **Moral reasoning is often post-hoc.** Haidt's "social intuitionist model" shows that we feel the moral judgment first (the moral *intuition*, often disgust-flavored) and then reason backward to construct a justification. Asked to defend the judgment, people generate reasons; when those reasons are dismantled, the judgment usually doesn't move (moral "dumbfounding").

The non-obvious operational insight: **separating the felt-signal from the moral claim is a high-leverage move.** If you can identify "I'm feeling moral disgust" as the emotion *first*, you can then ask the second-order question: "Is this disgust pointing at a genuine purity violation, or is it a category error (treating something foreign as something contaminating)?" The probe doesn't require you to revise your judgment — it just makes the felt-component visible.

A second insight: **the CAD triad maps moral emotions to moral foundations.** Contempt → community violations (hierarchy, loyalty); Anger → autonomy violations (fairness, rights); Disgust → divinity/purity violations (sanctity, degradation). Mapping your reaction to a foundation surfaces what moral grammar you're actually using.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the probe prevents:

1. **Moralizing distaste.** Treating a personal taste as a moral judgment. The probe forces the question: is this a purity violation, or is it just unfamiliar / unpleasant?
2. **Stuck cross-foundation arguments.** When two people are arguing from different moral foundations (one from fairness/autonomy, the other from purity/sanctity), they generate non-overlapping reasons. The probe surfaces which foundation you're actually using, opening the possibility of cross-foundation translation.
3. **Disgust-as-policy-input.** Allowing a disgust reaction to drive policy preferences without examining whether the disgust is calibrated. Disgust evolved to protect against pathogens and incest; its modern firings on out-groups, foods, sexual practices, etc. may or may not track genuine harm. The probe lets you check.

This tool is descriptive (helps you see your own moral grammar), not normative (doesn't tell you what to value). Useful for self-awareness, cross-cultural moral analysis, and policy debate preparation.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|  1   | Notice the aversion. What concretely triggered it? (Stay specific.)             |
|  2   | Probe the felt-character: is this DISGUST (gross / contaminating / want to      |
|      | wash / curled lip), CONTEMPT (looking-down / dismissive), or ANGER              |
|      | (provoked / want to confront)? Often more than one is present; weight them.    |
|  3   | Map to moral foundation: Disgust → Sanctity/Purity. Contempt → Loyalty /        |
|      | Authority / Liberty-from. Anger → Fairness / Care / Liberty-of.                |
|  4   | Articulate the moral claim. Now that the foundation is named, what specifically |
|      | do you say is wrong? Write it.                                                  |
|  5   | Cross-foundation test: Could someone using a different moral foundation hold    |
|      | the opposite view in good faith? What would they say?                           |
|  6   | Calibration: Is the disgust signal tracking a genuine harm or a category-error? |
|      | Disgust evolved to protect against pathogens and incest; check whether the      |
|      | object actually fits.                                                            |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
Moral Foundations Disgust Probe Worksheet

WHAT TRIGGERED THE REACTION (specific, concrete):
________________________________________________________________

FELT-CHARACTER (weight 1-5 each):
   Disgust  (gross / contaminating / curled lip)         ___
   Contempt (looking-down / dismissive / superior)       ___
   Anger    (provoked / wanting confrontation / heat)    ___
   Fear     (avoidant / wanting distance / chest-tight)  ___

DOMINANT FELT-SIGNAL: ___________________

LIKELY MORAL FOUNDATION (Haidt):
   [ ] Care / Harm                  (anger flavor)
   [ ] Fairness / Cheating          (anger flavor)
   [ ] Loyalty / Betrayal           (contempt flavor)
   [ ] Authority / Subversion       (contempt flavor)
   [ ] Sanctity / Degradation       (disgust flavor)
   [ ] Liberty / Oppression         (anger or contempt)

MORAL CLAIM (what specifically you say is wrong):
________________________________________________________________

CROSS-FOUNDATION TEST:
   Could a person using a DIFFERENT foundation arrive at the
   opposite conclusion in good faith?  [ ] Yes  [ ] No

   If yes, what foundation would they use, and what would they say?
   ____________________________________________________________

CALIBRATION CHECK (especially for disgust):
   Is this signal tracking:
   [ ] A genuine harm (consistent with the foundation's design)
   [ ] An unfamiliar / non-harmful difference (category error)
   [ ] Unclear — needs more thought

NET: Does the reaction survive examination, weaken, or strengthen?
________________________________________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** (1) **The probe doesn't require you to change your view.** Naming the foundation isn't an argument that the foundation is invalid. Some sanctity violations are genuine harms; some aren't. The probe just makes the structure visible. (2) **Disgust is hard to argue with cognitively.** If you find your disgust survives a careful calibration check, that's a data point — not a flaw. (3) **In groups, the probe surfaces hidden cross-foundation arguments.** A group disagreeing about a policy often has one faction reasoning from Fairness and another from Sanctity. Naming the foundations explicitly is the first step toward useful dialogue.

## Related Tools and Frameworks

- **Affect Labeling** — labels the emotion before this probe diagnoses its moral grammar.
- **Lazarus Appraisal Framework** — appraisal-level cousin; this probe is the moral-foundation lens specifically.
- **Veil of Ignorance** — orthogonal moral analysis lens; pairs well when you want to add a fairness-test to a disgust-driven judgment.
- **Cognitive Bias Checklists** — adjacent debiasing tool; this probe is a *moral* debiasing variant.
