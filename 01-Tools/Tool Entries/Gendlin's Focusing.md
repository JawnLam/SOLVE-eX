# Gendlin's Focusing

**One-line summary:** A six-step protocol for accessing the *felt sense* — a vague but specific bodily-knowing of a situation that carries meaning the mind hasn't yet articulated — and letting it speak in its own terms.

**When to reach for it:** Decisions where rational analysis has stalled, complex situations that "don't sit right" without your knowing why, creative blocks, therapeutic processing of difficult experience, or as a regular practice for staying in contact with what you actually know.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Eugene Gendlin's research on psychotherapy outcomes found that successful clients shared a behavior: they would pause, attend inward, and speak from a vague, bodily, partly-formed sense — not from concepts. The unsuccessful clients spoke fluently from concepts but never paused into the inarticulate. Focusing is the protocol Gendlin abstracted from the successful clients' move and taught as a learnable skill.

The non-obvious operational insight is the *felt sense* — distinct from emotion (anger, sadness), distinct from thought (ideas, judgments), and distinct from physical sensation (ache, warmth). It's the *whole situation* held in the body in a vague-yet-specific way: the felt-quality of "this project", "my relationship with X", "what's wrong with this draft". When you attend to it patiently, it can shift; when it shifts, what felt stuck becomes unstuck — usually accompanied by a small bodily release.

The six-step structure is teachable in a few hours but takes years to refine. Today Focusing has trainers in 50+ countries; applications include therapy, decision-making, creative work, conflict resolution, and as a meditative-attentional practice in its own right.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes Focusing addresses where conceptual reasoning fails:

1. **Stuck-decision paralysis.** Some decisions resist analysis because the relevant information isn't fully articulated yet. Focusing accesses the not-yet-articulated.
2. **Premature articulation.** Quickly putting a complex experience into words ("I'm angry because…") often forecloses the more accurate, more nuanced thing that was about to come. Focusing teaches patience with the inarticulate.
3. **Body-mind dissociation.** Knowledge work can drift entirely cognitive; the body's signals (subtle tension, vague unease) get ignored. Focusing rebuilds the channel.

For consulting and decision work, the practice yields decisions that "sit right" — that integrate analytical findings with the gestalt the body has tracked all along. It also, less measured but consistently noted, makes practitioners better at sensing what their clients actually mean (vs. what they explicitly say).

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | CLEARING A SPACE — Notice what's on your mind. List each concern; mentally       |
|      | "set it aside," not solving it, just acknowledging. Continue until something    |
|      | space-like opens.                                                               |
|    2 | FELT SENSE — Pick one situation/concern. Attend to how the WHOLE of it sits     |
|      | in the body — usually somewhere torso-level. Don't think about it; sense it.    |
|    3 | HANDLE — Find a word, phrase, or image that captures the quality of the         |
|      | felt sense ("heavy", "bunched-up", "trying to peer over a wall").               |
|    4 | RESONATING — Hold the handle and the felt sense together. Does the handle       |
|      | match? If not quite, let a better one come. Iterate until "yes, that's it."    |
|    5 | ASKING — Ask the felt sense an open question: "What is this all about?"        |
|      | "What's the worst of it?" "What does this need?" Wait, don't think.            |
|    6 | RECEIVING — When something comes (a phrase, image, or shift), receive it        |
|      | without analyzing. Acknowledge it. Often a small bodily release accompanies.    |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
SIX-STEP FOCUSING WORKSHEET

    Posture:     □ seated, feet on floor, comfortable but alert
                 □ minimal external distraction, ~20–30 minutes
                 □ pen / paper nearby (don't analyze, just note)

    STEP 1 — CLEARING A SPACE
      "What's between me and feeling fine right now?"
      List concerns, set each aside (gently, not dismissively):
        ___________________________________________________________
        ___________________________________________________________
        ___________________________________________________________
      Pause when something more spacious opens.

    STEP 2 — FELT SENSE
      Pick one concern: ____________________________________________
      Where in the body does this concern register?
        □ throat   □ chest   □ stomach   □ shoulders   □ everywhere
      How does the whole of it FEEL?  (vague is fine; don't force precision)
        ___________________________________________________________

    STEP 3 — HANDLE
      A word, phrase, or image that captures the quality:
        ___________________________________________________________

    STEP 4 — RESONATING
      Hold handle + felt sense. Does it fit?  □ yes  □ not quite
      If not quite, let a better handle come: ____________________________
      Iterate until: "Yes, that's it."

    STEP 5 — ASKING
      Ask the felt sense (silently, then wait):
        □ "What is this all about?"
        □ "What's the worst of it?"
        □ "What does this need?"
        □ "What would feel right here?"

      Wait without filling the silence. The mind will offer thoughts; let them
      pass. The felt sense's answer often comes 30–90 seconds later, sometimes
      as a phrase, sometimes as an image, sometimes as a felt shift.

    STEP 6 — RECEIVING
      Whatever came, receive it. Don't analyze. Don't argue.
      If it shifted — note where in the body the shift was.
      Acknowledge: "I see you. Thank you."

    POST-SESSION NOTE (5–10 min later, after returning to ordinary mind)
      Handle that fit:           ____________________________________
      What came in step 5:        ____________________________________
      Bodily shift, if any:       ____________________________________
      What I now know that I didn't before:  ___________________________

COMMON PITFALLS

    ✗  Treating felt sense as emotion or sensation (it's neither — it's holistic)
    ✗  Filling the wait with thinking (the answer comes from below thinking)
    ✗  Forcing a handle when one isn't ready (better to wait or use placeholder)
    ✗  Analyzing what came in step 6 instead of receiving (kills the process)
    ✗  Using Focusing to override what the felt sense says ("but I've decided to
        do it anyway") — the practice depends on respecting the felt sense's report

WHEN TO USE THE COMPLETE 6 vs. ABBREVIATED FORMS

    Full 6 steps:    when stuck, before major decisions, in therapy contexts
    Abbreviated:     daily check-in (steps 2–4 only), 2–3 minutes
    Partner Focusing: take turns, listener reflects without advising — deepens practice
```

> **Operational notes:** Three disciplines. (1) Patience with the inarticulate is the whole skill. The instinct, especially for trained analytical thinkers, is to leap to interpretation — "this must be about my fear of X." Focusing requires sitting with the *not-yet-named* until a name actually comes from the felt sense rather than from the mind. The tell: a name from the mind feels neutral; a name from the felt sense produces a small "yes, that's it" — sometimes accompanied by physical relaxation. (2) Don't argue with what comes. The receiving phase is non-judgmental; the felt sense reports what it reports, and arguing ("but I shouldn't feel that") drives the practice underground. (3) Use a partner when possible. Partner Focusing — one person Focuses, the other reflects without advising — accelerates skill development markedly. The reflector's discipline is to mirror what the Focuser said (in their own words) rather than interpret. Fourth: Focusing complements analysis; it doesn't replace it. The decision-quality move is to do analysis, then Focus on the proposed conclusion. If the felt sense disagrees, that's important information — usually pointing to a consideration the analysis missed.
