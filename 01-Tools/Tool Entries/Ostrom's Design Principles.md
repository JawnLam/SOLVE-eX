# Ostrom's Design Principles

**One-line summary:** Eight design principles — clear boundaries, congruent rules, collective-choice arrangements, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, external recognition, and nested enterprises — that empirically distinguish long-lasting commons-governance institutions from failed ones.

**When to reach for it:** Designing or reforming any shared-resource governance system (organizational shared services, environmental commons, intellectual commons, community resources), diagnosing why an existing commons institution is failing, evaluating proposed governance changes, and any case where the question is "how do we get people to share this without it collapsing?"

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) overturned Garrett Hardin's claim that commons require either privatization or state control. Through comparative empirical study of dozens of long-lasting commons-governance institutions — Spanish irrigation systems, Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese fisheries, Maine lobster grounds, Philippine zanjeras, Nepalese forests — Ostrom identified eight **design principles** that distinguished institutions that sustained their resources for generations from institutions that failed.

The eight principles:

1. **Clearly defined boundaries** — both the resource and the user community have boundaries that are clear and understood by all
2. **Congruence** — rules about appropriation and provision are matched to local conditions
3. **Collective-choice arrangements** — most users participate in modifying the rules
4. **Monitoring** — appropriators or their accountable agents actively monitor conditions and behavior
5. **Graduated sanctions** — rule-breakers face penalties proportional to the offense, escalating with repeated violations
6. **Conflict-resolution mechanisms** — low-cost local arenas exist for resolving disputes
7. **External recognition** — outside authorities (governments, courts) recognize the community's right to organize
8. **Nested enterprises** (for large systems) — governance is layered, with smaller units handling local issues and larger units handling cross-unit concerns

The non-obvious operational insight is that **commons governance is not just rule-design; it's the entire institutional ecology.** A community can have excellent rules and still fail if monitoring is weak. Strong monitoring without graduated sanctions produces resentment without behavior change. Sanctions without conflict resolution produce escalation. The eight principles work as a system; weakness in any one degrades the whole.

A second insight: **the principles describe what successful commons institutions have in common, not a top-down recipe for designing them.** Ostrom is explicit that institutions evolve from local conditions and cannot generally be imposed wholesale. The principles serve better as an audit checklist (does this institution have these features?) than as a design template (build these eight features and you'll succeed).

A third insight, which Ostrom developed further in later work (*Understanding Institutional Diversity*, 2005), is that **scale matters**. The eight principles were derived from small to medium-scale commons (dozens to hundreds of users). Larger commons (national parks, atmospheric commons, internet protocols) require nested governance — multiple layers of the principles operating at different scales — which is why Principle 8 was added.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "rules fix everything" assumption.** Many commons-governance reforms focus on rule design and neglect monitoring, sanctions, and conflict resolution. The result: nominally good rules with weak enforcement that drift back to tragedy. The eight principles surface the institutional infrastructure that rules need to function.
2. **The "external authority will solve it" expectation.** Imposing top-down rules without community participation (Principle 3) and without alignment to local conditions (Principle 2) typically fails. Even well-intentioned external regulation that ignores local knowledge produces noncompliance. Ostrom's work supports community-anchored rather than top-down design.
3. **The scale-blindness failure.** Designing for a single layer when the actual system requires multiple layers (Principle 8) produces governance that succeeds locally but fails at higher scales, or vice versa. Recognizing the scale problem opens up nested-enterprise design.

For consulting and organizational work, Ostrom's framework is also a powerful tool for designing internal shared-resource governance (shared platforms, common code repositories, shared budgets, internal "marketplaces"). The same principles that work for irrigation systems work for engineering platform teams.

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE EIGHT-PRINCIPLE AUDIT CHECKLIST

   For an existing or proposed commons institution:

   1. CLEAR BOUNDARIES
      [ ] Resource boundary clearly defined
      [ ] User community clearly defined
      [ ] Both are known and accepted by users

   2. CONGRUENCE
      [ ] Rules about use match the resource's biological / physical
          characteristics
      [ ] Rules about use match local conditions and customs
      [ ] Costs of providing the resource match benefits to users

   3. COLLECTIVE-CHOICE ARRANGEMENTS
      [ ] Most users can participate in modifying the rules
      [ ] Rule changes go through a recognized process
      [ ] Users have voice without needing external mediation

   4. MONITORING
      [ ] Conditions of the resource are monitored
      [ ] User behavior is monitored
      [ ] Monitors are accountable to users (not to outside authority alone)

   5. GRADUATED SANCTIONS
      [ ] Rule violations result in penalties
      [ ] Penalties are proportional to violation severity
      [ ] Penalties escalate for repeat offenders
      [ ] Sanctions are credible — actually applied, not just threatened

   6. CONFLICT-RESOLUTION MECHANISMS
      [ ] A local, low-cost forum exists for disputes
      [ ] Users have access without major resource expenditure
      [ ] Resolution is generally accepted as fair

   7. EXTERNAL RECOGNITION
      [ ] Outside authorities (state, courts) recognize the community's
          right to govern itself
      [ ] External authorities don't actively undermine local rules
      [ ] Where conflict exists, hierarchy of authority is clear

   8. NESTED ENTERPRISES (for large systems)
      [ ] Local governance handles local issues
      [ ] Larger units handle cross-local issues
      [ ] Linkages between layers are functional

   Score each principle 1-5. The lowest score is your leverage point.

THE WEAKEST-PRINCIPLE DIAGNOSIS

   Score interpretation:
       1 — absent or fundamentally broken
       2 — present but weak
       3 — adequate
       4 — strong
       5 — exemplary

   Where lowest score is BOUNDARIES:
       Clarify membership and resource extent before any other reform.

   Where lowest score is CONGRUENCE:
       Rules don't fit local conditions; consult users on what would.

   Where lowest score is COLLECTIVE-CHOICE:
       Users feel rules are imposed; build participation mechanism.

   Where lowest score is MONITORING:
       Most common failure point; strong rules + weak monitoring → tragedy.

   Where lowest score is GRADUATED SANCTIONS:
       Either no penalties (free-rider) or excessive penalties (resentment).
       Proportionality is the key.

   Where lowest score is CONFLICT RESOLUTION:
       Disputes escalate or fester; build local forum.

   Where lowest score is EXTERNAL RECOGNITION:
       External authority actively undermines or fails to support local
       rules; political work needed.

   Where lowest score is NESTED ENTERPRISES:
       Single-layer governance failing at scale; redesign as nested.

THE INSTITUTIONAL-DESIGN MATRIX (when building new)

                     Small scale  Medium scale  Large scale
   Boundaries         informal     formal       legally defined
   Congruence         folk rules   adapted      multi-level
   Collective-choice  consensus    representative voting
   Monitoring         peer         designated   professional
   Sanctions          social       fines        legal
   Conflict res.      mediation    arbitration  courts
   External recog.    local-only   recognized   constitutional
   Nesting            n/a          two-level    multi-level

   Use this matrix to calibrate the principles to scale. Many commons
   failures come from applying small-scale forms (peer monitoring,
   social sanction) at medium or large scale where they don't work.

THE ORGANIZATIONAL-COMMONS TRANSLATION

   Internal shared resource governance — shared codebase, shared
   budget pool, shared platform, internal services:

   Boundaries:        who can access, what counts as the resource
   Congruence:        rules fit how teams actually work
   Collective-choice: contributing teams have voice in governance
   Monitoring:        usage tracked; reviews scheduled
   Sanctions:         escalating consequences for misuse (warnings,
                      access reduction, etc.)
   Conflict res.:     designated arbiter team or process
   External recog.:   senior leadership backs the governance model
   Nesting:           team-level + business-unit-level + corporate-
                      level oversight

   Same eight principles, different vocabulary. The pattern translates
   directly.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES (what goes wrong)

   1. Strong rules, weak monitoring → drift to tragedy
   2. Strong monitoring, weak sanctions → enforcement theater
   3. External imposition without collective-choice → noncompliance
   4. Boundary ambiguity → conflict over who's a member
   5. Rules that don't fit local conditions → workarounds and erosion
   6. Single-scale governance for multi-scale problem → fails at edges
   7. External authorities undermining local rules → erosion of
      institutional legitimacy
   8. Conflict-resolution failure → escalation outside the system

   Most failed commons institutions exhibit 2-3 of these. Diagnose
   first; intervention follows.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) The eight principles work as a system. Strengthening one without addressing the others produces partial improvement at best. The audit reveals which is weakest; that's where intervention has the most leverage. (2) Ostrom's framework is descriptive of what successful institutions have, not a recipe for building them. Institutions evolve from local conditions; the principles serve better as audit and diagnosis than as top-down design. Where new design is required, treat the principles as constraints to satisfy, not as features to specify. (3) Scale matters. Small-commons forms (peer monitoring, social sanction) don't scale. Medium and large commons need formal monitoring, graduated sanctions with credible enforcement, and nested governance. Many commons failures are scale mismatches in disguise. (4) The framework translates to organizational commons. Internal shared resources (codebases, budget pools, platforms, services) follow the same dynamics. Apply the audit, find the weakest principle, strengthen it. The principles are domain-general because the underlying collective-action structure is.
