# Jobs-to-Be-Done

**One-line summary:** A reframing of customer behavior — popularized by Clayton Christensen — that treats customers as "hiring" products to do specific jobs (functional, emotional, social) rather than buying products for their features, redefining competitive landscape and innovation focus around job-completion.

**When to reach for it:** Innovation strategy (what should we build next?); product positioning (what job is this product hired for?); competitive analysis (what else gets hired for the same job?); marketing message development (speak to the job, not the features); customer interview design; and any context where understanding *why* customers buy reveals more than understanding *what* they buy.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD)** reframes customer behavior. The structure:

1. **Customers have jobs** — circumstances in which they need progress on something. Jobs are stable; the products that satisfy them change.
2. **Customers "hire" products** to do these jobs. The hiring is motivated by the job, not by the product features.
3. **Jobs have three dimensions:**
   - **Functional** — the practical task to be done
   - **Emotional** — how the customer wants to feel
   - **Social** — how the customer wants to be perceived
4. **Competition is anything else hired for the same job**, not just same-category products.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **the job, not the customer demographic, is the right unit of analysis.** A "millennial mom" doesn't have predictable purchasing behavior; the same person hires different products for different jobs (quick weekday lunch ≠ celebratory weekend dinner ≠ healthy meal-prep). Demographics describe customers; jobs describe purchase moments. Strategy framed by jobs identifies competition and innovation opportunities that demographics miss.

The classic example: Christensen studied a fast-food chain wanting to sell more milkshakes. They asked who buys milkshakes (demographics) and got mediocre data. They reframed: when does someone buy a milkshake? It turned out a large segment was bought in the morning, by drivers facing a long boring commute. The "job" was: keep me occupied through a 30-minute drive, fill me up until lunch, don't make me messy. Competition wasn't other milkshakes; it was bagels, donuts, or coffee. Improving the milkshake to do the job better (thicker so it lasts; chunks of fruit for variety; fits in cup holder) would win.

A second insight: **most innovation focuses on the wrong axis.** Adding features is product-focused; understanding the job better is customer-focused. Many features fail because they don't help the job; many simple products win because they nail the job.

A third insight: **the same customer hires different products for different jobs.** Same person buys McDonald's at noon (job: quick fuel) and a fancy meal at night (job: relax + connect). Treating the customer as a stable preference profile misses this.

A fourth insight: **the framework is methodologically demanding.** True JTBD discovery requires careful interviews probing the circumstances of purchase, the emotional/social dimensions, and competing alternatives the customer considered. Surface-level "what are you trying to do?" doesn't surface jobs.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "feature-list innovation" trap.** Adding capabilities without understanding which jobs benefit. Many products fail this way.
2. **The "demographic targeting" miss.** Marketing to "millennial moms" assumes stable preferences within a demographic; jobs framework reveals purchase-moment variability.
3. **The "wrong competitor" frame.** Competing only with same-category products misses the substitutes that actually steal customers (Netflix vs. board games, not Netflix vs. cable).

For product strategists, marketers, innovators, and anyone trying to understand why customers buy, JTBD is foundational reframe.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Choose a customer / purchase moment to study. Recent purchasers of your        |
|      | product or alternatives.                                                          |
|    2 | Interview deeply. What was happening when you decided to buy? What were you    |
|      | trying to accomplish? What else did you consider?                                |
|    3 | Identify the functional job. The practical task being accomplished.            |
|    4 | Identify the emotional job. How did the customer want to feel?                  |
|    5 | Identify the social job. How did the customer want to be perceived (by self,   |
|      | by others)?                                                                       |
|    6 | Map the competition by job. What else gets hired for the same job?              |
|    7 | Identify job-completion gaps. Where is the current product underperforming on  |
|      | the job?                                                                          |
|    8 | Translate to product / strategy decisions. What to build, message, position    |
|      | around the job?                                                                  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE JOBS-TO-BE-DONE FRAME

   STANDARD FRAME:
       Customer (demographic) buys product (features)
       Compete with same-category products
       Innovate by adding features

   JTBD FRAME:
       Customer (in a circumstance) hires product (for a job)
       Compete with anything else hired for the same job
       Innovate by improving job-completion

   The hiring metaphor: the customer "hires" the product
   to do a job; if the product does the job poorly, the
   customer "fires" it for an alternative.

THE THREE DIMENSIONS

   FUNCTIONAL JOB:
       The practical task. "Help me commute through
       traffic." "Help me close my client deal."

   EMOTIONAL JOB:
       How the customer wants to feel.
       "Feel competent / confident / safe / relaxed /
       in control."

   SOCIAL JOB:
       How the customer wants to be perceived (by self
       or others).
       "Be seen as a savvy shopper, a good parent, a
       sophisticated cook."

   Most products / services serve all three; great
   products serve them all well.

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — MILKSHAKE STUDY

   Customer: morning commuter buying milkshake.

   Functional: fill me up; occupy 30-min drive; don't
   make me messy.

   Emotional: comfort; small reward in a boring drive.

   Social: minimal — solitary moment.

   Competition for this job:
       Bagel (messy crumbs; eaten in <5 min)
       Donut (sticky fingers; gone fast)
       Coffee (no fullness)
       Yogurt (spoon required)
       Banana (too quick to eat; not satisfying)

   Each alternative does the job partially. The
   milkshake's strengths: long-lasting, no mess, hand-
   held, filling. Improving along the job axes (thicker,
   fruit chunks for variety, fits cup holder) wins
   share.

   Without the JTBD frame, the firm would have asked
   "what flavor do customers want?" — wrong question
   for this purchase moment.

THE COMPETITION-MAPPING-BY-JOB

   List anything else hired for the same job:

   Job: "Connect emotionally with my friends despite
   distance."
   Competitors: phone calls, text, Zoom, Instagram,
   long letters, sending physical gifts, in-person
   travel.
   The product (whatever your firm makes) competes with
   all of these.

   Job: "Look professional in remote meetings."
   Competitors: better camera, better lighting, virtual
   backgrounds, dressing up, choosing a quiet room.

   The job-defined competition often surprises the firm.

THE OUTCOME-DRIVEN INNOVATION VARIANT

   Tony Ulwick's structured method:

   1. Decompose the job into outcomes (specific
      sub-results).
      "Help me commute" decomposes into:
          - Stay alert
          - Stay occupied
          - Avoid hunger / thirst
          - Don't get messy
          - Manage cost

   2. Survey customers on importance and current
      satisfaction with each outcome.

   3. Identify outcomes with high importance + low
      current satisfaction — innovation opportunities.

   4. Prioritize product development around those
      outcomes.

   This turns JTBD from interpretive into measurable.

THE EMOTIONAL-AND-SOCIAL DEPTH

   The functional job is often easy to identify; the
   emotional and social jobs are usually where
   differentiation lives.

   "Why do people buy luxury cars?"
       Functional: transport (any car does this)
       Emotional: feel successful, in control, treated
       well
       Social: signal achievement, taste

   The luxury-car maker competing on functional alone
   loses to commodity cars; competing on emotional
   and social wins.

   "Why do people use Slack vs. email?"
       Functional: team communication
       Emotional: feel connected, less overwhelmed
       Social: be the kind of company that uses modern
       tools

   Slack's success isn't the chat features; it's the
   emotional-social bundle.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. STOPPING AT FUNCTIONAL
        Identifying only the practical task. Recovery:
        probe emotional and social.

   2. CONFLATING DEMOGRAPHIC WITH JOB
        "Millennials want X." Recovery: same person
        has different jobs in different moments;
        unbundle.

   3. WRONG COMPETITOR FRAME
        Comparing only to same-category products.
        Recovery: list everything else hired for the
        same job.

   4. RATIONALIZED INTERVIEWS
        Customers post-rationalize purchases. Recovery:
        ask about specific past purchases, not abstract
        preferences.

   5. PRODUCT-FIRST FRAMING
        "How do we sell more X?" — wrong question.
        Recovery: "What job is X hired for, and how
        well does X do it?"

   6. JOB INFLATION
        Treating every product feature as its own job.
        Recovery: jobs are at the level of progress
        the customer is making in their life, not at
        the level of feature use.

THE OPERATIONAL TEMPLATE

   Customer + purchase moment: _______________________

   Functional job: ___________________________________
   Emotional job: ____________________________________
   Social job: _______________________________________

   Competition (anything hired for the same job):
       _________________________________________________
       _________________________________________________

   Job-completion assessment:
       Where does our product win? __________________
       Where does it lose? __________________________
       Gap to fill: _________________________________

   Strategic implications:
       Product changes: ____________________________
       Messaging: __________________________________
       Positioning: ________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Don't stop at functional. The emotional and social dimensions are usually where differentiation lives; functional jobs are often commodity. The full picture requires probing all three. (2) Frame competition by job, not category. Same-category competitors are visible; substitutes hired for the same job are often the bigger threat. (3) Customer-interview rigor matters. JTBD discovery isn't satisfied by abstract "what do you want?" questions. Probe specific past purchases — what was happening, what alternatives were considered, what made the choice. (4) Job is unit of analysis, not customer. Same person has different jobs in different moments. Strategy keyed to demographic stability misses purchase-moment variability.
