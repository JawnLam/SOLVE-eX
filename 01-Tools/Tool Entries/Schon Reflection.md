# Schön Reflection-on-Action / Reflection-in-Action

**One-line summary:** A two-mode reflective practice — retrospective reflection-on-action and real-time reflection-in-action — that builds the practitioner's capacity to "read" situations and respond fluently from a developing repertoire, distinct from applying pre-existing theory.

**When to reach for it:** Professional practice contexts (design, teaching, clinical, planning, coaching) where situations rarely fit textbook cases; building reflective capacity in apprentices or supervisees; personal practice where you want to learn from experience more reliably than rumination provides.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Schön's 1983 critique of "technical rationality" — the model that professionals apply science / theory to practice — was foundational. His counter-claim: skilled practitioners *don't* operate by applying theory. They read each situation as a kind of case, draw on a felt-repertoire of similar prior cases, and respond fluently, often improvisationally. The expertise is in the case-reading and repertoire-building, not in theoretical knowledge.

Two modes:

1. **Reflection-on-action** — retrospective. After the situation, examine: What did I do? What was I responding to? What went well? What didn't? What does this case add to my repertoire?
2. **Reflection-in-action** — during. While the situation is unfolding, hold the practice in awareness: "What's this situation reminding me of? What's surprising here? Is the move I'm making right?" This is the harder mode; it requires capacity to be both in the action and watching the action.

The non-obvious operational insight: **the repertoire is the locus of expertise.** A reflective practitioner accumulates cases (this design problem solved by this move; this patient presented with these symptoms; this student struggled in this way) into a *felt* library. New situations are recognized as similar-to-cases-in-the-library, which guides response. The library is built through reflection-on-action.

A second insight: **reflection-in-action is what distinguishes virtuoso practice from competent practice.** The competent practitioner reflects after; the virtuoso reflects during, allowing real-time mid-course correction. This capacity is built by deliberate reflection-on-action over years.

A third insight: **Schön rejects technical rationality without rejecting expertise.** His framework is not anti-theory; it locates the bulk of professional expertise in something other than theory — in case-recognition, repertoire, and reflective capacity. Theory is one input; the felt-repertoire is the proximate decision-maker.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Theory-application mode.** Trying to deduce the right move from principles. Most situations are too complex for this; the felt-recognition of similar-cases is faster and more reliable.
2. **Experience-without-reflection.** Time-on-task that doesn't accumulate to expertise because the practitioner doesn't extract cases from experience.
3. **Reactive practice.** Acting without watching the action; never building reflection-in-action capacity. Many experienced practitioners are reactive-experts, not reflective-experts.

The framework is foundational in design education, professional education (medicine, teaching, social work), and adult learning. Schön taught at MIT and the framework is embedded in design and architecture curricula globally.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|  1   | After significant practice events, reflect ON action: write or speak through    |
|      | what happened, what you did, what you noticed, what you'd do differently.       |
|      | Add to your case library.                                                        |
|  2   | During practice, build reflection-in-action capacity: small pauses to notice   |
|      | "what is this situation reminding me of?" / "is my current move working?"      |
|      | Start small; capacity builds slowly.                                            |
|  3   | When stuck mid-practice, deliberately invoke a case from your repertoire:      |
|      | "Have I seen something like this before? What was the move there?"             |
|  4   | Notice surprise. When something doesn't go as expected, that's case-material — |
|      | examine the surprise carefully in reflection-on-action.                         |
|  5   | Distinguish theory-knowledge from repertoire-knowledge. Theory is one input;  |
|      | repertoire is what proximally guides the move.                                  |
|  6   | Share repertoire with apprentices via Cognitive Apprenticeship / Modeling — make|
|      | the case-library visible by narrating the cases.                                |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
Reflective Practice Journal Template

REFLECTION-ON-ACTION (post-event, 15-30 min)

DATE / EVENT:        ___________________________________

THE SITUATION (what was the case):
   _____________________________________________________

WHAT I DID:
   _____________________________________________________

WHAT I WAS RESPONDING TO (the situation's salient features):
   _____________________________________________________

WHAT SURPRISED ME:
   _____________________________________________________
   (surprise is the highest-value reflection material)

WHAT WORKED:
   _____________________________________________________

WHAT DIDN'T:
   _____________________________________________________

WHAT THIS CASE ADDS TO MY REPERTOIRE:
   A pattern recognized: ___________________________
   A move I'll keep:       ___________________________
   A move I'll abandon:    ___________________________
   A new question to track: __________________________

REPERTOIRE INDEX (cases tagged for retrieval):
   This case ↔ similar to prior case: _______________

------------------------------------------------------------

REFLECTION-IN-ACTION (during practice — for capacity-building)

MICRO-PAUSE PROMPTS (use during practice):
   - "What is this situation reminding me of?"
   - "Is my current move working?"
   - "What's surprising here?"
   - "If I were observing me, what would I notice?"

CAPACITY ASSESSMENT (weekly):
   How often did I successfully reflect-in-action this week?
   What helped? What got in the way?
```

> **Operational notes:** (1) **Reflection-on-action is the foundation.** Reflection-in-action capacity grows from years of reflection-on-action practice; don't expect to skip ahead. (2) **Surprise is the most valuable signal.** Cases that go as expected don't add much to the repertoire; cases that surprise are where new patterns emerge. (3) **Watch for "rumination" mislabeled as reflection.** Rumination loops; reflection structures and extracts. The journal template forces structure. (4) **Make the repertoire shareable.** Senior practitioners' biggest contribution is often externalizing their case library for the next generation (pairs with Cognitive Apprenticeship's Modeling step).

## Related Tools and Frameworks

- **After Action Review** — operational cousin; Schön is broader and more reflective, AAR is tighter and more action-oriented.
- **Double-Loop Learning** — adjacent reflective-learning framework (Argyris); Schön's reflection often produces double-loop insights.
- **Cognitive Apprenticeship Model** — pairs as the pedagogical instantiation; reflection-on-action externalizes the case library for apprentices.
