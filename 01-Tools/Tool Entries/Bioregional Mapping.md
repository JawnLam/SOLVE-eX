# Bioregional Mapping

**One-line summary:** A place-based inquiry protocol that re-orients spatial thinking around ecological geography (watersheds, ecoregions, soils, species, indigenous territories) rather than political-administrative boundaries — surfacing patterns of relation and dependency that political maps systematically obscure.

**When to reach for it:** Environmental, agricultural, or land-use decisions where political boundaries don't track ecological reality; community-organizing in contexts where bioregional identity is more meaningful than city/county; indigenous land-stewardship contexts; sustainability or regenerative-design strategy.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Peter Berg and Raymond Dasmann's foundational claim (1977): **the map you use shapes the decisions you make.** Political-administrative maps (states, counties, ZIP codes) are necessary for government but actively obscure ecological structure — they cut through watersheds, group disconnected ecosystems, and erase indigenous territory. Bioregional mapping re-foregrounds the living substrate.

Key bioregional features to map:

- **Watershed.** Where does water collect, flow, and exit? Most ecological structure organizes around watersheds.
- **Ecoregion.** Major ecological communities (e.g., Bailey's ecoregions, WWF's terrestrial ecoregions).
- **Soil type.** Foundational for what grows; rarely visible on political maps.
- **Climate zone.** Precipitation, temperature range, frost dates.
- **Indigenous territory.** Whose ancestral land is this? Most North American boundaries pre-date contact and persist as living political/cultural geography.
- **Species range.** Keystone and indicator species; corridors.
- **Human use patterns.** Traditional foodways, traditional medicine plants, traditional building materials.

The non-obvious operational insight: **the political-map-as-default is a learned habit.** Most planning happens at the level of "this city" or "this county," yet the ecological reality of "this watershed" or "this floodplain" is more relevant to decisions about water, agriculture, energy, fire, drought, species, and long-term habitability. The mapping practice retrains the planning unit.

A second insight: **bioregional maps support multi-generational thinking** (pairs naturally with Seven Generations Thinking). Watersheds and soils persist across the political turnover of any administration; decisions framed around them inherit longer time-horizons by default.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the protocol prevents:

1. **Political-boundary planning for ecological problems.** Water rights, fire management, species conservation all fail when planned on county/state lines.
2. **Erasure of indigenous geography.** Indigenous territories persist as living governance and cultural reality; political-only maps render them invisible.
3. **Decoupling from the substrate.** Decisions that don't reference soil/water/climate are decoupled from the conditions of long-term habitability. Bioregional mapping anchors decisions in the substrate.

The framework's adoption has grown in regenerative-agriculture circles (Permaculture, ecological restoration), indigenous-led conservation (e.g., Innu Nation land-management plans), and some post-political-geography governance experiments (the Cascadia Department of Bioregion).

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|  1   | Identify the bioregion. Most often the watershed. If unsure, USGS watershed     |
|      | boundary tools or equivalent give the major / minor watershed hierarchy.        |
|  2   | Layer the map. Each layer = one bioregional feature: watershed, ecoregion,      |
|      | soil, climate, indigenous territory, species ranges, traditional human use.    |
|  3   | Find the convergences. Where do multiple layers align? These are the strongest |
|      | bioregional boundaries.                                                          |
|  4   | Re-frame the decision in bioregional terms. Replace "this county" with "this   |
|      | sub-watershed of the X River system."                                            |
|  5   | Engage indigenous stewards if they exist for this territory. Their living      |
|      | knowledge of the bioregion is foundational; the mapping is incomplete without  |
|      | their input.                                                                     |
|  6   | Use the map for the decision. Notice what becomes visible that political maps |
|      | hid.                                                                              |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

```
   Political frame:   _______________________________

   - Patterns invisible in political maps:
     ___________________________________________
   - Decisions that need different scoping:
     ___________________________________________
   - Stakeholders not previously identified:
     ___________________________________________

INDIGENOUS ENGAGEMENT:
   Whose land is this? __________________________
   Engagement status:   __________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** (1) **The map is incomplete without indigenous-knowledge input** for any North American context. Bioregional mapping that erases indigenous geography reproduces the failure mode it's trying to address. (2) **Bioregional and political both matter** — the practice isn't to abolish political maps but to layer the bioregional view alongside. (3) **For decisions with regional scope**, bioregional re-framing is high-leverage; for highly local decisions, it may add overhead without clarity.
