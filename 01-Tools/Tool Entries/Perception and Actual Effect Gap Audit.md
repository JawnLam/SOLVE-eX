# Perception and Actual Effect Gap Audit

**Also known as:** Perception Gap Audit (field manual); the three-utilities check

**One-line summary:** A post-action diagnostic that distinguishes the three distinct quantities the master diagram calls "utility" — Expected (matrix prediction), Actual (real effect, often opaque), Perceived (what the actor believes they got) — and explicitly audits the gap between Actual and Perceived as the error term that compounds across cycles when left unchecked.

**When to reach for it:** Immediately after an action whose effect has been observed, especially when the actor reports the move worked or the move failed without naming what they would have expected or how they would know; at the boundary between any two cycles, before the resource loop and the learning loop pass updates to the next cycle's starting condition; when an actor's confidence in a recent result feels disproportionate to the available evidence; when prior cycles have produced surprises that prior post-action assessments did not flag.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Perception and Actual Effect Gap Audit** is the discipline that prevents the framework's most consequential silent failure: the actor carrying an undetected perception gap into the next cycle. The audit names three distinct quantities (Field Manual p. 18):

- **Expected utility** — what the matrix predicted before action. The U(PP) number for the option selected. Exists only in the actor's head, only before action.
- **Actual utility** — the real value reality delivered, measured against the original target. Not the actor's belief about the value; it is the value. Frequently opaque to the actor.
- **Perceived utility** — what the actor believes they got. Actual utility filtered through hope, defensiveness, ego, prior belief.

The framework's central claim about why these diverge is structural (Field Manual p. 18). Expected and actual diverge because reality has its own constraints and other actors are running their own moves. Actual and perceived diverge because the actor is human — prone to inflation when winning, deflation when losing, denial in either direction.

The non-obvious quantity is the **perception gap** itself — the distance between Actual and Perceived. The dissertation's framing: "the actual effect on a component of the target's decision matrix is often opaque to the actor" (Lam 2020 p. 156). The field manual sharpens this to a diagnostic claim: "An actor who systematically over-perceives their wins will steadily overinvest in actions whose actual utility is lower than they think. An actor who systematically under-perceives their losses will fail to update interests when an update is overdue. The perception gap is the framework's quiet but central claim: most strategic mistakes are not failures of analysis but failures of perception" (Field Manual p. 19).

A second insight: **perception gaps compound across cycles.** "A small inflation in Cycle 1 (over-reading a partner's warmth) produces a larger inflation in Cycle 2 (over-scoring the probability of concessions). The cure is to ask, at every cycle boundary: what is the gap between what I think I got and what I actually got?" (Field Manual p. 31, Cathna Lessons.)

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **Three-utilities collapse.** Treating "the move worked" as a single quantity that does not distinguish what was predicted, what reality delivered, and what the actor believes erases the gap that drives downstream error.
2. **Self-reported success.** "I got what I wanted" without external triangulation is perceived utility, not actual utility. If the actor never asks how they would know, they cannot detect when perceived diverges from actual.
3. **Cycle-boundary skip.** The resource loop and the learning loop pass updates from this cycle to the next. If the perception gap is not audited before that pass, the gap propagates into the next cycle's starting condition.

For executives, founders, and any actor running iterated decision cycles, the audit is the discipline that makes the next cycle better than the last instead of worse.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

The audit runs in five short questions. It works best when the user is asked to write each answer before the next question is read.

1. **What did the matrix predict?** Recover Expected utility — the U(PP) number for the option selected, the success criterion stated before action.
2. **What actually happened?** Describe the actual effect — the observable, externally-verifiable consequence of the action. Where possible, name a piece of evidence external to the actor's own report (a meeting decision, a written outcome, a counterparty's behavior).
3. **What do you believe you got?** Surface perceived utility — the actor's current internal assessment of the move. This is the answer they would give if asked "how did it go?"
4. **Compare Actual and Perceived.** Is there a gap? In which direction? Inflation (perceived > actual) is the common failure when the move was framed as a win; deflation (perceived < actual) is the common failure when the move was framed as a loss.
5. **Name the gap, in one sentence, before the next cycle starts.** The naming is what prevents propagation. If the user can write the gap down, they will detect it when it compounds in cycle N+1.

A useful test: if the actor cannot describe Actual independently of Perceived — if their answer to "what actually happened" is the same as their answer to "what do you believe you got" — then external triangulation is missing and the audit has not actually run.

## Worked Example

A regional VP runs the first cycle of a pilot program proposal. Her matrix predicted U(PP) = +4 (modest workhorse). The action: a one-page proposal co-authored with a business-unit GM, an informal hallway conversation with the CEO three days before the proposal lands, a deliberate copy-in to the CTO to forestall accusations of going around him.

Running the audit:
- **Expected utility:** +4.
- **Actual utility:** the CEO approves the pilot — but with a steering committee chaired by the CTO "to ensure data integrity standards." The VP is in the room, but the gavel is not hers. Actual utility is roughly +1 (the pilot is approved, but the governance she most wanted is unfavorable).
- **Perceived utility:** closer to +3 — she over-reads her presence in the room and under-weights the chair assignment. She comes out of the meeting feeling the pilot was a clean win.
- **Gap:** Perceived − Actual = +2. The CTO chair will steadily constrain the pilot's framing across the next two cycles; the VP will be working from a starting condition more advantageous than reality supports.

The audit names the gap explicitly before Cycle 2 starts. The VP carries an explicit annotation forward: "the chair assignment will matter; do not let Cycle 2's interest assume governance neutrality." (Adapted from Field Manual ch. 7, Cresterly Cycle 1, p. 22.)

## Common Failure Modes

- **Treating "the move worked" as a single quantity.** Without distinguishing the three utilities, the gap cannot be detected. The audit collapses to perceived utility plus a self-report.
- **No external evidence for Actual.** If the actor's answer to "what actually happened" is internal — feelings, intuitions, sense of momentum — the audit is reading perceived utility twice. Real triangulation requires a counterparty's behavior, a written outcome, or another externally-verifiable signal.
- **Skipping the cycle-boundary audit.** Running the audit eventually, after several cycles, is too late. The gap compounds; auditing cycle 3's gap will not unwind cycles 1 and 2.
- **Audit collapses into rationalization.** When the actor's self-image is at stake, the audit can become a justification for the perceived utility. The discipline is to write Actual before Perceived; the order matters.
- **Asymmetric audit: only the wins.** Auditing the wins inflates confidence; auditing only the losses inflates self-criticism. The discipline is to audit every cycle's gap, regardless of which direction the cycle went.
