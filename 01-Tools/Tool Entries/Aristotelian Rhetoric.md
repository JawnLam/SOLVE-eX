# Aristotelian Rhetoric

**One-line summary:** A three-appeal model — ethos (speaker character), pathos (audience emotion), logos (argument structure) — used to design and audit persuasive communication, framed within three genres of speech and four points of stasis (dispute).

**When to reach for it:** Any persuasion task where the speaker, the audience, and the argument all matter — executive proposals, town halls, change communications, public statements during a crisis, courtroom-style argument, fundraising appeals, sales narratives.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (c. 350 BCE) gave the West its first systematic theory of persuasion, and the framework remains operationally complete. He named three modes by which a speaker moves an audience:

- **Ethos** — the speaker's perceived character, credibility, and goodwill. The audience's answer to "should I trust this person?"
- **Pathos** — the audience's emotional state. The speaker's ability to evoke, sustain, or redirect feeling. The audience's answer to "do I care?"
- **Logos** — the structure of the argument itself. Evidence, reasoning, examples, enthymemes (truncated syllogisms drawing on shared premises). The audience's answer to "does it hold together?"

The non-obvious operational insight is that the three appeals are *interdependent* and the persuader must hit a defensible level on all three. A logically airtight argument from a low-credibility speaker fails. A deeply emotional appeal without supporting reasoning collapses on reflection. A perfectly credible speaker delivering content the audience does not feel implicated by is ignored. Most persuasive failures are not "the wrong appeal" but *imbalance* — over-investing in the appeal the speaker is naturally good at and under-investing in the others.

Aristotle also distinguished three **genres** of rhetoric, each with a different time-orientation and aim:

- **Forensic / judicial** — about the past; aim is justice (did X happen? Was it just?)
- **Deliberative / political** — about the future; aim is benefit (should we do Y? Will it work?)
- **Epideictic / ceremonial** — about the present; aim is honor / blame (praise, eulogy, brand storytelling)

Confusing the genre is a common failure: a deliberative argument (we should do this) gets stuck because the audience is in forensic mode (figuring out who is to blame for the past). Naming the genre realigns the move.

Finally, **stasis theory** identifies four points of dispute: did it happen (fact), what is it (definition), how serious is it (quality), and who decides (procedure / jurisdiction). Skipping a prior stasis to argue a later one wastes the audience.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "logos trap."** Technical experts and analytical leaders default to logos — pile evidence, structure arguments, lay out reasoning — while neglecting ethos (why am I the right person to be saying this?) and pathos (why does the audience care today?). Audiences disengage well before the logic fails.
2. **Genre confusion.** A change announcement that should be deliberative (here is what we will do, here is why it benefits us) gets delivered as epideictic (praise of past performance) and lands as evasive. Or a crisis statement that should be forensic (here is what happened, here is accountability) gets delivered as deliberative (here is what we will do next) and lands as deflective.
3. **Stasis-skipping.** Arguing the merits ("we should adopt this strategy") before settling the prior question ("is this even our problem to solve?") leaves the audience back at stasis 1 (jurisdiction) while the speaker is at stasis 4 (procedure). The argument fails because the audience never agreed it had begun.

For consulting and executive communication, the Aristotelian framework is the simplest universal audit: a five-minute review of any draft (am I credible? do they care? does it hold? what genre? what stasis?) catches more persuasive failures than any single check.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the audience: who must be moved, and from what state to what state?    |
|    2 | Identify the genre: forensic (past), deliberative (future), or epideictic (now)? |
|    3 | Locate the stasis: where does the dispute actually live — fact, definition,     |
|      | quality, or jurisdiction? Resolve prior stases before arguing later ones.       |
|    4 | Audit ethos: why am I the right person to say this? What credentials, prior     |
|      | track record, signals of goodwill am I bringing?                                |
|    5 | Audit pathos: what is the audience feeling now, and what should they feel by    |
|      | the end? What concrete images, stakes, or stories will get them there?         |
|    6 | Audit logos: what is the actual argument structure — claim, evidence, warrant?  |
|      | Where are the enthymemes (premises shared with the audience but not stated)?    |
|    7 | Balance check: are all three appeals at a defensible level? Which is weakest?   |
|      | Reinforce it.                                                                   |
|    8 | Draft / deliver / iterate. After delivery, audit the failure (if any) by genre, |
|      | stasis, and appeal — usually one is the broken thing, not all three.            |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE THREE APPEALS (audit each before drafting)

   ETHOS — character, credibility, goodwill
       Why am I the right person to be saying this?
       What signals of competence am I bringing?
       What signals of integrity / goodwill toward this audience am I bringing?
       Where am I a credibility risk for this audience?

   PATHOS — audience emotion
       What is the audience FEELING right now (anxious, complacent, betrayed, hopeful)?
       What do they need to FEEL by the end (resolve, urgency, relief, conviction)?
       What concrete images / stories / stakes will move them from one to the other?
       What emotion am I evoking by accident that I do not intend?

   LOGOS — argument structure
       What is the central claim?
       What evidence supports it?
       What is the warrant (the principle that links evidence to claim)?
       Which premises am I asserting vs. assuming the audience already shares
         (the enthymemes)?
       Where is the argument weakest — and is the weak point load-bearing?

THE THREE GENRES (pick one — or know which one you are blending)

   FORENSIC / JUDICIAL — about the past
       Aim: justice. Did this happen? Was it right? Who is responsible?
       Tense: past. Audience role: judge.
       Common contexts: post-mortems, accountability statements, legal argument.

   DELIBERATIVE / POLITICAL — about the future
       Aim: benefit. Should we do this? Will it work? At what cost?
       Tense: future. Audience role: decision-maker.
       Common contexts: strategy proposals, policy debate, change announcements.

   EPIDEICTIC / CEREMONIAL — about the present
       Aim: honor / blame. What deserves praise? What deserves censure?
       Tense: present. Audience role: witness.
       Common contexts: eulogies, brand stories, vision statements, anniversaries.

   Genre-mismatch failure: delivering one genre when the audience needs another.
   Diagnostic: ask the audience what question they are trying to answer right now.

THE FOUR STASES (resolve prior ones first)

   1. STASIS OF FACT       — Did it happen? Is the situation as described?
   2. STASIS OF DEFINITION — What kind of thing is it? How is it categorized?
   3. STASIS OF QUALITY    — How serious is it? Is it justified or harmful?
   4. STASIS OF JURISDICTION — Who has the authority to decide? Is this venue
                                 the right one?

   Stasis-skipping failure: the speaker is at stasis 3 (how serious) while the
   audience is still at stasis 1 (did it even happen) or stasis 4 (is this our
   problem). The argument fails because no shared starting point exists.

   Diagnostic: ask the audience "where do you disagree?" Their answer reveals
   the live stasis.

THE FIVE CANONS (for delivery, not just design)

   INVENTION    — What to say (the appeals + the substance)
   ARRANGEMENT  — In what order
   STYLE        — In what words / tone
   MEMORY       — How to internalize for delivery
   DELIVERY     — Voice, body, pacing in the moment

   Modern practitioners often default to invention + arrangement and neglect
   style + delivery. For high-stakes communication, all five are operational.

PRE-DELIVERY AUDIT (5-minute checklist)

   [ ] Audience clearly identified (who exactly, what state they are in)
   [ ] Genre named (forensic / deliberative / epideictic)
   [ ] Stasis correctly located (and prior stases addressed or skippable)
   [ ] Ethos signals present and appropriate to audience
   [ ] Pathos arc traced (start state → end state, with means to get there)
   [ ] Logos structure laid out (claim / evidence / warrant)
   [ ] Weakest appeal identified and reinforced
   [ ] Style and delivery rehearsed for the room

POST-DELIVERY AUTOPSY (when persuasion failed)

   Did they not believe me?       → ethos failure
   Did they not care?              → pathos failure
   Did the argument not hold?      → logos failure
   Did I answer the wrong question? → genre or stasis failure
   Did the words / pacing land flat? → style or delivery failure

   Most failures are one of these — fix the broken thing, not all of them.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Default to balance, not maximization. The goal is a defensible level on all three appeals, not the highest possible logos. Imbalance is the dominant failure mode. (2) Genre and stasis are upstream of appeals — get them wrong and no amount of ethos / pathos / logos work will save the speech. Always confirm them first. (3) Enthymemes are where rhetoric meets ideology. The premises you leave unstated reveal what you assume the audience already shares. Audit them: what would happen if I had to state this premise out loud? Sometimes that is exactly what should be done. (4) The framework is 2400 years old and remains complete because the underlying problem (one mind moving another via language) has not changed. Resist the temptation to "modernize" by replacing the categories. Modern frameworks (StoryBrand, SCQA, Heath Brothers) are nearly always specializations of Aristotelian moves; if you want first-principles fluency, learn Aristotle and treat the moderns as application notes.
