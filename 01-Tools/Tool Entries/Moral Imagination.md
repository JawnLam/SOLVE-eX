# Moral Imagination

**One-line summary:** The capacity to imagine the situations of others, alternative framings, novel possibilities, and unforeseen consequences — used to expand moral perception beyond habitual patterns and to surface ethical considerations that would otherwise remain invisible.

**When to reach for it:** Decisions that look purely numerical or operational but have human consequences (layoffs, product changes, policy implementations), ethical analysis where standard frameworks feel hollow, novel situations without clear precedent, design ethics, and any moment where "I hadn't thought of it that way" would be the right response.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Mark Johnson's *Moral Imagination* (1993) developed the philosophical case for treating imagination as a moral faculty alongside reasoning. The argument: ethical perception isn't just rule-application or outcome-calculation; it requires **imagining** — picturing other people's situations, simulating consequences, considering alternative framings, recognizing what one's habitual patterns leave out.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most ethical failures are failures of moral perception, not of moral reasoning.** When managers approve layoffs, product designers skip safety features, or policymakers cut programs, they often haven't done the wrong analysis — they haven't seen the moral situation in full. The numbers were correct; the human reality the numbers represented was unimagined. Moral imagination is the corrective: deliberately picture the people, circumstances, and consequences that the abstract presentation conceals.

Patricia Werhane's *Moral Imagination and Management Decision Making* (1999) operationalized this for business contexts. Her three operations:

1. **Disengagement** — step back from the immediate frame; recognize that current framing is one of many
2. **Imaginative reframing** — actively consider alternative framings, alternative actor roles, alternative outcomes
3. **Evaluation of new possibilities** — assess whether the new framings reveal moral considerations the original missed

A second insight: **moral imagination connects ethics to fiction, art, and narrative.** Reading literature about people in different circumstances, encountering art that disrupts habitual perception, narrating a decision from another's viewpoint — all of these exercise moral imagination. Adam Smith (1759) anticipated this: sympathy (his term) operates by imagining oneself into another's situation. The novel as a literary form has been argued (Martha Nussbaum) to be ethically formative because it trains moral imagination.

A third insight: **moral imagination has limits and biases.** We imagine more easily for those like us, in situations like ours, in cultures we know. Deliberate effort is required to imagine accurately for distant or different others. Without this effort, "moral imagination" can become projection — putting our own concerns into others' situations rather than discovering theirs.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The abstract-frame failure.** Decisions presented in financial, operational, or technical terms can hide the human consequences. Moral imagination redirects attention from the abstraction to the lived experience.
2. **The standard-framework hollow-feeling pattern.** Sometimes deontological / consequentialist analysis produces a verdict that doesn't sit right. Often the issue is that the standard frame missed something. Moral imagination explores what was missed.
3. **The novel-situation paralysis.** New technologies, new business models, new social dynamics often outrun existing ethical frameworks. Moral imagination helps reason about cases that don't yet have established paradigms (a casuistry-bridging move).

For consultants, executives, designers, and policy-makers, moral imagination is the disposition that catches what frameworks alone miss — the human reality embedded in abstract decisions.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Disengage from the immediate frame. What's the dominant framing of this          |
|      | decision (financial, operational, legal, technical)? Recognize that this is one |
|      | framing among possible others.                                                   |
|    2 | Identify the people affected. Move from abstractions ("the workforce") to       |
|      | specific imagined individuals (a 47-year-old manager who's been here 12 years).|
|    3 | Imagine their situation. What are they doing right now? What does their day      |
|      | look like? What changes for them after this decision?                           |
|    4 | Imagine consequences not in the original frame. Effects on family, community,   |
|      | health, identity, future. Effects on people not directly named in the analysis.|
|    5 | Imagine alternative framings. How would this look from the customer's           |
|      | perspective? From a regulator's? From a journalist's? From a 10-year-future    |
|      | retrospective?                                                                   |
|    6 | Use literature, history, fiction. Are there cases in fiction or history that    |
|      | help you see what's at stake? (Often yes — narrative trains moral imagination.)|
|    7 | Bring the imagined material back to the decision. Does it change what counts    |
|      | as the right action? Often it does.                                              |
|    8 | Watch for projection. Make sure you're imagining their situation, not           |
|      | importing your concerns into it. Reality-check via conversation when possible.  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE THREE-STEP IMAGINATION PROTOCOL (Werhane)

   1. DISENGAGEMENT
       Step back from the immediate frame.
       Question 1: What's the dominant framing here?
       Question 2: Whose interests does this framing center?
       Question 3: What does this framing leave out?

   2. IMAGINATIVE REFRAMING
       Actively consider alternatives.
       Question 1: What would this look like from another person's
       point of view?
       Question 2: What if the actors were reversed?
       Question 3: What if this were happening to me?
       Question 4: What if this had been done by us 10 years ago —
       how would it read now?

   3. EVALUATION
       Assess what the new framings reveal.
       Question 1: Did imagination surface considerations the
       original frame missed?
       Question 2: Do those considerations change the verdict?
       Question 3: How should the decision change?

THE PERSPECTIVE-SHIFT EXERCISES

   For any decision with human consequences, imagine specifically:

   - The person directly affected, on the day of impact
   - Their family member or close friend
   - A journalist writing about this in 5 years
   - A future historian assessing this in 50 years
   - A customer / citizen / voter affected indirectly
   - A child of someone affected, asking what happened
   - The decision-maker's future self, looking back

   For each: what do they see? What do they feel? What do they
   say about the decision?

THE DETAIL-IMAGINATION DISCIPLINE

   Generic imagination produces generic results. Specific
   imagination produces moral perception.

   Bad: "I imagine the affected employees will be sad."
   Good: "Maria, who's been here 12 years, told her teenage
        daughter last month that this job had given them
        stability after the divorce. She's about to learn
        that's ending."

   The specifics (Maria, 12 years, daughter, divorce, the
   conversation) are what make the imagination morally productive.
   Avoid the generic.

THE PROJECTION GUARD

   Moral imagination can become projection — putting your own
   concerns into others' situations rather than discovering theirs.

   Diagnostic questions:
       Have I imagined what THEY would feel, or what I would feel
       in their position?
       Have I checked my imagination against actual people in
       similar situations?
       Have I imagined them as full people (with their own values,
       priorities, concerns) or as flat instances of "person
       affected"?

   The defense against projection is conversation. Whenever
   possible, ask the actually-affected what they think — or read
   first-person accounts from people in similar situations.

THE LITERATURE AND HISTORY USE

   Reading literature, history, and journalism trains moral
   imagination. Specific recommendations from ethicists:

   For management ethics:
       Studs Terkel, Working (interviews with workers about their
       jobs)
       Barbara Ehrenreich, Nickel and Dimed (journalist's account
       of low-wage work)
       Erik Larson, Devil in the White City (architecture and
       moral choices in industrial America)

   For broader ethics:
       Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, George Eliot (novels training
       imagination across class and circumstance)
       James Baldwin (essays on race and moral perception)
       Hannah Arendt, Eichmann in Jerusalem (banality of evil
       through inability to imagine others)

   The practice: when wrestling with an ethical decision, ask
   what literature / history offers as analog. Often a pre-existing
   account illuminates the situation more than abstract analysis.

THE INSTITUTIONAL APPLICATION

   For organizations:

   Decision-making norms:
       Require explicit perspective-shifting in major decision
       memos. ("From the perspective of [affected party], this
       decision...")

   Onboarding to ethics:
       Teach moral imagination through case studies that include
       the human details, not just the abstract structure.

   Stakeholder engagement:
       Don't just survey stakeholders abstractly. Have decision-
       makers spend time with people affected by the decision
       (front-line workers, customers, community members).

   The discipline of moral imagination is institutional as well
   as personal — built through process design that demands it.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. ABSTRACT-FRAME LOCK-IN
        Decisions presented numerically; humans become abstract.
        Recovery: deliberately imagine specific people.

   2. PROJECTION
        "I imagine they'd feel..." (= I would feel).
        Recovery: check imagination against actual people.

   3. NEAR-ONLY IMAGINATION
        Easy to imagine for those like us; harder for distant
        others. Recovery: deliberate effort for far / different
        others; literature / history as bridge.

   4. SINGLE-PERSPECTIVE LIMIT
        Imagining only one type of stakeholder. Recovery: sweep
        through the perspective list (multiple stakeholders).

   5. IMAGINATION-WITHOUT-ACTION
        Imagining well but not letting it affect the decision.
        Recovery: explicit step from imagination to decision
        change.

THE QUICK-USE TEMPLATE

   Decision: __________________________________________________

   Currently framed as: ______________________________________

   Whose perspective is centered in current framing: __________

   Specific person to imagine (most affected): _______________
       Their day: _____________________________________________
       What changes for them: ________________________________
       What they'd say: ______________________________________

   Alternative perspectives:
       ______________________________________________________

   What does imagination surface that the original frame missed:
       ______________________________________________________

   Decision change (if any): ________________________________
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Specific imagination beats generic imagination. The discipline of imagining specific people in specific circumstances is what produces moral perception; abstract "the affected workforce" produces little. (2) Guard against projection. Imagining what you would feel in their situation is not the same as imagining what they would feel. The check is conversation — actually talk to the affected, when possible, or read first-person accounts. (3) Use literature and history. They train moral imagination across distance. When wrestling with an ethical question, ask what existing accounts illuminate the situation; often the account is more revealing than abstract analysis. (4) Translate imagination into decision change. Imagining without action is theater. The discipline includes asking, after the imagination work: how does this change what I should do?
