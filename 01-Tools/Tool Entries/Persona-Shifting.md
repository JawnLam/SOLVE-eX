# Persona-Shifting

**One-line summary:** A deliberate practice of inhabiting a specific named audience member — their biography, current state of knowledge, prior beliefs, and live concerns — *before* drafting communication, so the message starts where they are rather than where the speaker assumes.

**When to reach for it:** Any high-stakes communication where the audience cannot be re-engaged later (cold outreach, pitch decks, public statements, sales copy, change announcements), product / service design where users are not present in the room, and policy or strategy work where multiple distinct audiences must each be carried forward by the same artifact.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Persona-shifting is the discipline of *being someone else for the duration of a draft*. Rather than describing the audience in third person ("our buyers tend to value…"), the practitioner adopts a specific named persona — biography, role, prior beliefs, current emotional state, what they read this morning, what frustrated them last week — and writes from inside that perspective. The output is communication that begins where the audience is rather than where the speaker assumes they are.

The tradition has multiple sources. Direct-response copywriting (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966) formalized the move with the **awareness-stage ladder**: a prospect is unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or most-aware, and copy must enter at their actual stage rather than the stage the writer wishes they were at. UX design (Cooper's personas, 1999) gave the move a methodological form for product work. Negotiation training (Fisher and Ury's "go to the balcony"; Cialdini's compliance frames) extended it to live persuasion.

The non-obvious operational insight: **most communication failure is failure of starting point, not of substance**. Audiences do not stop reading because the argument is wrong; they stop reading because the opening sentence assumes a state they are not in. A reader who is problem-aware will skip past a paragraph that explains the problem. A reader who is unaware will not understand a paragraph that names the solution. The discipline of persona-shifting forces the writer to find the audience's actual starting state and meet them there.

Three operational modes:

- **Construction mode** (before drafting) — build the persona, then write
- **Audit mode** (after drafting) — read the draft as the persona and mark every place where the draft loses them
- **Live mode** (in conversation) — silently shift into the listener's perspective at the point of pushback; speak the next sentence from inside their position before returning to your own

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three classes of failure persona-shifting prevents:

1. **The "expert curse" opening.** Domain experts open at their own awareness level (most-aware) when the audience is at problem-aware or solution-aware. The opening sentence presupposes context the audience has not built. By the third sentence, the audience has stopped reading and the speaker has not yet made their point.
2. **The composite-audience trap.** A draft written for "our customers" or "the executive team" averages over multiple distinct audiences and lands as generic for all of them. Persona-shifting forces commitment to a specific person — the message becomes sharper, and if multiple audiences must be served the writer can produce variants rather than a single underwhelming composite.
3. **The empathy-without-action failure.** Generic empathy ("I understand they're busy") is psychologically validating but operationally inert. Persona-shifting demands a specific simulation: what is this person *doing* when they encounter my message? On what device? Under what emotional load? With what already-open tabs? Those concrete answers shape the message.

For consulting and B2B selling specifically, persona-shifting is the discipline that lets one practitioner credibly serve multiple stakeholder types in a single engagement — by writing each artifact from inside the relevant persona's perspective rather than from the consultant's own.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Name a specific person — real or composite. Give them a first name, role,        |
|      | tenure, recent quote you've heard them say. Generic personas ("the user") fail. |
|    2 | Place them physically: where are they when they encounter this message? What    |
|      | device? What time of day? What is the cognitive load already on them?           |
|    3 | Locate their awareness stage: unaware / problem-aware / solution-aware /        |
|      | product-aware / most-aware. Most communication errors enter at the wrong stage. |
|    4 | Surface their prior beliefs about the topic: what do they already think they    |
|      | know? What evidence have they already accepted or dismissed?                    |
|    5 | Surface their current emotional state and live concerns: what is competing for  |
|      | their attention right now? What recent event is shaping their reading of this?  |
|    6 | Draft the FIRST sentence from inside their perspective. Will they keep reading? |
|    7 | Iterate: at every paragraph break, ask "would this person still be with me?"    |
|      | If not, what did I lose them on?                                                |
|    8 | Audit (cold-read mode): set the draft aside, return as the persona, read.       |
|      | Mark every place that drifts out of their world. Rewrite those sections.        |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE PERSONA SHEET (build this BEFORE drafting)

   Name (specific):                _______________________________________
   Role / title:                   _______________________________________
   Tenure / context:               _______________________________________
   Recent quote (real or imagined): _______________________________________
   Where they are when they encounter the message:
       Physical location:          _______________________________________
       Device:                     _______________________________________
       Time of day / week:         _______________________________________
       What else is open / active: _______________________________________
   Cognitive load right now:       _______________________________________
   Emotional state:                _______________________________________
   What happened to them this week that's shaping their reading:
                                   _______________________________________

THE AWARENESS LADDER (Schwartz)

   Stage 1 — UNAWARE:
       Doesn't know they have the problem this message addresses.
       Opening must NAME the problem from their world's vocabulary.

   Stage 2 — PROBLEM-AWARE:
       Knows they have the problem; doesn't know solutions exist.
       Opening must validate the problem and gesture at solution space.

   Stage 3 — SOLUTION-AWARE:
       Knows solutions exist generically; not committed to a category.
       Opening must differentiate categories and place yours.

   Stage 4 — PRODUCT-AWARE:
       Knows your category; comparing options.
       Opening must distinguish your specific offering on dimensions
       they care about.

   Stage 5 — MOST-AWARE:
       Knows your specific offering; in evaluation or buying mode.
       Opening can be direct: price, terms, timing, next step.

   Diagnostic: where is THIS persona on the ladder? The opening must
   meet that stage. Skipping stages loses the audience; lingering at a
   stage they have passed bores them.

PRIOR-BELIEF AUDIT

   What do they already think they know about this topic?
       _______________________________________________________________

   What evidence have they already considered and accepted?
       _______________________________________________________________

   What evidence have they already considered and dismissed?
       _______________________________________________________________

   Where is the draft going to collide with what they already believe?
       _______________________________________________________________

   For collision points: the move is to acknowledge the existing belief
   first, then offer the case for revision. Skipping the acknowledgment
   reads as ignorance of their position.

THE FIRST-SENTENCE TEST

   Read your opening sentence aloud as the persona. Three diagnostics:

   1. Does this sentence assume context I have? If no, I'm gone.
   2. Is this sentence telling me something I already know? If yes,
      I'm bored.
   3. Is this sentence about me, or about the writer? If the latter,
      I'm not interested yet.

   Most failed openings fail on test 1 (assumes too much) or test 3
   (about the writer). Both are recoverable in 30 seconds of rewriting.

THE PARAGRAPH-BREAK CHECK

   At every paragraph break, the persona should be:
       (a) still with you, and
       (b) more curious or more committed than at the start of the
            paragraph.

   If neither, that paragraph is either wrong or out of order.

LIVE-MODE SHIFT (in conversation, at the point of pushback)

   When the listener pushes back:
       1. Silently shift INTO their perspective for ten seconds.
       2. Find the most charitable interpretation of their objection.
       3. Speak the next sentence from inside their position
          ("From where you sit, this looks like…").
       4. Then return to your own perspective with their concern
          incorporated, not bypassed.

   This is the same move as the construction-mode persona-shift, done
   in real time. The hardest discipline is the silent ten seconds —
   most speakers respond from their own position immediately, which
   reads as not having heard.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Specificity beats demographic abstraction. "The CFO" generates generic copy; "Maria, CFO at a 200-person SaaS company, two years in, just lost a deal because of pricing" generates writable copy. The persona must be specific enough that you could not write generically even if you tried. (2) Awareness-stage diagnosis is the highest-leverage single move. More communication errors come from entering at the wrong stage than from any other single failure. Build the diagnosis as a habit before drafting. (3) Persona-shifting is not the same as agreement. Inhabiting a perspective lets you write to it; it does not require endorsing it. The discipline is empathic accuracy — knowing what they think — not capitulation. (4) The cold-read audit (set the draft aside, return as the persona, read) is where most polish happens. Skipping it produces drafts that read well to the writer and badly to the audience. Schedule the time gap; don't audit immediately after writing.
