# Eulogy Exercise

**One-line summary:** A reflective exercise in which you imagine attendees at your funeral and articulate what you would want them to say — surfacing deep values that present-day decision-making often obscures.

**When to reach for it:** Career inflection points, mid-life transitions, end-of-year reflection, values clarification before major life decisions, executive coaching when surface goals seem misaligned with deeper purpose.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Stephen Covey introduced this exercise in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* (1989) as part of Habit 2: "Begin with the End in Mind." The mechanic is simple: imagine you are at your own funeral. Four people speak — a family member, a friend, a colleague / professional peer, and someone from a community or religious group. **What do you want each to say about you?**

The non-obvious operational insight is that **the eulogy bypasses surface goals.** Asked directly "what do you want from your life?" people often answer with surface achievements — a title, an income, a portfolio. Asked "what would you want said about you at your funeral?" the same people produce dramatically different answers — about character, relationships, integrity, contribution. The reframing reveals what was always being valued at the deepest level but suppressed in everyday decision-making.

A second insight: **the gap between current trajectory and desired eulogy is the design surface.** The exercise often surfaces uncomfortable misalignment — current life is producing achievements the speaker won't ultimately care about while neglecting things they will. Naming the gap is the intervention; closing it requires action that the framework doesn't dictate.

A third insight: **the exercise is more powerful when written, not just thought.** Speaking the eulogy aloud or writing it on paper produces a more honest output than internal rehearsal. The act of producing the text forces commitment to specific words.

The exercise has religious / monastic roots in *memento mori* practices but works in fully secular framings. The form doesn't depend on belief about afterlife; it depends on imagining how a finite life will be remembered.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The surface-goal trap.** Career and life planning that operates on what the speaker says they want often misses what they actually want. The eulogy exercise produces deeper output — what they want to be remembered for, which is closer to their actual values.
2. **The values-action misalignment.** Many people make decisions inconsistent with their stated values without noticing. The eulogy makes the misalignment visible: "I value family but my schedule shows I don't." The visibility prompts action.
3. **The deferred-priorities pattern.** "I'll focus on health / family / meaning later, after this work phase." The eulogy tests whether the deferral is sustainable: "If I died today, would the eulogy reflect 'later'?" Usually no, which forces present revision.

For coaches, advisors, executives, and individuals at inflection points, the exercise is one of the simplest and most-tested values-clarification tools in the literature — minutes to administer, often substantial in effect.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Block 30-60 minutes alone, in a quiet place. The exercise produces emotion;     |
|      | rushed completion is shallow.                                                    |
|    2 | Imagine your own funeral, decades from now. The setting is real and detailed.   |
|    3 | Choose four speakers: a family member, a close friend, a colleague / peer, and  |
|      | someone from a community / religious / civic context.                            |
|    4 | For each speaker, write what you would want them to say — not what they probably|
|      | would say given current life, but what you would WISH them to say if your life  |
|      | played out as you'd want.                                                         |
|    5 | Read each eulogy aloud. Notice what's said and what's not said. What words      |
|      | appear in multiple eulogies? Those are core values.                             |
|    6 | Compare to current trajectory. What in your current life is producing the kind  |
|      | of person who would receive these eulogies? What is producing someone else?     |
|    7 | Identify the gap. Name 1-3 specific changes that would close it.                 |
|    8 | Schedule a follow-up review (3-6 months) to revisit. Eulogy clarity fades       |
|      | without re-engagement; periodic return is what makes the exercise persistent.   |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FOUR-SPEAKER TEMPLATE

   Imagine your funeral, decades from now. Four speakers eulogize you.
   Write what you would WANT each to say.

   SPEAKER 1: Family member (spouse, child, sibling, parent)
       Their relationship to you: ________________________________
       What they say about you (3-5 sentences):
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________

   SPEAKER 2: Close friend
       Their relationship to you: ________________________________
       What they say (3-5 sentences):
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________

   SPEAKER 3: Colleague or professional peer
       Their relationship to you: ________________________________
       What they say (3-5 sentences):
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________

   SPEAKER 4: Community / civic / spiritual context
       Their relationship to you: ________________________________
       What they say (3-5 sentences):
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________
       ____________________________________________________________

THE READBACK ANALYSIS

   Read all four aloud. Then audit:

   What words / phrases appear across multiple eulogies?
       _______________________________________________________

   What's said about CHARACTER (vs. accomplishment)?
       _______________________________________________________

   What's said about IMPACT ON OTHERS (vs. self)?
       _______________________________________________________

   What's said about MEANING / PURPOSE (vs. tasks)?
       _______________________________________________________

   What's NOT said that you initially expected? What surprised you?
       _______________________________________________________

   The repeated themes are your deep values — operationalized as
   the things you'd want to be remembered for.

THE GAP DIAGNOSIS

   Compare the eulogy content to your current life:

   What in my current life is building toward these eulogies?
       _______________________________________________________

   What in my current life is building toward DIFFERENT eulogies?
       _______________________________________________________

   What's the largest gap between current trajectory and desired
   eulogy?
       _______________________________________________________

   Specific actions in the next 90 days that would close the gap:
       1. _____________________________________________________
       2. _____________________________________________________
       3. _____________________________________________________

THE LIVING-EULOGY VARIANT

   For higher emotional impact, do the exercise with a partner /
   coach:
       Person A writes their eulogy.
       Person B reads it aloud as if eulogizing.
       Person A listens to their own desired eulogy spoken about
       them.

   Hearing the eulogy spoken (vs. just reading it) often produces
   stronger clarity about misalignment than reading alone. Reserved
   for trusting relationships; not appropriate for all coaching
   contexts.

THE 80-YEAR-OLD-SELF VARIANT

   Imagine yourself at 80. Write a letter from your 80-year-old
   self to your present self.

       What does my 80-year-old self thank me for?
       What do they regret?
       What do they wish I had started sooner?
       What do they wish I had stopped sooner?

   Same operational principle (using a future viewpoint to clarify
   present values), different framing — preferred by some who find
   the funeral image too dark or too final.

THE PERIODIC RE-ENGAGEMENT

   The eulogy clarity fades within weeks if not re-engaged.
   Schedule periodic returns:

       Quarterly: re-read your eulogies; review trajectory; adjust.
       Annually: re-write fresh eulogies. They evolve.
       At inflection points (job change, life event): re-do.

   The exercise is not one-and-done; the value is in its
   ongoing use as a compass.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. RUSHED COMPLETION
        15 minutes between meetings produces shallow output.
        Recovery: protected 30-60 minute block.

   2. WRITING WHAT'S PROBABLE, NOT WHAT'S WANTED
        "Here's what they'd actually say given my current life"
        misses the point. Recovery: write what you WISH they'd
        say.

   3. AVOIDING DIFFICULT TRUTHS
        Some eulogies will reveal uncomfortable misalignment.
        Recovery: write through the discomfort; the discomfort is
        the signal.

   4. ONE-AND-DONE
        Single completion fades fast. Recovery: schedule
        re-engagement.

   5. INTELLECTUALIZING
        Treating the exercise as an analytical task rather than
        emotional one. Recovery: allow yourself to feel what comes;
        the emotion is part of the design.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Write, don't just think. The act of producing words on paper or aloud forces commitment to specifics that internal reflection avoids. The exercise's power comes from the friction of articulation. (2) Write what you WANT said, not what's probable. The exercise reveals values, not predictions. The probable eulogy given current trajectory is informative, but the desired eulogy is the goal. (3) The gap between trajectory and desired eulogy is the actionable surface. Naming the gap is the intervention; closing it requires action that emerges from the gap analysis. (4) Re-engage periodically. Single completion fades within weeks. The exercise's long-term value comes from quarterly or annual revisit, not from the first session. Build it into a recurring rhythm.
