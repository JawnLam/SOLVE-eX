# Narrative Therapy

**One-line summary:** A therapeutic approach that treats psychological difficulty as a *story* the client lives within — and works to *externalize* the problem (separating problem from person), surface "unique outcomes" inconsistent with the problem-saturated story, and re-author the dominant narrative.

**When to reach for it:** When a client's identity has fused with a problem ("I'm an anxious person", "I'm a failure"); when prior therapy has narrated the difficulty as character flaw; group or community contexts where shared narratives matter; or as a values-clarification frame.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

White and Epston's central move is *externalization*: shift the linguistic frame from "I am anxious" to "Anxiety has been visiting me a lot lately." This relocates the problem outside the person, allowing client and therapist to study the problem's tactics, its preferred conditions, its allies and opponents — without the client being identified-with-the-problem.

The non-obvious operational insight is the search for *unique outcomes* — moments when the dominant problem-story didn't hold. Even chronic depression has occasional minutes of curiosity; even chronic procrastination has finished tasks. These exceptions, treated as data rather than anomalies, are the seed of an alternative story. Re-authoring builds out from these moments — what kind of person could that be? What values were operative? What does this say about my capacities?

The work is collaborative — therapist as co-author, not expert interpreter. The client's lived experience and meanings are honored; therapist questions help the client surface and connect threads they'd dismissed.

The approach has been adopted broadly in family therapy, community work (especially with marginalized populations whose dominant cultural narratives oppress), and increasingly in coaching contexts.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes narrative work addresses:

1. **Identity-problem fusion.** "I am X" makes change feel like self-betrayal. Externalization restores agency.
2. **Pathology language.** Diagnostic labels can become identity descriptors. Narrative therapy questions which language serves the client and which traps them.
3. **Story-poverty.** Many clients have only one story about themselves, problem-saturated. Re-authoring expands the available repertoire.

For coaching and self-development, narrative techniques are especially useful for transitions — career changes, role shifts, identity transitions — where the existing story doesn't accommodate emerging reality.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Listen for the dominant problem-saturated story. What's the headline?           |
|    2 | Externalize the problem — give it a name and address it as separate from the   |
|      | person. "How long has Anxiety been visiting?" "What does Procrastination tell  |
|      | you?"                                                                           |
|    3 | Map the problem's effects — what does it influence? Where? When?                |
|    4 | Map the problem's tactics — how does it gain ground? What conditions help it?  |
|    5 | Search for unique outcomes — moments contrary to the problem-story. "Tell me   |
|      | about a time when X didn't get the better of you."                              |
|    6 | Thicken the alternative story — what does the unique outcome say about the     |
|      | person? What values, capacities, history does it reveal?                        |
|    7 | Identify "audience" for the alternative story — who would recognize and       |
|      | support this version?                                                           |
|    8 | Document the new narrative — letters, certificates, narrative maps. White &   |
|      | Epston used written documents extensively.                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
EXTERNALIZATION CONVERSATION TEMPLATE

    Problem (named externally — give it a name):
      "____________________________________________"
      e.g., "the Anxiety", "the Procrastination", "the Critical Voice"

    HISTORY OF THE PROBLEM
      When did it first show up?  ______________________________________
      What does it want?           ____________________________________
      What does it tell you about yourself? ___________________________

    EFFECTS / INFLUENCE OF THE PROBLEM
      On work:           ___________________________________________
      On relationships:  ___________________________________________
      On body:           ___________________________________________
      On future-imagining:_________________________________________
      On self-relationship: _______________________________________

    TACTICS OF THE PROBLEM (what conditions support it?)
      It does best when: __________________________________________
      It gains ground via: ________________________________________
      Allies:             __________________________________________
      Opposition / weakening conditions: __________________________

UNIQUE OUTCOMES (moments contrary to the problem-story)

    Recent moment when the problem didn't get the better of me:
      ___________________________________________________________________

    What did I do that worked?  _________________________________________
    What did I value in that moment?  ___________________________________
    What capacity does that reveal in me?  ______________________________
    What does that tell me about who I am, beyond the problem-story?
      ___________________________________________________________________

ALTERNATIVE STORY DEVELOPMENT

    Headline of the dominant story (problem-saturated):
      "____________________________________________________________"

    Alternative headline (begin tentatively):
      "____________________________________________________________"

    Evidence supporting the alternative (unique outcomes, capacities,
    history-of-resistance to the problem):
      1. _________________________________________________________
      2. _________________________________________________________
      3. _________________________________________________________

    Values implicit in the alternative story:
      ___________________________________________________________________

    Audience — who would recognize this version of me, and how?
      ___________________________________________________________________

DEFINITIONAL CEREMONY (group narrative practice)

    The client tells their alternative story to an "outsider witness team."
    Witnesses respond not by evaluating but by:
      • What expression caught their attention?
      • What image of the client's life did this evoke?
      • What in their own life did this evoke?
      • Where did this take them?

    The client then witnesses the witnesses, completing the loop. The
    structure thickens the alternative story through community
    acknowledgment — particularly powerful in marginalized-population
    contexts.

LETTER WRITING (Epston's practice)

    After significant therapy moments, write letters that:
      • Document the unique outcomes that emerged
      • Honor the values made visible
      • Invite further development of the alternative story

    The letter is the artifact; clients keep them, re-read them,
    share them. Story made tangible.
```

> **Operational notes:** Three disciplines. (1) Be careful with externalization. The technique is liberating when the problem is felt as identity-fused (depression, anxiety, addiction), but can be inappropriate when it externalizes harm someone has done — externalizing "the violence" can let an abuser off the hook. Use it for the client's own internal struggles, not for relational harms requiring accountability. (2) Trust unique outcomes even when small. A single moment of capacity is enough to seed re-authoring; the discipline is taking it seriously rather than dismissing as "just one time." Thicken slowly. (3) Honor the client's language. Narrative therapy refuses expert diagnostic vocabulary in favor of the client's own meaning-making. The therapist's question-stance is genuinely curious; if you find yourself mentally translating the client's experience into clinical categories, you're outside the practice. Fourth: written documents (letters, certificates, narrative maps) are not decorative — they're the practice's artifact. The story made tangible holds against drift back into problem-saturation.
