# Internal Family Systems (IFS)

**One-line summary:** A therapeutic model that treats the psyche as a system of distinct *parts* (each with its own perspective, history, and protective function) plus a central *Self* — and works with parts rather than against them.

**When to reach for it:** Inner conflict (one part wants X, another fears it), self-criticism that doesn't yield to standard CBT, processing protective behaviors (perfectionism, control, addiction, dissociation), trauma work in a non-pathologizing frame.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

IFS proposes that what feels like a unified self is actually a *system* of parts. Some parts are *managers* (proactive — keeping things controlled, perfectionistic, achievement-oriented), some are *firefighters* (reactive — numbing, distracting, lashing out when distress breaks through), and some are *exiles* (the wounded younger parts the others protect). All parts are doing something *for* the system, even when their behavior is destructive; the model treats all parts as well-intentioned.

The non-obvious operational insight is the *Self* — not another part but the underlying ground state, characterized by Schwartz's "8 C's": calm, curious, compassionate, courageous, confident, clear, creative, connected. When parts step back, Self can contact and reparent the wounded exiles. The work is not eliminating parts but updating their roles via Self's leadership.

This reframe is therapeutic because it *de-pathologizes* the inner critic, the addict, the dissociator. Each is a part with a history, a protective function, and the capacity to relax once its underlying concern is met. Force against parts (suppress, reason away) entrenches them; meeting them shifts them.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes IFS addresses:

1. **Inner war.** "Just stop being anxious" / "Just stop self-sabotaging" sets one part against another. IFS gets all parts on the same team.
2. **Pathologized self-criticism.** Treating the inner critic as enemy intensifies it. IFS treats it as a misguided protector and asks what it's protecting.
3. **Trauma without re-traumatization.** Direct exposure to traumatic material without parts work can destabilize. IFS gets protectors' permission first; exiles are reached only when protectors agree.

For consulting and self-development work (with appropriate caveats about clinical scope), IFS is increasingly used in coaching contexts — its non-pathologizing frame lets people engage inner conflict productively without adopting a "diagnosis" frame.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Notice a part: what voice / feeling / behavior is present right now?            |
|    2 | Locate it — where in the body? What does it look / sound like?                  |
|    3 | Get curious about it (Self stance: curiosity, compassion). Ask: what's its job? |
|    4 | Find out what it's afraid would happen if it didn't do its job.                 |
|    5 | Often, this surfaces a protected part (an exile). Ask the protective part if    |
|      | you can meet what it protects.                                                  |
|    6 | If permission given, contact the exile from Self. Witness its experience.       |
|    7 | "Unburden" — invite the exile to release the burden it's carrying (often an    |
|      | old belief or feeling from a specific time).                                    |
|    8 | Update protector roles: with the exile no longer needing protection, what new   |
|      | role would the protector like?                                                  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE 6 F's PROTOCOL (Schwartz)

    1. FIND — locate the part. "Where is it in or around your body?"

    2. FOCUS — turn attention toward it. Drop other concerns briefly.

    3. FLESH OUT — get to know it. What does it look like? Sound like?
       How old does it seem? What's its name (let one come)?

    4. FEEL TOWARD — how do you feel toward this part right now?
       □ curious / compassionate / connected (Self is present — proceed)
       □ critical / afraid / angry (another part is in the way — work with that
          part first; ask it to step back so you can hear the original part)

    5. BEFRIEND — get to know its history, its job, what it fears.
       Questions: How long has it been doing this? What's it afraid would
       happen if it stopped? What does it want for you?

    6. FEAR (often the climax) — what does this part fear most?
       Often surfaces what it's protecting.

PARTS-MAP TEMPLATE

    PART NAME / DESCRIPTOR: _______________________________________________
    Type: □ manager  □ firefighter  □ exile  □ unclear
    Body location: _________________________________________________________
    Apparent age: __________________________________________________________

    Job (what does it DO?): _______________________________________________
    Fear (what's it afraid would happen if it stopped?): _________________
    What it wants for me: _________________________________________________
    What it might be protecting:  _________________________________________

    Stance from Self (8 C's check):
      [ ] calm     [ ] curious   [ ] compassionate   [ ] courageous
      [ ] confident [ ] clear    [ ] creative        [ ] connected

    Conversation notes (what it said when invited):
      ___________________________________________________________________

EXILE WORK (when a protector grants access)

    Exile age:                _____________________________________________
    What it carries (burden): ____________________________________________
    Originating context:       ____________________________________________
    What it needs from you:    ____________________________________________

    UNBURDENING
      Invite the exile to release the burden. Common imagery: hand it to
      light, water, fire, earth — whatever feels right.
      Replace with what it actually wants: love, warmth, witnessing.

    PROTECTOR ROLE UPDATE
      With the exile no longer needing protection, what new role would the
      protector like?  ____________________________________________________
      What support does it need from you?  _______________________________

THE 8 C'S OF SELF (signs Self is present, not a part)

      Calm        — settled, not activated
      Curious     — wanting to learn, not judge
      Compassionate — open to suffering without fixing
      Courageous  — able to stay with hard material
      Confident   — trusting the process, not needing certainty
      Clear       — perception not distorted by reactivity
      Creative    — solutions / images / phrases arise
      Connected   — feeling related to the part, not estranged

      If these aren't present, another part is in the way. Notice it; ask it
      to step back; offer to come back to it later.

CAUTIONS

    [ ] Trauma work especially benefits from a trained IFS therapist
    [ ] Severe dissociative tendencies require specialized approach
    [ ] If a protector won't step back even after being heard, that's
        information — its concern hasn't been fully understood
    [ ] Self-application has scope; clinical conditions warrant clinical care
```

> **Operational notes:** Three disciplines. (1) Don't fight parts. The instinct to "shut up the inner critic" or "suppress the addict" entrenches them. The IFS move is curiosity — asking what the part is doing for you. (2) Confirm Self-presence before working. The 8 C's are diagnostic; if they're absent, another part is up front (often a part that fears the work). Work with that part first. (3) Trust the part's own pace. Exile work especially must not be rushed; a protector that grants access partially is to be respected, not bullied through. Fourth: IFS's non-pathologizing language is the philosophy. Treat the inner addict / critic / perfectionist as a part with history, not as a defect. The reframe is therapeutic in itself — clients often soften toward themselves the moment "I'm broken" becomes "a part of me is doing something it learned to do."
