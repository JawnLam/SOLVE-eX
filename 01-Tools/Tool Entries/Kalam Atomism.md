# Kalam Atomistic Reasoning (Ash'arite)

**One-line summary:** Islamic speculative theology (Ash'arite school, 10th c.) — reality is composed of momentary atoms; God creates each moment afresh; apparent causation is habit of divine action, not real necessity.

**When to reach for it:** Engaging theological reasoning about causation, divine agency, and the structure of moment-to-moment reality where the question is whether ordinary cause-and-effect descriptions track real necessities or whether they are habit-of-divine-action descriptions.

## Purpose

Kalam ('speech' or 'discourse') is Islamic speculative theology. The Ash'arite school (al-Ash'ari, ~10th c.) developed an atomistic ontology: reality is composed of momentary atoms (jawhar) that God creates afresh in each moment. Apparent causation between events (fire causes burning) is not real necessity — it is the habit ('ada) of divine action. God could in principle ordain that fire-touching-cotton not produce burning, as in the miracle of Abraham's protection from Nimrod's fire. The cognitive method: when reasoning about reality, suspect chains of cause-and-effect and look for atomic-momentary descriptions. Influenced Christian medieval theology (occasionalism) and indirectly modern philosophy of science.

## How To Use

Approach a causal claim — 'X causes Y.' Ask: do we know this from a real necessity in the nature of X and Y, or from habitual co-occurrence? In Kalam's frame: even apparent necessity is habit of divine action; we cannot rule out God's prerogative to act otherwise. Practically: this disciplines causal claims, reminding the inquirer that habitual co-occurrence is empirically certain but metaphysically contingent. The frame produces (somewhat surprisingly) close affinities with modern Humean skepticism about causation. Used in Islamic theological argument; useful as a cognitive frame for examining the modal status of causal claims more generally.
