# Sun Tzu Asymmetric Logic

**One-line summary:** Sun Tzu's strategic principles — emphasizing indirect approach, deception, positional advantage, and winning before direct contest — used in any setting where direct adversarial confrontation is suboptimal compared to maneuvering, position, and surprise.

**When to reach for it:** Competitive business strategy, market entry against incumbents, negotiation positioning, political campaigns, organizational politics, and any context where direct confrontation is more costly than indirect approach.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

*The Art of War* (Sun Tzu, ~5th century BCE) is the foundational Chinese strategic classic. Its core distinction from Western military thought (especially Clausewitz) is the emphasis on **avoiding direct confrontation** in favor of position, deception, and indirect approach. Key principles:

1. **"Subdue the enemy without fighting"** — the supreme excellence of strategy is winning without direct contest
2. **"Know yourself and your enemy"** — foundational; without this, "in every battle you will be defeated"
3. **"Avoid strength, attack weakness"** — concentrate force where opponent is weakest, not strongest
4. **"Be where they are not"** — surprise, indirect approach, unpredictable position
5. **"All warfare is based on deception"** — make opponent see what isn't, not see what is
6. **"Hold what they value"** — leverage what opponent must protect to constrain their action
7. **"Use spies"** — information advantage is foundational

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most strategic contests are won before direct conflict.** By the time forces meet, the outcome is largely determined by prior positioning, information advantage, and the relative attrition of resources spent reaching that point. Sun Tzu's framework directs attention to these prior moves rather than to the climactic battle.

A second insight: **asymmetric matters more than absolute strength.** A smaller force with positional advantage, surprise, or information superiority can defeat a larger force lacking those. The framework's relentless focus on creating asymmetries — being where opponent isn't, knowing what opponent doesn't, holding what opponent must protect — is the operational core.

A third insight: **the framework transfers extensively to business strategy.** Competitive markets are extended-conflict environments where position, information, and indirect approach matter more than force. Modern business strategy literature (Porter, Sun Tzu and Business, Business Wars) draws heavily on Sun Tzu.

A fourth insight: **the framework has limits.** Direct confrontation is sometimes unavoidable, and deception can corrode trust over time. Sun Tzu is one strategic lens among several (Clausewitz, OODA, modern business strategy frameworks). Use as part of a strategic toolkit, not as a complete substitute.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The direct-confrontation default.** Western strategic thought often defaults to direct contest; Sun Tzu redirects to whether the contest is necessary at all. Many "victories" via indirect approach avoid the costs of direct contest entirely.
2. **The "we have more resources" complacency.** Larger force / market position / capital often wins... unless opponent creates asymmetries. Sun Tzu's discipline anticipates and creates asymmetries before the larger party recognizes them.
3. **The information-advantage neglect.** Many strategists pour resources into action while neglecting reconnaissance. Sun Tzu's emphasis on knowing self and opponent (and using spies / scouts) is the corrective.

For executives in competitive markets, military strategists, political campaigners, negotiators, and any strategist facing adversarial competition, Sun Tzu provides a foundational lens emphasizing indirection, position, and information.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Know yourself. Strengths, weaknesses, resources, alliances, information,         |
|      | morale, leadership, position. Be honest.                                         |
|    2 | Know the opponent. Same dimensions; gather via observation, intelligence, and    |
|      | inference.                                                                       |
|    3 | Identify asymmetries. Where are you stronger / weaker? Where do information     |
|      | gaps favor whom?                                                                 |
|    4 | Avoid direct contest where opponent is stronger. Maneuver to where you have     |
|      | advantage; force opponent to engage you on your terms.                          |
|    5 | Create position before contest. Pre-position; build alliances; secure           |
|      | information; deny opponent information.                                          |
|    6 | Use deception strategically. Make opponent see what isn't, miss what is.        |
|      | (Caveat: deception in business has trust costs.)                                |
|    7 | If contest is unavoidable, concentrate force on opponent's weakness, not       |
|      | strength. Attack the unprotected; pin the protected.                            |
|    8 | Know when to retreat / withdraw. Sun Tzu emphasizes preserving forces;          |
|      | sometimes the best strategic move is non-engagement.                             |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FOUNDATIONAL PRINCIPLES (the practical core)

   1. KNOW YOURSELF AND YOUR ENEMY
       "If you know neither yourself nor your enemy, in every
       battle you will be defeated."
       Audit: strengths, weaknesses, resources, intentions of
       both sides.

   2. SUBDUE WITHOUT FIGHTING
       The supreme excellence: achieve objectives without
       direct contest.
       Methods: position, alliance, threat, attrition, denial.

   3. AVOID STRENGTH, ATTACK WEAKNESS
       Concentrate force on opponent's weakest points.
       Demonstration of force on strong points to pin them.

   4. BE WHERE THEY ARE NOT
       Surprise, indirect approach, unpredictable position.

   5. ALL WARFARE IS DECEPTION
       Make opponent see what isn't; not see what is.
       (Caveat: deception in business has long-term trust
       costs. Use carefully.)

   6. HOLD WHAT THEY VALUE
       Leverage what opponent must protect to constrain their
       action.

   7. USE SPIES / INTELLIGENCE
       Information advantage is foundational. Investment in
       reconnaissance pays disproportionate returns.

   ASYMMETRIES:
       Where you have advantage: _____________________

       Where information gaps favor you: _____________

THE INDIRECT-APPROACH CATALOG

   1. POSITIONAL MOVES (before contest)
       Build alliances, secure resources, deny resources to
       opponent, occupy advantageous positions.

   2. INFORMATION MOVES
       Gather opponent intelligence; deny opponent intelligence
       on you; selective disclosure for deception.

   3. ATTRITION
       Force opponent to spend resources; deny opponent
       resupply; lengthen their supply / decision chains.

   4. ALLIANCE
       Convert potential enemies; isolate opponent; create
       multi-front pressure without direct engagement.

   5. SURPRISE
       Attack at unexpected time / place / way; preserve
       initiative.

   6. DENIAL
       Block opponent's preferred moves; force them into
       suboptimal alternatives.

   7. WITHDRAWAL
       When direct contest favors opponent, withdraw to
       preserve forces for better opportunity.

THE BUSINESS-STRATEGY APPLICATIONS

   MARKET ENTRY VS. INCUMBENT:
       Direct attack on incumbent's core market is usually
       suicidal (incumbent has scale, brand, distribution).
       Indirect: enter underserved segments incumbent
       neglects (Christensen's disruption theory is
       structurally Sun Tzu-compatible).

   COMPETITIVE PRICING:
       Direct price war destroys margins for both. Indirect:
       differentiate to escape direct comparison; create
       segments where opponent can't follow profitably.

   PRODUCT COMPETITION:
       Direct feature war is matched by competitor's product
       team. Indirect: build platform / ecosystem / network
       effects opponent can't replicate.

   NEGOTIATION:
       Direct demand confronts opponent's BATNA. Indirect:
       reframe to surface alignment / shared interests
       opponent's BATNA didn't anticipate.

THE INFORMATION-ADVANTAGE INVESTMENT

   "Foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot
   be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any
   deductive calculation. Knowledge of the enemy's
   dispositions can only be obtained from other men."

   Sun Tzu: invest aggressively in spies / intelligence.

   Business translation:
       Customer research (your spies in the customer's mind)
       Competitor intelligence (legal: financial filings,
       hiring patterns, customer interviews)
       Market sensing (signals before they become trends)
       Internal pulse (what's happening across own org)

   Strategists who short-fund information are reliably
   outmaneuvered by those who don't.

THE DECEPTION CAVEAT

   Sun Tzu's "all warfare is deception" was for warfare.
   In business / politics / sustained relationships, deception
   has long-term costs:

   - Trust erosion
   - Reputation damage when caught
   - Cumulative paranoia in relationship
   - Legal / regulatory consequences

   Use deception sparingly. Most modern strategic value comes
   from positional advantage, information, indirect approach —
   not deception per se. Reserve deception for contexts where
   the trust cost is acceptable (rare in ongoing business).

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. DIRECT-CONFRONTATION DEFAULT
        Going head-on when indirect would work. Recovery:
        explicit indirect-options analysis before direct.

   2. SELF-KNOWLEDGE NEGLECT
        Knowing opponent better than self. Recovery: own-
        strengths-weaknesses audit as routine practice.

   3. INFORMATION UNDERFUNDING
        Limited intelligence → blind strategic moves.
        Recovery: invest in reconnaissance.

   4. DECEPTION OVERUSE
        Long-term trust erosion. Recovery: use deception
        sparingly; rely more on position and information.

   5. WITHDRAWAL-AS-WEAKNESS PERCEPTION
        Refusing to withdraw when contest is unfavorable.
        Recovery: Sun Tzu treats withdrawal as preservation
        of forces; reframe.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Direct contest is rarely the optimal first move. The framework's central claim is that position, information, and indirect approach often win before direct conflict. Audit indirect options before committing to confrontation. (2) Self-knowledge equals opponent-knowledge. Many strategists thoroughly analyze the opponent while remaining vague about their own strengths and weaknesses. The discipline of explicit own-audit is the underrated half. (3) Information advantage compounds. Investments in intelligence, customer research, competitor sensing pay disproportionate returns. Most strategic failures are partly information failures. (4) Use deception carefully. Sun Tzu was writing for warfare; in business and sustained relationships, deception has trust costs that often outweigh tactical gains. Position, information, and timing produce most of the value the framework points to without the costs of explicit deception.
