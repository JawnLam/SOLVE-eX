# Continuing-Bonds Model

**One-line summary:** A model of grief in which the relationship with the deceased is *transformed rather than severed* — the lost person continues as inner figure, transmitted values, kept objects, and ongoing influence — and this continuation is part of healthy adaptation, not pathology.

**When to reach for it:** Bereavement support, especially when the bereaved has been told to "let go" or "move on"; processing complex grief; supporting children and adolescents through loss; cultural contexts that already practice ancestor connection but have been told this is unhealthy.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

For most of the 20th century, Western grief theory followed Freud's "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917): healthy grief required *withdrawal of attachment* from the lost object. Persistent attachment was seen as failure of grief work. Klass, Silverman, and Nickman's 1996 empirical research overturned this: bereaved people who had clearly adapted well typically had *strong* continuing connections — they spoke to the deceased, kept significant objects, felt ongoing influence, dreamed about them, made decisions partly based on what the lost person would want. The connection was transformed (no longer in-person, no longer mutual in the same way) but emphatically not severed.

The non-obvious operational insight is that *continuing bonds and adaptation are compatible* — sometimes mutually supportive. Carrying the lost person forward as inner figure provides moral compass, comfort, motivation. It also acknowledges that important relationships don't end at death, even when their form changes radically.

Cultural context matters: many non-Western traditions (East Asian ancestor practices, Latin American Día de los Muertos, Indigenous traditions) have always practiced continuing bonds. The continuing-bonds model brings Western clinical theory into alignment with these long-standing practices.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the model corrects:

1. **"Let go" pressure.** Standard cultural advice tells the bereaved to detach. The model reframes: don't detach; transform.
2. **Pathologized inner conversation.** Talking to / dreaming about / sensing the deceased is normal and often supportive. The model removes the pathology label.
3. **Premature object disposal.** Pressure to clear out belongings can disrupt grief; objects are anchors for continuing bond and serve the work.

For bereavement support and self-application, the continuing-bonds frame gives explicit permission to maintain inner connection — often a relief to people who feared they were "stuck."

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Notice existing continuing bonds — inner conversations, kept objects, sensed   |
|      | presence, dreams, decisions made with the lost person in mind.                  |
|    2 | Reframe — these are not failures of grief; they are transformations of the      |
|      | relationship. Honor them.                                                       |
|    3 | Choose how to nurture the bond. Conscious cultivation makes the connection      |
|      | a resource rather than an intrusion.                                            |
|    4 | Track which bonds support and which complicate. (Some bonds tie us to           |
|      | unresolved difficulties; those need work, not preservation.)                    |
|    5 | Find ritual or practice that honors the bond at meaningful intervals —          |
|      | anniversaries, transitions, decisions.                                          |
|    6 | Support new life. Continuing bonds and new attachments are not in zero-sum;     |
|      | both can grow.                                                                  |
|    7 | Discuss with others when relevant. Children especially benefit from explicit    |
|      | permission to continue connection.                                              |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
CONTINUING-BONDS INVENTORY

    Lost relationship: __________________________________________________
    Years since loss:   ________

    Current bonds (note which are present)
      [ ] Inner dialogue: I talk to them in my head; they "answer"
      [ ] Sensed presence: I feel them at certain moments / places
      [ ] Dreams: they appear in dreams; meaningful or troubling?
      [ ] Kept objects: belongings I've retained as anchors
      [ ] Symbols: meanings, images, songs that carry their presence
      [ ] Internalized values: I make decisions partly based on what
          they would have wanted / who they would have admired
      [ ] Continuing influence: my work, parenting, relationships shaped
          by them
      [ ] Rituals: anniversary / birthday observance, visits to grave /
          memorial spaces, lighting candles, etc.

    For each present bond:
      Does it support me right now?         □ yes  □ neutral  □ complicates
      Should I cultivate, accept, or work?  ____________________________

    Bonds that COMPLICATE
      Some bonds carry unresolved material — guilt, ambivalence, anger
      that wasn't worked. These benefit from grief work, not severing.
      Note any:
      ____________________________________________________________________

CULTIVATION PRACTICES (when the bond is supportive)

      Inner dialogue:    intentional conversations at decision points
      Object connection: care for kept objects as ongoing relationship
      Letter writing:    annual or as-needed letters to / from
      Ritual:            anniversaries marked with intentional practice
      Transmitted values: explicit "what they taught me about X"
      Story telling:     keeping their story alive within community

CONNECTION WITH NEW LIFE

    Continuing bonds and new attachments are not in zero-sum.
    Common worry: "If I love someone new, am I betraying the person I lost?"
    Reframe: continuing connection includes the bereaved's continuing
    growth, including new love. The lost person's care for you doesn't
    end at their death; their wishing-you-well includes your new life.

    Where new life feels guilt-laden:
      What permission would the lost person have given me, if asked?
      ___________________________________________________________________

    What ongoing bond can be carried into new life as resource (rather
    than competitor)? ___________________________________________________

CHILDREN AND CONTINUING BONDS

    Children especially benefit from explicit, age-appropriate permission
    to continue the relationship.

      • "It's okay to talk to her. You can imagine what she would say."
      • "It's okay to feel her with you sometimes."
      • Photos visible. Stories told. Memorials kept.
      • Don't enforce "moving on" timelines.

CULTURAL & SPIRITUAL CONTEXT

    Many traditions support continuing bonds explicitly:
      • Day of the Dead / Día de los Muertos (Mexican / Latin American)
      • Obon (Japanese ancestor return)
      • All Souls' Day (Christian)
      • Ancestor veneration (East Asian, African, Indigenous)

    These practices long predate the clinical-theory recognition; the
    continuing-bonds model brings clinical theory into alignment with
    centuries of human practice.
```

> **Operational notes:** Three disciplines. (1) Continuing bonds and Worden's tasks are complementary. The four-tasks frame describes the work; continuing-bonds describes the *form* the relationship takes after the work is done. They aren't competing models. (2) Distinguish supportive from complicating bonds. A bond carrying unresolved guilt or anger needs grief work, not preservation; a bond carrying transmitted love needs cultivation. The audit is the discipline. (3) Permission matters — for self and for those we support. The cultural script "let go" is wrong; replacing it with explicit permission to continue (transformed) connection often produces visible relief. Fourth: continuing bonds work especially well for children, who benefit from concrete forms (photos, stories, "what would Grandma say?") that maintain the relationship as ongoing resource rather than only past.
