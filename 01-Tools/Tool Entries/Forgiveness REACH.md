# REACH Forgiveness Practice

**One-line summary:** A five-step protocol — Recall, Empathize, Altruistic gift, Commit, Hold — for moving through forgiveness work; distinct from reconciliation (which requires the other party), forgiveness is a one-party practice that releases the hurt's grip on the forgiver without requiring approval of the offense.

**When to reach for it:** Persistent rumination about a past hurt; bitterness or resentment that is consuming psychological resources; in clinical work for trauma where forgiveness work is a recognized therapeutic move (with appropriate care); preparing for difficult conversations where reconciliation is being considered; spiritual practice where forgiveness is a religious commitment.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Worthington's foundational distinction: **forgiveness is not reconciliation.** Reconciliation requires the offender to participate (acknowledgment, repair, behavior change); forgiveness is a one-party practice the wronged person undertakes for *their own well-being*. Forgiveness:

- Does NOT require forgetting.
- Does NOT require approving the offense.
- Does NOT require restoring trust or relationship.
- Does NOT require the offender to participate or even know.
- DOES release the hurt's grip on the forgiver's psyche.

The REACH protocol:

1. **Recall the hurt.** Honestly. In detail. Don't sanitize. This is harder than it sounds; many people who claim to have "moved on" are still carrying unprocessed material.
2. **Empathize with the offender.** Not to excuse, but to understand. Try to imagine the conditions, history, or wounds that produced their behavior. This step is for *complexification*, not exoneration.
3. **Altruistic gift of forgiveness.** Recall a time someone forgave you for something significant. Notice what that gift was worth. Forgiveness here is offered as that kind of gift — to yourself first, to the offender second.
4. **Commit publicly.** Tell a witness (trusted person, therapist, journal entry, ritual gesture). Public commitment makes the forgiveness durable.
5. **Hold onto forgiveness.** When the bitterness returns (it will), return to the commitment. Forgiveness is iterative, not a one-time act.

The non-obvious operational claim: **forgiveness is a unilateral move whose benefit accrues to the forgiver.** The offender may never know; may be dead; may still be actively hostile. The forgiver still benefits, measurably (Worthington's clinical research: reductions in stress, anxiety, rumination, blood pressure; improvements in immune function and reported well-being).

A second insight: **the empathy step is the hardest and the most important.** Trying to imagine *why* the offender did what they did (without excusing) breaks the simple-narrative grip that bitterness depends on. Real people are complicated; bitterness requires flat villains.

A third insight: **forgiveness has dimensions of decisional and emotional.** Worthington distinguishes *decisional forgiveness* (the choice / commitment) from *emotional forgiveness* (the felt-release). The decision can be made before the feeling arrives; the feeling often follows the practice rather than preceding it.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the protocol prevents:

1. **Forgiveness as approval.** Conflating forgiveness with sanctioning the offense. The protocol holds these apart explicitly.
2. **Reconciliation-required.** Believing forgiveness requires the offender's participation. Many wronged people cannot heal because the offender is unavailable, hostile, or dead. The unilateral move solves this.
3. **One-and-done forgiveness.** Treating forgiveness as a single declaration. The "Hold" step accommodates the iterative reality.

The framework's evidence base includes 30+ years of clinical trials (Worthington, Enright, McCullough) showing forgiveness interventions reduce psychological symptoms and improve health outcomes. Some critiques: cultural specificity, over-emphasis on individual psychology vs. systemic injustice, risk of premature forgiveness work in trauma contexts.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|  1   | Identify the hurt. Specifically. What was done; by whom; what was lost.        |
|  2   | RECALL. Sit with the hurt fully. Note all the felt-content. Don't sanitize.    |
|      | A therapeutic container helps if the hurt is severe.                            |
|  3   | EMPATHIZE. Imagine the offender's conditions, history, or own wounds. Not to   |
|      | excuse; to complicate the flat-villain narrative. Holds with: this DOES NOT    |
|      | sanction the offense.                                                            |
|  4   | ALTRUISTIC GIFT. Recall a time someone forgave you. What was that worth? Now  |
|      | offer the same kind of gift — first to yourself, then to the offender.          |
|  5   | COMMIT. Tell a witness. Make the commitment concrete: journal entry, letter   |
|      | (sent or not), ritual gesture, conversation with therapist.                     |
|  6   | HOLD. When the bitterness returns, notice it without surprise. Return to the  |
|      | commitment. This is normal; the practice is the holding.                        |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

```
   What happened (specific):

   What is the felt-content NOW when I recall this?
   _____________________________________________________

E — EMPATHIZE (complicate the narrative, do NOT excuse):
   What might have been true about the offender's:
   - Conditions: ___________________________________
   - History:    ___________________________________
   - Own wounds: ___________________________________
   - Limits:     ___________________________________

   This does NOT change my judgment that the offense was wrong.
   It changes the flat-villain narrative to complex-person.

A — ALTRUISTIC GIFT:
   A time someone forgave me (significant): _______________
   What was that gift worth to me? _________________________
   I offer this same kind of gift now —
   first to myself (releasing the grip):    _______________
   then to the offender:                     _______________

C — COMMIT publicly:
   Witness (person, journal, ritual): _______________________
   The commitment, in words: _______________________________

H — HOLD:
   When bitterness returns (it will), I return to:
   _____________________________________________________

   This practice is iterative. Each return is the practice, not failure.

DECISIONAL vs EMOTIONAL forgiveness check-in:
   I have decided to forgive: [ ] yes  [ ] partial  [ ] not yet
   I feel forgiveness:        [ ] yes  [ ] partial  [ ] not yet
   (The decision can precede the feeling; the feeling often follows.)
```

> **Operational notes:** (1) **Forgiveness work is not appropriate for active abuse.** If the offender is still actively harming, the work is safety and protection, not forgiveness. Forgiveness work assumes the harm has stopped or you have separated. (2) **Premature forgiveness can re-traumatize** in trauma contexts. Work with a trauma-informed therapist if severe. (3) **Some offenses may not be forgivable in this framework**, and that's a legitimate outcome — the protocol is a tool, not an obligation. (4) **Forgiveness is independent of restoration of trust.** You may forgive without ever speaking to the person again; you may forgive and reconcile; both are valid.

## Related Tools and Frameworks

- **Loving-Kindness Meditation (Metta)** — pairs as the affective container; the Difficult-person stage of Metta operationalizes the Empathize step.
- **RAIN** — pairs as the acute-emotion handling for residual material.
- **Ignatian Discernment of Spirits** — relevant for the spiritual layer of forgiveness work.
- **NVC Self-Empathy Practice** — pairs as the needs-clarification cousin (what need was violated by the offense).
