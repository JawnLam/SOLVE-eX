# Mom Test

**One-line summary:** A customer-discovery interview method — Rob Fitzpatrick's — built around three rules (talk about their life not your idea; ask about specifics in the past not generics about the future; talk less and listen more) that surface what is actually true about a market by removing the user-validation contamination that destroys early-stage discovery.

**When to reach for it:** Early-stage problem validation (does the problem actually exist in the world?); cold-network conversations where you can't ask leading questions; warm-network conversations where you need to extract behavioral data through the politeness layer; pivot-or-persist decision support; any context where you are about to ask people "would you use this?" and would benefit from a method that gets useful answers instead of nice ones.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**The Mom Test** is a method for getting truthful, decision-useful data from customer-discovery conversations. The procedure derives from three rules Fitzpatrick formalizes:

1. **Talk about THEIR life, not YOUR idea.** The moment you describe your idea, you've contaminated the data — the other person now knows what you want to hear, and politeness compels them to give it. The rule's operational form: never describe the idea in a discovery conversation. Ask about their actual behavior, problems, and workarounds in the relevant domain.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics about the future.** "Would you use an app that tracks screen time?" produces a useless answer because the person hasn't actually faced the decision. "The last time you wondered what your kid was up to on their phone, what did you actually do?" produces a behavioral data point. Past-tense specifics constrain the conversation to behavior that has already happened.
3. **Talk less and listen more.** The conversation's job is to learn what is true about their world, not to validate your hypothesis. If you find yourself explaining your idea, defending it, or asking yes/no questions, the conversation has stopped producing data.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **warm-network conversations are systematically biased by politeness, NOT by lack of insight.** The Mom Test name comes from the canonical case: ask your mom about your business idea and she'll say it's brilliant. That doesn't mean your mom is lying about her opinion of you — it means the conversation's structure prevents her from giving you data. The Mom Test rules restructure the conversation so even your mom would give you useful behavioral data.

A second insight: **commitments and currencies matter more than compliments.** A genuine signal is when the person spends time, money, or social capital — "could you introduce me to the friend you mentioned?" / "would you pay $20 to try a prototype?" / "can we schedule a follow-up in two weeks?" Compliments ("that sounds great!") are noise; commitments are data.

A third insight: **the method works for cold-network and warm-network alike.** Cold-network conversations are honest by default but lack rapport; warm-network conversations have rapport but are biased by politeness. The Mom Test rules are agnostic — they produce useful data in both directions.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "everyone loves it" false positive.** Warm-network conversations almost always produce positive sentiment. Without the Mom Test rules, founders interpret this as validation and proceed; the false positive can burn months of runway before reality intrudes.
2. **The hypothesis-led question trap.** "Would you use X?" / "Does this make sense?" / "Is this a problem you have?" — all yes-leading. The Mom Test rules force questions that don't telegraph the expected answer.
3. **The opinion-vs-behavior conflation.** Opinions about hypothetical futures are unreliable; behavior in the past is data. The Mom Test rules systematically route conversations toward behavioral data.

For founders, product researchers, customer-discovery practitioners, and anyone whose decisions depend on understanding what is actually true about a market or user, the Mom Test is essential — it is the canonical method for the warm-network-bias problem.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Identify the behavioral domain you need data about (not the idea — the domain). |
|    2 | Draft questions that ask about specific past behavior in that domain. Never     |
|      | "would you," always "the last time you did X, what happened?"                    |
|    3 | Before the conversation, prepare a check: would these questions still work if   |
|      | the person had never heard of your idea? If not, rewrite.                       |
|    4 | In conversation, do not describe the idea. If asked, deflect ("I'm trying to    |
|      | learn what's actually going on in this space before I commit").                  |
|    5 | After each answer, probe for specifics: when, what exactly, what did you do      |
|      | next, what would have made it easier, what would have made it harder.            |
|    6 | Listen for commitments and currencies: time, money, social capital, follow-up   |
|      | scheduled. These are the signal. Compliments are noise.                          |
|    7 | After the conversation, write down the behavioral data (not the opinions). One  |
|      | data point per past-behavior reference.                                          |
|    8 | After ≥5 conversations, look for repeated patterns. Patterns are evidence;       |
|      | individual data points are anecdotes.                                            |
|======|=================================================================================|
```
