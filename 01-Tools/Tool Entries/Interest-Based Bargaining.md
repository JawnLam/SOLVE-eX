# Interest-Based Bargaining

**One-line summary:** A negotiation method that distinguishes positions (what parties demand) from interests (the underlying needs) and seeks creative options serving both parties' interests rather than splitting differences between fixed positions.

**When to reach for it:** Any negotiation where the relationship matters beyond a single deal, complex multi-issue negotiations where positional trades are possible, situations where the apparent positions seem irreconcilable but the underlying needs may not be, and labor / partnership / vendor / collaborative negotiations.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton's *Getting to Yes* (1981) presented an alternative to the dominant negotiation approach of the time — **positional bargaining**, in which each side stakes out a claim and concessions are extracted incrementally toward a middle. The authors observed that positional bargaining tends to produce poor outcomes: relationships are damaged, creative options go undiscovered, and the eventual agreement is often worse for both sides than what's structurally possible.

The four principles of **interest-based bargaining** (sometimes called principled negotiation):

1. **Separate the people from the problem** — distinguish the substantive issues from the personal / relational dynamics. Attack the problem, not the person.
2. **Focus on interests, not positions** — a position is what someone demands ("I need $X"); an interest is the underlying need it serves ("I need security / status / liquidity"). Same interests can often be served by multiple positions.
3. **Invent options for mutual gain** — before deciding, brainstorm broadly. Most negotiations underutilize creative options that would benefit both parties.
4. **Insist on objective criteria** — when interests genuinely conflict, anchor the resolution to external standards (market rates, precedent, expert assessment) rather than to who has more leverage.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most negotiation impasses are position-position conflicts, not interest-interest conflicts.** Two parties demanding incompatible positions often have compatible underlying interests, but neither party has surfaced their actual interests, so neither can find the integrative options that would serve both. The classic *Getting to Yes* example: two siblings fighting over an orange. One wants the rind to bake a cake; the other wants the juice. Their *positions* are incompatible (each wants the whole orange); their *interests* are complementary (rind for one, juice for the other). Surfacing interests reveals the integrative solution.

The discipline therefore has a sequence: **never trade positions before you've explored interests.** When the counterparty states a position, ask "what's important about that for you?" — the answer is usually their interest, and it's almost always more flexible than the position.

A second insight: **interests come in layers.** The surface interest (price, terms, scope) often hides deeper interests (security, autonomy, relationship, identity, status). Reaching the deeper layers usually requires trust and time, which is why interest-based bargaining works best in relationships that will continue beyond a single deal.

A third insight: **objective criteria depersonalize hard tradeoffs.** When interests genuinely conflict and one party has to accept less, anchoring to external standards ("the market rate is X," "the standard contract has Y," "the appraisal says Z") makes the result feel fair regardless of the leverage difference. Subjective tradeoffs ("I need this more than you do") rarely close.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The position-trap stalemate.** Negotiations that lock onto positions early ("we need 30% margin," "we need 90 days payment terms") miss the underlying interests. When positions seem irreconcilable, the framework redirects: what's the actual need behind the position? That redirection often reveals integrative options invisible from the position level.
2. **The relationship-damage cost.** Positional bargaining often leaves both parties feeling defeated even after a deal closes — concessions feel like losses, and the next round starts from worse ground. Interest-based bargaining preserves relationships across deals, which compounds in repeated interactions.
3. **The "split the difference" failure.** Splitting differences between positions often produces outcomes nobody actually wanted. Both parties have given up on options that would have served them better. Interest-based bargaining finds those options.

For consulting and partnership work, interest-based bargaining is the discipline that produces durable agreements, in contrast to positional bargaining which produces fragile ones. Most "complete the deal at any cost" negotiations end with one party feeling exploited, which destroys the relationship even if the deal closes.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Before negotiation: list YOUR positions (what you want) and YOUR interests       |
|      | (why you want it). Multiple interests usually underlie each position.            |
|    2 | Try to anticipate the counterparty's positions and interests. What do they say   |
|      | they want? What's the underlying need?                                           |
|    3 | In the negotiation, separate people from problem. Manage the relationship        |
|      | (active listening, acknowledgment, civility) separately from the substantive    |
|      | issues. Attack the problem, not the person.                                      |
|    4 | Surface interests by asking. "What's important about that for you?" "Why does    |
|      | this matter?" "What would this enable?" These questions move from position to    |
|      | interest.                                                                        |
|    5 | Brainstorm options before deciding. Generate many possibilities — including      |
|      | ones outside the original frame. Don't evaluate during brainstorming; that       |
|      | shuts down creativity.                                                           |
|    6 | Map options to interests. Which options serve both parties? These are the       |
|      | integrative candidates.                                                          |
|    7 | When interests genuinely conflict, invoke objective criteria. External           |
|      | benchmarks, precedent, expert assessment, market rates — anchor the resolution   |
|      | to standards both parties accept.                                                |
|    8 | If positional bargaining recurs (counterparty refuses to engage on interests),   |
|      | name the dynamic explicitly. Sometimes the counterparty's strategy IS pure       |
|      | positional bargaining; in that case, BATNA discipline (separate entry) matters  |
|      | more than interest-based moves.                                                  |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FOUR PRINCIPLES (memorize)

   1. SEPARATE THE PEOPLE FROM THE PROBLEM
        Manage the relationship and the substance separately.
        Show respect; attack ideas.

   2. FOCUS ON INTERESTS, NOT POSITIONS
        Position = what they demand
        Interest = why they want it (the underlying need)

   3. INVENT OPTIONS FOR MUTUAL GAIN
        Brainstorm broadly before deciding.
        Most negotiations leave value on the table by failing to
        explore options.

   4. INSIST ON OBJECTIVE CRITERIA
        When interests conflict, use external standards.
        Market rates, precedent, expert assessment, neutral benchmarks.

THE INTEREST-MAPPING TEMPLATE

   YOUR SIDE:
   Position 1: ________________________________________________________
       Interest behind it: ____________________________________________
       Why this interest matters: _____________________________________
       Other ways this interest could be served: ______________________

   Position 2: ________________________________________________________
       Interest behind it: ____________________________________________
       Why this interest matters: _____________________________________
       Other ways this interest could be served: ______________________

   THEIR SIDE (your best estimate):
   Position 1: ________________________________________________________
       Interest behind it (estimated): ________________________________
       Why this interest matters (estimated): _________________________
       Other ways this interest could be served: ______________________

   Position 2: ________________________________________________________
       Interest behind it (estimated): ________________________________
       Why this interest matters (estimated): _________________________
       Other ways this interest could be served: ______________________

   INTEGRATIVE OPTIONS (serve both parties' interests):
   Option A: __________________________________________________________
       Serves your interest in: _______________________________________
       Serves their interest in: ______________________________________

   Option B: __________________________________________________________
       Serves your interest in: _______________________________________
       Serves their interest in: ______________________________________

THE INTEREST-SURFACING QUESTIONS

   When the counterparty states a position, ask:

   1. "What's important about that for you?"
   2. "What would this give you that you don't have now?"
   3. "If you got [stated position], what problem would that solve?"
   4. "What's the underlying need this is meant to address?"
   5. "What are you protecting / preserving / building with this?"
   6. "If we couldn't do exactly this, what other ways could we get
       to what you actually need?"

   These questions are not interrogative — they're curious. The
   counterparty must feel they're being heard, not interrogated. Use
   1-2 of these per exchange, with active acknowledgment of their
   answer.

THE FIVE LAYERS OF INTEREST (often present)

   SURFACE:    Specific terms (price, timeline, scope)
   STRUCTURAL: Organizational needs (cash flow, risk allocation,
                approval processes)
   STRATEGIC:  Broader goals (market position, future flexibility,
                relationship value)
   IDENTITY:   Self-concept and reputation needs (being seen as
                fair, principled, strong, generous)
   EMOTIONAL:  Underlying feelings (security, autonomy, respect,
                belonging)

   Most negotiations surface only the top one or two layers. Reaching
   the lower layers requires trust and time, but is often where
   integrative solutions live.

THE OPTIONS-INVENTION DISCIPLINE

   Before evaluating any option:
       1. Generate at least 6 options.
       2. Include some you'd never propose (boundary options).
       3. Include some that explore expanded scope (add elements,
          combine deals).
       4. Include some that explore reduced scope (remove items
          either side doesn't really need).
       5. Include some that change timing or sequencing.
       6. Include some that introduce third parties or external
          arrangements.

   The act of generating widens the possibility space. Most
   negotiations evaluate too few options and miss the integrative
   ones.

   Brainstorming rule: NO evaluation during generation. Evaluation
   in mid-brainstorm shuts down creativity.

THE OBJECTIVE-CRITERIA INVENTORY

   When interests genuinely conflict, anchor to:

       MARKET RATES — what others are paying / accepting
       PRECEDENT — what we (or others) agreed to before
       EXPERT JUDGMENT — independent appraisal, audit, opinion
       LEGAL STANDARDS — statutory / regulatory benchmarks
       SCIENTIFIC EVIDENCE — research findings, data
       CUSTOM / PRACTICE — what's standard in this industry / context
       EFFICIENCY — what produces the most total value
       FAIRNESS PRINCIPLES — equal treatment, equal sacrifice, etc.

   The objective criterion has to be acceptable to both parties.
   Cherry-picked criteria fail; mutually-acceptable criteria succeed.

WHEN INTEREST-BASED BARGAINING FAILS

   The framework assumes both parties are willing to engage. Some
   failures:

   1. PURE POSITIONAL COUNTERPARTY
        Some negotiators are committed to positional bargaining
        (cultural, training, strategic). Interest-questions get
        answered with restated positions. Defensive move: BATNA
        discipline + objective criteria.

   2. ZERO-SUM SUBSTANCE
        Some genuine disagreements are pure distributional fights —
        there's a fixed amount to divide, no integrative options
        exist. The framework still helps with relationship management
        and objective criteria but doesn't manufacture integrative
        options that don't exist.

   3. POWER ASYMMETRY
        When one side has a much stronger BATNA, they may reasonably
        decline integrative moves and demand more. The framework
        works best when BATNAs are roughly comparable.

   4. INTERESTS DELIBERATELY HIDDEN
        Some counterparties know their interests but won't reveal
        them strategically. Long-term trust-building is the only fix;
        short-term, work with what's visible.

   For these cases: keep the four principles as orientation, but
   rely more heavily on BATNA discipline and objective criteria.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Never trade positions before you've explored interests. The single most leveraged habit in interest-based bargaining is asking "what's important about that for you?" before any concession exchange. The answer is almost always more flexible than the stated position. (2) Brainstorm options before evaluating. Mixed brainstorm-and-evaluate sessions produce few integrative options. Discipline the sequence: generate first, evaluate after, as a separate phase. (3) Use objective criteria when interests genuinely conflict. Subjective tradeoffs ("I need this more") rarely close gaps; mutually-accepted external standards usually do. Build a small library of objective criteria you can invoke at the moment they're needed. (4) Combine with BATNA discipline. Interest-based bargaining works best when both parties have viable alternatives — power asymmetries can override the framework. Always know your BATNA in addition to your interests; the framework and BATNA-ZOPA are complementary, not alternatives.
