# Ikigai Diagram

**One-line summary:** A four-circle Venn diagram showing the intersection of what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for — used to surface vocations that satisfy multiple dimensions of meaning at once.

**When to reach for it:** Career exploration, role redesign, side-project selection, retirement planning, helping a coachee identify high-meaning work, and any case where the question is "what kind of work would integrate purpose, skill, and viability."

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

The Western "Ikigai diagram" is a four-circle Venn:

- **Love** — what you love doing (passion, joy)
- **Good at** — what you're skilled at (mastery, talent)
- **World needs** — what's needed (mission, contribution)
- **Paid for** — what people will pay for (vocation, viability)

Pairwise intersections produce four named states:

- Love × Good at = **Passion**
- Love × World needs = **Mission**
- World needs × Paid for = **Vocation**
- Good at × Paid for = **Profession**

The full four-way intersection is labeled **Ikigai** ("a reason for being"). The implication: a life that integrates all four is most fulfilling.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **most career frustrations come from operating in only 2-3 of the four circles.** A profession (good at + paid for) without love or mission produces burnout. A passion (love + good at) without paid-for or world-need produces struggle. Naming which circles your current work occupies — and which it doesn't — diagnoses the type of dissatisfaction.

A second insight: **the four circles operate on different time scales.** "Love" and "good at" are personal and relatively stable. "World needs" and "paid for" are external and shift with markets, technology, and culture. A job that hit Ikigai a decade ago may now miss because external circles moved.

A critical caveat: **the four-circle Western diagram is not authentic Japanese Ikigai.** Traditional Japanese sources (Kamiya, Mogi) describe Ikigai as small daily reasons to live — morning coffee, garden tending, time with grandchildren — not as career integration. The four-circle diagram is a Western adaptation by Marc Winn (2014) blending Andrés Zuzunaga's earlier Venn with the Japanese term. Japanese scholars (Akihiro Hasegawa) have noted the divergence. Use the diagram as a useful reflection tool; don't claim it represents authentic Japanese tradition.

A third insight: **the diagram is best used as a question bank, not as a goal.** Trying to "find your Ikigai" can paralyze because all four overlap is rare and hard to engineer. Using the diagram to ask "what's missing in my current work" is more tractable and more often actionable.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "passion only" trap.** Pursuing love + good at without world-needs or paid-for produces struggling-artist patterns. Naming the missing circles helps decide whether to add them or accept the trade.
2. **The "profession only" exhaustion.** Operating only in good-at + paid-for produces burnout despite financial success. The diagnosis points to what's missing — usually love or mission — and where to add it.
3. **The "find it, then act" paralysis.** Treating Ikigai as a destination produces inaction. Treating the diagram as a working tool ("what 1-2 hours per week could I add to fill the missing circle?") produces incremental progress.

For coaches, career advisors, and individuals at career inflection points, the Ikigai diagram is a high-utility reflection tool — limitations acknowledged, the structured four-circle prompt produces useful output even when the destination isn't reached.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Draw or fill in the four circles. For each, list 5-10 specific items.            |
|      | Specificity matters: "writing technical analyses" not "communication."          |
|    2 | Identify pairwise intersections. What appears in both Love and Good at? In Love  |
|      | and World needs? Etc. Note overlaps.                                             |
|    3 | Identify your current work's position on the diagram. Which circles does it     |
|      | currently occupy? Which doesn't it?                                              |
|    4 | Diagnose the dissatisfaction (if any). Burnout pattern (good at + paid for, no  |
|      | love)? Struggling pattern (love + good at, no paid for)? Hollow pattern (world   |
|      | needs + paid for, no love)?                                                      |
|    5 | Identify candidate paths in the unfilled intersections. What would add the      |
|      | missing circle without abandoning current strengths?                             |
|    6 | Test small. Don't pursue full Ikigai immediately. Add 1-2 hours per week of an  |
|      | activity that fills the missing circle. Observe over weeks.                     |
|    7 | Iterate. Real Ikigai-seeking is iterative; expect 6-18 months of adjustment.    |
|    8 | Periodic review. External circles shift; what was Ikigai-aligned can drift out  |
|      | over time. Re-do the diagram annually.                                           |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FOUR-CIRCLE INVENTORY

   WHAT I LOVE (5-10 items, specific):
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________

   WHAT I'M GOOD AT (5-10 items, specific):
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________

   WHAT THE WORLD NEEDS (5-10 items, specific):
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________

   WHAT I CAN BE PAID FOR (5-10 items, specific):
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________
       _____________________________________________________

THE PAIRWISE INTERSECTIONS

   LOVE × GOOD AT (Passion):
       What appears in both? __________________________________

   LOVE × WORLD NEEDS (Mission):
       What appears in both? __________________________________

   WORLD NEEDS × PAID FOR (Vocation):
       What appears in both? __________________________________

   GOOD AT × PAID FOR (Profession):
       What appears in both? __________________________________

   3-WAY OVERLAPS:
       LOVE + GOOD AT + PAID FOR (no World Needs):
            "Comfortable but no contribution"
       LOVE + GOOD AT + WORLD NEEDS (no Paid For):
            "Meaningful but financially unsustainable"
       LOVE + WORLD NEEDS + PAID FOR (not Good At):
            "Aspirational but unproven; needs skill-building"
       GOOD AT + WORLD NEEDS + PAID FOR (no Love):
            "Sustainable but draining"

   4-WAY OVERLAP (full Ikigai):
       What appears in ALL four? __________________________

THE CURRENT-WORK DIAGNOSIS

   My current work occupies these circles:
   [ ] Love
   [ ] Good at
   [ ] World needs
   [ ] Paid for

   Diagnosis (which pattern fits):
       4 of 4 → Ikigai-aligned. Watch for drift.
       3 of 4 (no Love) → "Profession exhaustion" pattern.
                          Add love OR accept trade-off.
       3 of 4 (no Paid for) → "Struggling artist" pattern.
                              Add monetization OR accept trade.
       3 of 4 (no Good at) → "Beginner mismatch" pattern.
                              Build skill OR pivot.
       3 of 4 (no World needs) → "Comfortable irrelevance" pattern.
                                  Add mission OR accept.
       2 of 4 → "Significant misalignment" — major redesign or trade.
       1 of 4 → "Nearly all wrong" — significant change required.
       0 of 4 → Investigate immediately.

THE INCREMENTAL-ADDITION DESIGN

   If your current work is missing one circle, design a small
   addition rather than a full pivot:

   Missing: Love
   Action: Add 1-2 hours/week of activity you love within current
           role context (a project, side initiative, mentoring,
           creative time).

   Missing: World needs
   Action: Volunteer in or contribute to a domain you care about,
           1-4 hours/week. Builds the missing dimension without
           leaving current job.

   Missing: Paid for
   Action: Side project or moonlighting that monetizes a skill or
           passion. Tests viability without full pivot.

   Missing: Good at
   Action: Deliberate skill-building (course, mentorship, deliberate
           practice) — typically 3-6 months before yielding shift.

   Most successful Ikigai redesigns happen incrementally over
   years, not via single big-bang career changes.

THE EXTERNAL-CIRCLE-DRIFT WARNING

   "World needs" and "paid for" shift over time:
   - Industries change; what's needed now may be obsolete in 10 years
   - Markets pay differently for the same skill across decades
   - Cultural valuation of professions shifts

   Implication: Ikigai isn't permanent. Re-do the diagram every
   year or two; what was aligned can drift out.

   For early-career: bias toward "good at" and "paid for" while
   exploring "love" and "mission."
   For mid-career: bias toward "love" and "world needs" if first
   two are stable.
   For late-career: bias toward "love" and "world needs" with
   minimal "paid for" required.

THE JAPANESE-SOURCE CAVEAT

   Traditional Japanese Ikigai (Kamiya, Mogi):
   - Daily small joys count (morning routine, hobbies, family time)
   - Doesn't require career integration
   - Doesn't require monetization
   - Often emerges in late life (retirement, reduced demands)

   Western 4-circle Ikigai (Winn, Zuzunaga):
   - Career-focused
   - Includes monetization criterion
   - Aimed at finding vocational sweet spot

   These are DIFFERENT concepts. The Western diagram is a useful
   tool but not authentic to the Japanese tradition. If you find
   the 4-circle approach too transactional, the original Japanese
   concept (small daily reasons-for-being) may serve better.

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. SEEKING THE 4-WAY OVERLAP IMMEDIATELY
        "I need to find all four overlapping right now." Recovery:
        accept incremental addition; full overlap is rare and slow.

   2. VAGUE INVENTORY
        "I love connecting with people" is too vague to act on.
        Recovery: specific items ("teaching small groups," not
        "connecting with people").

   3. STATIC TREATMENT
        Treating the diagram as one-time exercise. Recovery:
        annual re-do; external circles shift.

   4. CONFLATING WITH JAPANESE TRADITION
        "Ikigai" as a career concept is Western adaptation, not
        authentic Japanese. Recovery: use the tool without making
        cultural-tradition claims you can't support.

   5. PARALYSIS BY NON-INTERSECTION
        "Nothing appears in all four circles." Recovery: most
        people's Ikigai is in 3-way overlaps, not 4-way. Aim for
        3, hope for 4.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) The diagram is a question bank, not a destination. Use it to diagnose what's missing in current work, not to require finding the perfect 4-way intersection. The latter often produces paralysis. (2) Specific items beat vague ones. "I love writing technical case studies" is actionable; "I love communicating" isn't. The inventory's value depends on its specificity. (3) Add incrementally. Most successful Ikigai redesigns happen through years of small additions, not single big-bang career changes. Add 1-2 hours per week of a missing circle; observe; iterate. (4) Acknowledge the source caveat. The Western 4-circle diagram is a popularization, not authentic Japanese tradition. Use the tool's utility without making tradition claims you can't support.
