# Feynman Technique

**One-line summary:** A four-step learning method attributed to Richard Feynman: choose a concept, explain it in plain language as if to a 12-year-old, identify gaps where your explanation breaks down, study those gaps, and simplify with analogies — exposing pseudo-understanding that jargon usually conceals.

**When to reach for it:** Studying complex technical material; preparing to teach; onboarding to a new domain; testing whether you actually understand something or just have surface familiarity; communication-skill development; and any context where the difference between knowing-the-words and understanding matters.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

**Feynman Technique** uses plain-language explanation as a learning test. The four steps:

1. **Choose a concept.** Be specific.
2. **Explain it on paper or aloud as if to a 12-year-old.** Use simple language; avoid jargon. If you must use a technical term, define it in plain words.
3. **Identify gaps.** Where does your explanation break down? Where do you reach for jargon as a shortcut? Where do you have to say "and then magic happens"?
4. **Go back and study the gaps. Then simplify with analogy.** Find ways to convey the concept that work for a non-expert listener.

The non-obvious operational insight is that **jargon often masks not-knowing.** Saying "this is a Markov decision process with reward shaping under partial observability" sounds expert; if you can't explain what each phrase actually means and how they connect, you've labeled rather than understood. The plain-language constraint exposes the difference.

A second insight: **the technique is iterative.** First explanation reveals gaps; second explanation incorporates new study; gaps shrink. Iteration converges toward genuine understanding.

A third insight: **the technique pairs with Einstein's reported standard.** "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." (Probably misattributed but the principle stands.) The Feynman Technique operationalizes this standard.

A fourth insight: **the framework also produces communication skill.** A side effect of practicing plain-language explanation is becoming better at explaining things to others. The teacher must understand to teach; the technique builds both.

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the framework prevents:

1. **The "I read it; I know it" illusion.** Reading produces surface familiarity that feels like understanding. The Feynman test exposes the gap.
2. **The "jargon-as-knowing" trap.** Memorizing terminology can substitute for actual understanding; the plain-language constraint distinguishes them.
3. **The "expert-curse" communication failure.** Experts often can't explain to novices because they've forgotten the basics; practicing simple explanation maintains accessibility.

For students, autodidacts, technical professionals, teachers, and anyone whose work depends on actually understanding (not just being able to recite), the Feynman Technique is foundational learning discipline.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Choose a specific concept to learn or test.                                      |
|    2 | Get a piece of paper or imaginary 12-year-old listener.                          |
|    3 | Explain the concept in plain language. No jargon (or define every term).        |
|    4 | Notice gaps: where you stumble, reach for jargon, or skip to "magic."           |
|    5 | Study those gaps specifically. Read, ask, watch, work problems.                 |
|    6 | Re-explain. Has the gap closed?                                                   |
|    7 | Iterate until explanation is clean and complete.                                |
|    8 | Add analogies — connections to familiar concepts the listener already knows.   |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
THE FOUR-STEP METHOD

   STEP 1: CHOOSE
       Specific concept, not vague topic.
       "Bayes' theorem," not "probability."

   STEP 2: EXPLAIN SIMPLY
       Imagine listener is a curious 12-year-old.
       Plain language. No jargon.
       If you use a term, define it.

   STEP 3: IDENTIFY GAPS
       Where did you stumble?
       Where did you reach for jargon as shortcut?
       Where did you say "and then..."?
       Where would the 12-year-old ask "but why?"
       and you'd be stuck?

   STEP 4: STUDY + SIMPLIFY
       Go back to source material on the gaps.
       Find analogies — what familiar thing is this
       like?
       Re-explain with the analogy.
       Iterate.

THE GAP-DETECTION SIGNALS

   Signs you're hitting a gap:
       Reaching for jargon ("it's basically a stochastic
       process")
       Saying "you'd need to take a course on..."
       "It's complicated" without complication
       "Trust me, this works"
       Hand-waving over a step
       Defining terms with other jargon
       Visible discomfort

   Signs you've closed the gap:
       Plain language flows naturally
       Connections to familiar concepts work
       12-year-old's likely questions can be answered
       You can use analogies without confusion

THE WORKED EXAMPLE — BAYES' THEOREM

   Step 1: Choose Bayes' theorem.

   Step 2 (first attempt):
       "Bayes' theorem says P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B).
       It's a rule for updating probabilities given new
       evidence."
       
       Gap: this defines symbols but doesn't explain
       why the formula or what "updating probabilities"
       means in practice.

   Step 3: gaps include:
       What does "given" mean precisely?
       Why does the formula take that specific form?
       How would I use it?
       What's the intuition?

   Step 4: study + analogize:
       Analogy: imagine medical testing for a rare
       disease. 1 in 1000 has the disease. The test is
       95% accurate.
       
       If you test positive, what's the probability you
       have the disease?
       
       Naive: 95%. (False!)
       Bayes: combines prior probability (1/1000) with
       test accuracy (95%).
       
       Walk through the calculation in plain numbers:
       1000 people, 1 has disease. Test correctly
       identifies them (positive). 999 don't. Test
       wrongly says positive on 5% of them = 50.
       
       So among "tests positive," 1 actual case + 50
       false positives = 51. Probability of disease
       given positive test = 1/51 ≈ 2%.
       
       The "Bayes" insight: the rare disease's low base
       rate dominates; even an accurate test on a rare
       condition produces mostly false positives.

   Step 2 (second attempt with analogy):
       "Bayes' theorem helps you update what you believe
       when you get new evidence. It's especially
       important when the thing you're testing for is
       rare. Here's an example..."
       [walks through the medical-testing example]

   The second explanation is clear; the gap closed.

THE ANALOGIES POWER

   Analogies are the conceptual bridge from unfamiliar
   to familiar:

   "DNA is like a recipe book."
   "Memory hierarchy is like a desk + filing cabinet +
   off-site storage."
   "Cells communicate via chemical messengers, like
   sending letters in the body's postal system."

   Good analogies:
       Connect to genuinely familiar concepts
       Highlight the right structural similarity
       Acknowledge where the analogy breaks down

   Bad analogies:
       Connect to other unfamiliar concepts
       Mislead about the structure
       Take the analogy too literally

THE COMMON FAILURE MODES

   1. JARGON CHEATING
        Using technical terms without defining them.
        Recovery: every term gets plain-language
        definition.

   2. SHALLOW EXPLANATION
        Description without mechanism. "X causes Y"
        without why. Recovery: probe one level
        deeper.

   3. SKIPPING THE GAPS
        Acknowledging gaps but not closing them.
        Recovery: study + re-explain.

   4. PERFORMATIVE SIMPLICITY
        Sounds simple but actually elides important
        complexity. Recovery: include the structural
        details that matter.

   5. ANALOGY OVER-RELIANCE
        Analogies replace understanding. Recovery:
        analogies illustrate; understanding is
        underneath.

   6. NO LISTENER (REAL OR IMAGINED)
        Explaining to no one; missing the listener-
        questions that drive depth. Recovery: real or
        imagined naive listener.
```

> **Operational notes:** Four disciplines. (1) Plain language is the test. The discipline is refusing jargon shortcuts. Each technical term either gets a plain-language definition or comes out of the explanation. (2) Identify gaps honestly. The gaps are where the learning happens; pretending they don't exist defeats the technique. Mark them; study them; re-explain. (3) Use analogies, but don't replace understanding with them. Analogies bridge unfamiliar to familiar; they're scaffolding. Underneath the analogy, real structural understanding should be present. (4) Iterate. First explanation is rarely the final one; the technique is iterative. Each round of explain-gaps-study-re-explain consolidates understanding.
