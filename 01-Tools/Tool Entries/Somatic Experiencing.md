# Somatic Experiencing

**One-line summary:** A body-oriented trauma therapy that treats trauma as incomplete protective response stored in the nervous system, and works gently — via titration, pendulation, and bodily completion — to release the held activation rather than re-experience the event.

**When to reach for it:** Trauma residue (single-incident or developmental) that hasn't yielded to talk therapy; chronic stress patterns with strong somatic component; preparatory work before deeper trauma processing; or as a complement to other modalities for embodied integration.

---

## Purpose Of This Thinking Tool

Peter Levine's central observation: animals that face mortal threat in the wild typically *don't* develop PTSD-like patterns. Humans often do. The difference, Levine proposed, is that animals complete the protective response (fight, flight, freeze, then a discharge — often visible tremor — followed by orientation and re-engagement). Humans frequently interrupt the cycle through cognitive override, social inhibition, or because the threat persisted past the body's capacity. The held activation persists as trauma symptoms.

The non-obvious operational insight is *titration*: working with a small piece of the activation at a time, alternating between contact with the difficult material (*pendulation*) and contact with bodily resources (places of comfort, support, ground). This prevents re-traumatization and allows the nervous system to slowly metabolize the held activation. Successful processing often involves small involuntary movements — a tremor in the legs, a deep spontaneous breath, a sigh, gentle shaking — which Levine interprets as the discharge of completed protective response.

The work is body-led, not narrative-led. The story of the trauma may or may not be the focus; the *physiology* of the held response is. This makes SE accessible to clients for whom narrative re-telling is re-traumatizing or impossible (preverbal trauma, dissociative defenses).

## Why Use This Thinking Tool

Three failure modes the somatic approach addresses:

1. **Talk therapy plateau.** Many trauma clients reach a point where additional narrative processing doesn't help; the residue is in the body, not the story.
2. **Re-traumatization risk.** Direct exposure work without nervous-system regulation can re-traumatize. Titration prevents this.
3. **Cognitive bypass.** Clients who intellectualize trauma may understand much without changing physiologically. Body-led work bypasses the intellectualization.

For coaching and self-development contexts (with clinical-scope caveats), basic SE moves — orientation, resourcing, pendulation — are useful regulating skills even outside trauma contexts.

## How To Use This Thinking Tool

```
|======|=================================================================================|
| Step |                                     Action                                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
|    1 | Establish *resource*: a place in the body or imagination that feels safe,       |
|      | grounded, calm. Spend time there.                                               |
|    2 | *Orient* — slow, deliberate looking around. Engage neck and eyes. Where in      |
|      | the room feels safe? Let the system register present-time safety.               |
|    3 | Bring attention to the activation — *titrated*. A small piece, briefly. Don't  |
|      | flood. Notice sensation: where, what kind, what intensity.                      |
|    4 | *Pendulate* — when activation grows, return to resource. Then back to           |
|      | activation. The alternation is the therapeutic move.                            |
|    5 | Notice spontaneous bodily completions — small tremors, deep breaths, sighs,    |
|      | postural shifts, micro-movements. Let them complete; don't suppress.            |
|    6 | After processing, take time to integrate. Hydrate, walk, return slowly.         |
|    7 | For trauma work, do this with an SE-trained practitioner. Self-application is  |
|      | scoped to general regulation, not trauma processing alone.                      |
|======|=================================================================================|
```

## The Actual Thinking Tool

```
RESOURCING WORKSHEET (build first, before any activation work)

    A place in your body that feels resourced (calm, supported, grounded):
      Location:     ___________________________________________________
      Quality:      ___________________________________________________

    A felt-sense imagery resource (a remembered or imagined place / person / animal):
      ___________________________________________________________________

    A movement that feels supportive (e.g., hand on chest, feet on ground):
      ___________________________________________________________________

    A breath pattern that settles you:
      ___________________________________________________________________

    Practice returning to these resources several times daily, BEFORE you
    need them. The goal is for the system to know the path.

ORIENTATION PRACTICE (~5 min, daily)

    1. Slowly turn the head, letting the eyes lead. Look around the room.
    2. Notice three things that feel safe or neutral.
    3. Notice the felt experience of registering safety — does posture shift?
       Breath deepen?
    4. Repeat — slow pace, let the system integrate.

    Effect: trains the social engagement system (Porges' ventral vagal),
    counters the threat-scanning bias common in trauma.

TITRATION & PENDULATION TEMPLATE (with practitioner support)

    Activation focus (small piece): _______________________________________

    Sensation in body: ____________________________________________________
    Intensity (0–10):  _____

    Pendulate to resource:  ______________________________________________
    Sensation now:                                          intensity: ___

    Return to activation, briefly:                          intensity: ___
    Notice any spontaneous body movement: __________________________________

    Pendulate again. Repeat 3–6 times.

    What completed (if anything)? Tremor, deep breath, sigh, posture shift?
      ___________________________________________________________________

    Closing: extended resource time. Notice what's different from start.

INTEGRATION SIGNS (the system has metabolized something)

      • Spontaneous deep breath / sigh
      • Visible tremor in legs, hands, or whole body — let it complete
      • Postural shift (often: shoulders drop, neck lengthens)
      • Sense of "coming back" — present moment registers more vividly
      • Mild fatigue followed by genuine rest (not collapse)

WHEN TO ENGAGE A PRACTITIONER

    SE for trauma processing requires a trained practitioner. Self-application
    is appropriate for:
      [ ] Daily orientation and resourcing practice
      [ ] General regulation skills
      [ ] Integration after therapy sessions (with practitioner consent)

    NOT appropriate alone:
      [ ] Active trauma processing
      [ ] Severe dissociation
      [ ] Suicidality or acute crisis
      [ ] Re-engaging traumatic memory

CAUTIONS

    [ ] Don't push activation. The discipline is small doses; flooding
        re-traumatizes.
    [ ] Spontaneous discharge (tremor) is normal — don't suppress, don't
        amplify; let it run its course.
    [ ] Some clients have difficulty *feeling* the body initially. This is
        often dissociation; work with a practitioner to gently re-establish
        body contact.
```

> **Operational notes:** Three disciplines. (1) Resource first, always. Without resource, there's no "home base" to pendulate back to; activation work without resource is just exposure and risks re-traumatization. The discipline of building resource before any activation work is non-negotiable. (2) Trust the body's wisdom. The micro-movements, sighs, and tremors are not "noise" — they're the discharge of held activation. The SE move is to give them room and not interpret/judge. (3) Stay small. SE's slogan is "less is more" — a small, brief contact with the activation, alternated with resource, is more effective than long exposure. The temptation, especially for clients with trauma backgrounds and avoidant defenses, is to push through; this counter-instinct is what makes the practice work. Fourth: the body-led approach complements, not replaces, narrative work. Many clients integrate by alternating somatic processing with narrative reflection — what came up in the body becomes meaning in the story.
