- # PHASE 0 - Using S.O.L.V.E. eX.
  - # Introduction.
    - The S.O.L.V.E. eX. method aims to enhance individual and team thinking skills and productivity. It is grounded in two key habits of outstanding thinkers:
      - 1. They take a structured problem solving approach to address the issue at hand.
      - 2. They use appropriate questions and thinking tools.
    - # Take a structured problem solving approach to address the issue at hand.
    - To be an exceptional thinker, it's not just about asking the right questions but also using a structured problem solving process. A structured problem-solving process is like a mental framework that guides how we think. It's a sequence of steps, and skipping any can lead to ineffective thinking. For example:
      - The phase of your problem solving influences the questions you ask.
      - The questions you ask determine the kind of information you collect.
      - The information you gather shapes how you analyze the situation.
      - Your analysis leads to specific conclusions.
      - These conclusions guide the creation of ideas to be passed onto the next phase in the problem-solving process.
      - Finally, your ideas and solutions drive the actions you take.
    - As you can see, everything starts with understanding where you are in the problem solving process. Misjudging this or jumping ahead can disrupt the flow of effective thinking. Each phase in the problem-solving process serves a purpose, building upon the previous step to ensure a comprehensive approach. Thus, recognizing and respecting the importance of each phase is crucial for successful problem-solving. For instance, rushing into solution development without fully understanding the problem can lead to suboptimal outcomes or missed opportunities. Similarly, gathering data without a clear idea of what information is needed can lead to analysis paralysis or irrelevant conclusions. By meticulously following each step of a structured problem-solving process, you ensure that your thinking is systematic, thorough, and aligned with the goal at hand. This approach minimizes errors, enhances clarity, and leads to more innovative and effective solutions.
    - The S.O.L.V.E. eX. method is structured into six distinct phases, each with specific steps.
      - PHASE 1: "S" - Articulate The Current STATE.
        - Step 1.1 - Describe The Situation.
        - Step 1.2 - Write Problem Statement.
        - Step 1.3 - Frame The Situation.
      - PHASE 2: "O" - Define The Desired OBJECTIVE.
        - Step 2.1 - Reasons To Resolve.
        - Step 2.2 - Write The Goal Statement.
        - Step 2.3 - Establish The Requirements.
      - PHASE 3: "L" - LEARN The Facts And Data.
        - Step 3.1 - Finding Current Facts And Gathering Current Data.
        - Step 3.2 - Finding Past Facts And Gathering Past Current Data.
        - Step 3.3 - Finding Systems Facts And Gathering Systems Current Data.
        - Step 3.4 - Finding Future Facts And Gathering Future Current Data.
      - PHASE 4: "V" - VISION: Create An Abundant Inventory Of Divergent Ideas.
        - Step 4.1 - Analyses Of Root Causes For Possible Backtracking, Revisitation, Or Reversion.
        - Step 4.2 - Expansive Idea Formation for Creative Solution Exploration.
        - Step 4.3 - Anticipative Solution Crafting for Future State Management.
        - Step 4.4 - Idea Refinement for Practical Implementation and Organizational Integration.
      - PHASE 5: "E" - EVALUATE The Inventory Of Options.
        - Step 5.1 - Define the Evaluation Criteria.
        - Step 5.2 - Select The Right Decision Tools.
        - Step 5.3 - Validate Decision Impact and Execution.
      - PHASE 6: "eX" - eXECUTE The Chosen Solution.
        - Step 6.1 - Designing And Testing The Solution.
        - Step 6.2 - Creating An Action Plan.
        - Step 6.3 - Implementing The Action Plan.
        - Step 6.4 - Conducting Follow-up And Taking Feedback.
      - Each phase begins with a decision point to determine its necessity. If a phase is deemed unnecessary, the process moves to the decision point of the next phase. Additionally, there are facilitators and coaches of this process who should employ loopback mechanisms for reassessment, allowing a return to previous phases for reevaluation.
    - This structured, iterative approach enables comprehensive problem-solving, from understanding and framing the problem to evaluating options and implementing solutions, ensuring thoroughness and adaptability in addressing complex business challenges.
  - # They use appropriate questions and thinking tools.
  - S.O.L.V.E. eX. is a method to improve thinking and problem-solving by asking the right questions. It's based on the idea that effective thinkers ask great questions but might not always remember them when needed. So, S.O.L.V.E. eX. provides a list of questions for each phase of problem-solving.
  - Here's a simplified guide to using it:
  - 1. Identify your current phase in the 6-phase problem-solving process.
  - 2. Pinpoint the specific step in the phase you are at.
  - 3. Choose from three types of questions:
    - Exploratory Assessment: These questions help analyze and understand the problem in depth. They focus on defining the problem, its effects, and the factors causing it.
    - Practicable Analysis: This set focuses on the practical aspects of solving the problem. It includes questions about immediate actions, decision-making, and implementation.
    - Critical Evaluation: These questions aim to critically assess solutions for their long-term viability and broader impact.
  - 4. Be judicious in asking questions – too many can be counterproductive.
  - 5. Get familiar with the Thinking Tool associated with the questions and consider other Recommended Thinking Tools. These tools include analytic techniques, conversation guides, data collection templates, and activity worksheets with expert facilitation questions.
- # PHASE 1 - Articulate The Current STATE.
  - ## Introduction to Phase 1.
    - Defining the current state is essential to our structured approach to business problem-solving. This module is fundamentally designed to enhance your ability to accurately assess and articulate the present state of any business challenge you encounter. In problem-solving, understanding and defining the current state is as crucial as envisioning the solution.
    - How we perceive and define problems often sets the stage for our solutions. Too frequently, in the rush to address issues, there's a tendency to misidentify the problem or create solutions for the wrong issues. This misstep can lead to ineffective strategies, wasted resources, and missed opportunities. Therefore, investing time and thought into thoroughly understanding the problem is vital before leaping into solution mode.
    - In this phase, our focus is to cultivate a disciplined approach to defining problems, structured into three key steps:
      - Step 1.1 - Describe the Situation: The first step is developing a deep understanding of the situation. This involves gathering detailed information about the issue at hand, examining the context in which it exists, and understanding its nuances. We’ll explore techniques to observe and collect relevant data, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of the situation.
      - Step 1.2 - Write the Problem Statement: Once you grasp the situation, the next step is to articulate the problem. Writing a problem statement is a critical skill in business problem-solving. It requires clarity, precision, and a focus on the core issue. We’ll guide you through crafting a statement that succinctly captures the essence of the problem, avoiding common pitfalls like vagueness or overgeneralization.
      - Step 1.3 - Frame the Situation: The final step is framing the situation within a broader context. This includes considering the implications of the problem, its impact on various stakeholders, and how it aligns with organizational objectives. Framing the situation is about seeing the bigger picture and understanding the problem's place within it. This broader perspective is crucial in developing practical, sustainable solutions.
    - By the end of this phase, you will be equipped with the tools and insights to define the current state of business problems accurately. This skill is crucial for developing effective strategies and ensuring that your problem-solving efforts are directed appropriately, laying a solid foundation for future success in your managerial endeavors. Let’s begin this journey of sharpening our problem-definition skills to become more effective and strategic business problem-solvers.
  - ## Step 1.1 - Describe The Situation.
    - ### Intro.
      - The first step in problem-solving is to create a brief background description of the present situation or problem. This description aids in comprehending the characteristics, extent, and possible effects. It should highlight the unique aspects of the situation to prevent relying on generic solutions.
      - When addressing complex business problems, it's crucial to have a clear understanding of the situation at hand. A structured approach to defining the problem can lead to more effective and tailored solutions. This can be achieved by using facilitator questions to guide the writing of a concise background description. Keep the following key concepts in mind as you select which questions to address:
      - 1. Identify Key Facets of the Situation: Use facilitator questions to uncover the essential elements of the current situation or problem. Questions should explore the who, what, when, where, why, and how of the situation. This helps in gaining a comprehensive understanding of the problem.
      - 2. Assess the Scope and Impact: Evaluate the extent of the problem and its potential impact on different areas of the business. Understand how it affects various stakeholders, processes, and the organization's goals. This assessment helps in prioritizing the issue and planning an appropriate response.
      - 3. Highlight Uniqueness: Focus on identifying what makes this situation unique. Understanding the specific characteristics that differentiate this problem from others prevents falling into the trap of applying generic, 'one-size-fits-all' solutions. This encourages more creative and effective problem-solving.
      - 4. Write a Concise Background Description: Based on the insights gained, write a brief background description of the problem. This description should be clear, focused, and encompass all critical aspects identified. It serves as a reference point for the problem-solving process, ensuring that all discussions and solutions are relevant and targeted.
      - 5. Encourage Broad Participation: Encourage participation from all attendees in a workshop setting. Diverse perspectives can add depth to the problem understanding and contribute to more innovative solutions.
      - This approach ensures that the problem is thoroughly understood before jumping into solutions. By focusing on a clear, concise background description shaped by facilitator questions, managers can approach problem-solving with a more strategic and effective mindset, leading to innovative solutions and closely aligned with the unique aspects of the problem.

    - ### Questions.
      - #### Group A: Exploratory Assessment for Situation Description.
        - In this group, the focus is on thoroughly examining the situation to create a detailed background description. Questions are designed to help understand the unique nature of the problem, its scale, and the various systemic factors at play. This investigative approach aims to unearth the situation's underlying dimensions. The goal is to craft a narrative that captures the essence of the problem, highlighting its distinct characteristics and setting the stage for tailored solutions.
        - Is the current situation a problem, an issue, or an opportunity?
        - When did this situation (problem) first appear? How quickly is it spreading?
        - Why hasn't the problem already been solved? Might time alone resolve it?
        - What are the main conflicts or disagreements?
        - Who was involved when this situation/problem started?
        - What factors have contributed most to the situation?
        - What makes this problem unique?
        - What is the likely cause of this situation?
        - From what viewpoint can this situation be seen better?
        - Who gains from this problem/situation? Who loses?
        - Where is the problem most (or least) noticeable?
        - How does this situation affect different stakeholders within and outside the organization?
        - Are there any historical precedents or parallels to this situation?
        - How is this situation perceived by different levels within the organization?
        - What potential long-term developments could arise from this situation if left unaddressed?
        - How does this situation align or conflict with the organization's values and mission?
        - Are there external factors (like market trends or regulatory changes) influencing this situation?
        - What are the short-term and long-term risks associated with this situation?
        - How does this situation interrelate with other challenges or opportunities the organization is facing?
        - What insights or lessons can be drawn from similar situations in other organizations or industries?
        - How does this situation impact employee morale and organizational culture?
        - What indirect effects might this situation have on other projects or initiatives?
        - How do current market conditions or industry trends influence this situation?
        - What hidden aspects of this situation haven't been fully explored yet?
        - How does this situation interact with the organization’s existing strategic plans?
        - Are there any legal or compliance-related factors to consider in this situation?
        - What are the communication challenges associated with this situation?
        - How does this situation differ in various geographic or demographic segments?
        - What is the potential for this situation to escalate if not addressed promptly?
      - #### Group B: Practicable Analysis for Situation Description.
        - Questions in this group are centered on the operational context of the situation, aiding in the development of a pragmatic background description. They explore the immediate realities and practicalities of the situation, such as identifying key players, understanding existing limitations, and outlining the scope of what can be realistically achieved. This analysis is critical for painting a clear picture of the current state of affairs, providing a grounded basis for subsequent problem-solving steps.
        - What needs immediate attention?
        - Who is the problem owner?
        - What relevant person thinks this is not a problem at all?
        - Is this a one-time situation that is not likely to happen again?
        - What aspects of this situation/problem are beyond your/team's control?
        - When will the problem get better (or worse)?
        - What are the biggest obstacles to solving the problem?
        - Is the situation being exaggerated or promoted to serve another purpose?
        - Is the situation best served with problem-solving thinking or opportunity thinking?
        - What specific resources are required to address this situation?
        - How has the organization responded to similar situations in the past?
        - What are the immediate financial implications of this situation?
        - How does this situation impact the organization's operational efficiency?
        - What role do technological factors play in this situation?
        - How might different solution strategies affect different departments or teams?
        - What time frame is feasible for addressing this situation?
        - How can communication about this situation be managed within and outside the organization?
        - What are the most significant barriers to immediate action in this situation?
        - What specific skill sets are required to address this situation effectively?
        - How do current organizational policies and procedures influence this situation?
        - What is the impact of this situation on customer satisfaction and engagement?
        - How does this situation affect supply chain and vendor relationships?
        - What are the immediate and medium-term budgetary considerations for this situation?
        - How does this situation affect the organization's competitive positioning?
        - What are the potential technological solutions or innovations that could be leveraged?
        - What internal or external expertise is needed to address this situation?
        - How can we ensure agility and flexibility in responding to this situation?
      - #### Group C: Critical Evaluation for Situation Description.
        - In this category, the emphasis is on critically appraising the situation to enhance the background description. Questions guide the problem-solver in considering the broader context and future implications of the situation. This involves probing into the deeper motivations, potential biases, and possible long-term outcomes of the current scenario. The aim is to ensure that the description of the situation is comprehensive and forward-looking, taking into account the sustainability and ethical dimensions, as well as its alignment with the organization’s future trajectory.
        - What is acceptable about this situation? What is unacceptable?
        - Is this situation just noise and a waste of time to work on?
        - If you did nothing, what is the worst thing that could happen?
        - How will you know when you have resolved the situation/problem?
        - Should the situation be helped, ignored, fought, suppressed, exposed, or hidden?
        - What is gained by not resolving the situation? What is lost by solving the situation?
        - How does this situation reflect the organization's internal culture and dynamics?
        - What ethical dilemmas does this situation present?
        - How might personal or departmental biases affect the perception and handling of this situation?
        - In what ways could resolving this situation impact the organization's long-term strategy?
        - What would be the impact on stakeholder trust and relationships if the situation is not adequately addressed?
        - How could this situation evolve in the next five to ten years?
        - What are the implications of this situation for the organization's reputation and brand image?
        - How does this situation fit into the broader industry context and trends?
        - What lessons can the organization learn from this situation for future strategic planning?
        - How does this situation challenge or reinforce the organization's core values?
        - What are the long-term implications of this situation for employee development and retention?
        - How could different approaches to this situation affect the organization's social responsibility?
        - What are the potential ethical conflicts presented by this situation?
        - How might resolving this situation influence future leadership and governance within the organization?
        - What are the potential impacts on the organization's long-term financial health?
        - How could this situation shape public perception and media narratives about the organization?
        - What are the long-term technological implications or requirements related to this situation?
        - How might this situation inform future risk management and contingency planning?
  - ## Step 1.2 - Write Problem Statement.
    - ### Intro.
      - Creating an effective problem statement is perhaps the most critical step in problem-solving. A problem that is accurately and clearly defined directs people's thoughts, efforts, and activities effectively. A specific and well-articulated statement aids in correct diagnosis and resolution of the problem. Conversely, a vague problem statement leads to scattered and unfocused thinking.
      - In the context of problem-solving, it's essential to emphasize the importance of a well-crafted problem statement. Developing this statement is crucial as it sets the tone for all subsequent steps in problem-solving. Keep the following key concepts in mind as you select which questions to address:
      - 1. Understanding the Role of a Problem Statement: Begin by highlighting the significance of an accurate problem statement. It's the foundation for practical problem-solving, guiding the focus and direction of all efforts.
      - 2. Characteristics of an Effective Problem Statement: Discuss the elements that make a problem statement effective. It should be clear, concise, specific, and focused. Avoid technical jargon to ensure it is understandable by all stakeholders.
      - 3. Techniques for Crafting a Problem Statement: Introduce problem statement methodologies. This might include brainstorming sessions, the '5 Whys' technique for root cause analysis, and seeking inputs from various stakeholders to gain diverse perspectives.
      - 4. Common Pitfalls to Avoid: Warn against vagueness and over-complexity in problem statements. Explain how these can lead to misdirected efforts and inefficient use of resources.
      - 5. Engagement Activities: Engage participating problem-solvers in exercises to practice formulating problem statements. Use real-life scenarios or past challenges within the organization for practical application.
      - 6. Evaluating Problem Statements: Teach how to assess a problem statement for clarity and specificity critically. Encourage peer review and discussion to refine these statements.
      - 7. Alignment with Business Goals: Emphasize the importance of aligning problem statements with broader organizational objectives. This ensures that solving the problem contributes to the company's overall strategy.
      - 8. Documenting and Communicating the Problem Statement: Discuss best practices for documenting and communicating the problem statement effectively to all relevant parties. Clarity in communication prevents misunderstandings and aligns team efforts.
      - By expanding on the concept of a problem statement in this structured manner, problem-solvers will be equipped with the tools and understanding necessary to approach problem-solving more effectively. This ensures that their efforts are targeted, efficient, and aligned with the organization's strategic goals.

  - ## Step 1.3 - Frame The Situation.
    - ### Intro.
      - Usually, we are trained to think analytically in a way that views problems as separate entities. Yet, in our increasingly interconnected world, problems are rarely isolated; instead, they exist on a continuous spectrum that is constantly evolving. This set of questions offers a broader perspective on where the problem lies, ensuring that the solutions developed benefit the entire situation.
      - In a problem-solving setting for business managers, it's crucial to cultivate a mindset that looks beyond traditional analytical thinking, which often isolates problems. Keep the following key concepts in mind as you select which questions to address:
      - 1. Understanding Interconnectedness: Begin by highlighting the interconnected nature of modern business problems. Emphasize how issues in one area can impact others, necessitating a holistic view.
      - 2. Identifying the Continuum: Teach managers to recognize that problems are part of a continuum. Please encourage them to see issues not as static but as evolving entities within a more extensive system.
      - 3. Broadening Perspectives: Introduce exercises that encourage thinking from multiple perspectives. Use case studies to demonstrate how viewing problems in a broader context can lead to more effective solutions.
      - 4. Evolving Problem-Solving Approaches: Discuss the need for adaptive problem-solving strategies that evolve as the situation changes. Encourage flexibility and agility in thinking.
      - 5. Systemic Impact Analysis: Train managers to analyze how solutions might affect various parts of the organization and beyond, including stakeholders, customers, and the market.
      - 6. Encouraging Collaboration: Stress the importance of collaborative approaches in understanding and solving interconnected problems. Promote cross-functional teamwork.
      - 7. Developing Forward-Thinking Solutions: Guide managers to develop solutions that address the immediate problem and consider future implications and potential changes.
      - 8. Using Scenario Planning: Implement scenario planning exercises to help managers anticipate different solutions in a dynamic, interconnected environment.
      - 9. Building Resilience and Adaptability: Focus on building resilience and adaptability in problem-solving, enabling managers to respond effectively to unforeseen changes and challenges.
      - By expanding on these concepts, the workshop can equip business managers with the skills and mindset needed to tackle modern, interconnected problems effectively, ensuring solutions are comprehensive and beneficial for the entire organization and its environment.

      - Perspective Pivot Workshop.

      - 2. Assigning Responsibilities: Delegate tasks to specific team members, departments, or external partners. Assignments should be based on skills, experience, and availability, ensuring that the most qualified personnel handles each task.

      - 6. Integration with Organizational Structures: Align the action plan with the company's existing hierarchy, policies, and culture. This alignment is vital for securing support and cooperation across different levels of the organization and for seamless integration into ongoing operations.

      - #### Group B: Practicable Analysis for Action Plan Implementation.

  - ## Step 6.4 - Conducting Follow-up And Taking Feedback.
    - ### Intro.
      - Feedback is essential in adapting to changes and enhancing processes. This approach focuses on using feedback for four primary purposes: to adjust current operations, to prevent the recurrence of issues, to avoid future challenges, and to encourage ongoing improvement.
      - In business management, effectively handling feedback is crucial for continuous development and addressing problems. Keep the following key concepts in mind as you select which questions to address:
      - 1. Ongoing Corrections:
        - Develop a system to quickly integrate feedback into current operations, allowing real-time adjustments and improvements.
        - Implement a continuous monitoring process to assess the impact of these changes and ensure they are meeting the intended goals.
      - 2. Prevention of Problem Recurrence:
        - Analyze feedback to identify underlying causes of past issues.
        - Revise policies, procedures, and strategies to mitigate the risk of these issues happening again.
        - Equip teams with the necessary tools and knowledge to recognize and address these issues early.
      - 3. Averting Future Problems:
        - Use feedback to anticipate and prepare for potential future challenges.
        - Incorporate scenario planning and risk management strategies based on feedback trends and patterns.
        - Establish flexible and adaptive strategies that can quickly respond to unforeseen challenges.
      - 4. Continuous Improvement:
        - Create a culture where feedback is routinely collected, valued, and used for strategic planning.
        - Set up mechanisms to measure improvements and iteratively refine processes based on feedback.
        - Encourage innovation and experimentation as responses to feedback, fostering an environment of continual learning and development.
      - Additionally, the feedback process should be supported by:
      - Effective Communication: Ensure transparent and open channels for giving and receiving feedback.
      - Feedback Analysis: Systematically analyze feedback to extract meaningful insights and action points.
      - Engaging Stakeholders: Actively involve diverse stakeholders in the feedback process to gain comprehensive perspectives.
      - Responsive Action: Establish protocols for timely and effective responses to feedback, demonstrating commitment to continuous improvement.
      - By focusing on these strategies, businesses can effectively leverage feedback to adapt, prevent recurring problems, prepare for future challenges, and continuously improve their operations and strategy.
    - ### Recommended Thinking Tools.
      - Dynamic Feedback Mapper.
    - ### Questions.
      - #### Group A: Exploratory Assessment for Feedback and Follow-Up.
        - This group involves questions that dissect the feedback received during the action plan implementation. The focus is on understanding the nuances of the input, its implications on the current action plan, and identifying any unforeseen challenges or opportunities that have emerged. Questions here aim to analyze how the feedback correlates with the initial problem definition and whether it unveils any new aspects not considered initially.
        - What is not working?
        - What could be improved yet?
        - Might preventative maintenance avoid future problems?
        - Whom do you need feedback from? Customer? Employees? Suppliers? Vendors? Stakeholders?

        - If just one piece of feedback were available, what would it be, and how would it be used?
        - Could feedback be squelched when it does not fit preference?

        - In what ways does the feedback suggest adjustments to the timeline or scope of the action plan?

        - How has the feedback revealed new challenges or opportunities within the current market or operational environment?

        - What additional data or information might we need to understand and utilize the feedback fully?
        - How does the feedback interplay with the cultural and ethical considerations of the organization?
      - #### Group B: Practicable Analysis for Feedback and Follow-Up.
